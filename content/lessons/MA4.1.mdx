<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Differential equations specify a local rule, but existence, uniqueness and the interval where a solution survives require hypotheses. These distinctions matter before interpreting a numerical solver as a unique physical prediction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A slope field gives an allowed tangent at each state. If the rule changes too sharply near one state, several curves can leave the same starting point while still following its slopes.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

An initial-value problem specifies y′=f(t,y) and y(t₀)=y₀. A solution is differentiable on an interval and satisfies both the equation and initial condition there.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a continuous f near an initial point, a local existence theorem supplies at least one solution under its usual neighbourhood assumptions. Local Lipschitz continuity in y, together with suitable continuity in t, is a standard sufficient condition for uniqueness. Continuous f alone does not guarantee uniqueness. For $y'=\sqrt{|y|}$, y(0)=0, both y=0 and a curve that waits until a≥0 then follows y=(t−a)²/4 for t≥a satisfy the rule for forward time.

Separation for y′=g(t)h(y) integrates dy/h(y)=g(t)dt only where h(y)≠0. Constant equilibrium solutions with h(y)=0 must be checked before dividing. The integrated equation may define branches implicitly and may require sign or domain restrictions when inverted.

For a linear equation $y'+p(t)y=q(t)$, multiply by an integrating factor $\mu(t)=\exp(\int p(t)dt)$. Then $(\mu y)'=\mu q$, so an integral plus the initial value determines the solution on intervals where the coefficients satisfy the theorem assumptions. The arbitrary multiplicative constant in μ changes no resulting solution.

An equation M(t,y)dt+N(t,y)dy=0 is exact if it is the differential of a potential Ψ. On a suitable simply connected region, continuous partials and My=Nt give a standard exactness criterion. Integrating one partial requires adding an unknown function of the other variable, then fixing it from the remaining partial. An integrating factor can sometimes restore exactness, but no universal elementary recipe is promised.

Local uniqueness does not imply global existence. For y′=y² with y(0)=1, separation gives y=1/(1−t), which blows up at t=1. A solver stepping beyond a singularity is not automatically extending the same classical solution. Record the maximal interval and the state domain before interpreting a finite numeric output.


### Derive, test and transfer

For y′=a y with y(0)=y₀, separation or direct differentiation gives y=y₀ exp(at). Division by y during separation must be accompanied by the equilibrium solution y=0, which that division excludes. A derivation should check every recovered solution in the original equation.

Existence and uniqueness are different claims. The right side sqrt(|y|) is continuous but not locally Lipschitz at zero, permitting multiple solutions from y=0 that wait before departing. A numerical solver returning one curve does not establish uniqueness. Compare method assumptions with the behavior near equilibrium before interpreting a plotted solution as the only admissible trajectory.

</LessonSection>
