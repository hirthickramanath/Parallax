The one-dimensional nonrelativistic equation is iℏ∂ₜψ=[−ℏ²∂ₓ²/(2m)+V]ψ for a specified Hamiltonian domain and potential.

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A stationary state ψ(x,t)=φ(x)e⁻ⁱᴱᵗᐟℏ satisfies $-\hbar^2\phi''/(2m)+V\phi=E\phi$. Separation does not mean every time-dependent state is stationary: superpositions of distinct energies have evolving relative phases. Normalize bound states; scattering states require a flux or generalized-state normalization convention.

For an infinite well on 0&lt;x&lt;L, zero endpoint values give φₙ=√(2/L)sin(nπx/L) and $E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$, n≥1. A constant zero-energy function fails the Dirichlet boundaries unless it is identically zero. Finite wells instead require matching to decaying exterior solutions and can support only finitely many bound states.

For a finite nonsingular potential step, ψ and ψ′ are continuous when the mass is constant and no delta term is present. A delta potential causes a derivative jump derived by integrating the equation. The probability current is j=(ℏ/m)Im(ψ*ψ′), and the Schrödinger equation implies continuity ∂ₜ|ψ|²+∂ₓj=0 for a real local potential.

In a region E&lt;V, stationary solutions can be exponential. A finite barrier can transmit nonzero probability current through matching across both interfaces; a decaying amplitude alone is not automatically a nonzero transmitted current. Reflection and transmission probabilities use current ratios, not always raw amplitude squares. Hermitian boundary conditions and the full domain are essential for probability conservation.


### Derivation and transfer

For an infinite well on 0&lt;x&lt;L, normalized eigenfunctions are sqrt(2/L)sin(nπx/L) and energies scale as n²/L². The nonzero lowest energy follows confinement and boundary conditions, not a classical particle sitting at the bottom.

The explorer gives the n=1 probability density. A density is not a point probability; integrate it over an interval. Finite barriers allow penetration and require different matching conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In units π²ℏ²/(2mL²)=1, the first two infinite-well energies are 1 and 4, so the gap is 3. Doubling L at fixed m reduces every energy by a factor of four. The zero-point result is a boundary eigenvalue fact, not an extra constant potential inserted into the well.
