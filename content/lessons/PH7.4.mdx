<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

The canonical ensemble derives thermal probabilities and energy fluctuations without assuming that the subsystem energy stays fixed.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

At fixed T,V,N, canonical microstate probabilities are p_i=e⁻ᵝᴱⁱ/Z, β=1/(k_BT), with Z the sum over microstates including degeneracy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Combine a small system with a weakly coupled large isolated reservoir. The probability of subsystem energy E_i is proportional to the reservoir count Ω_b(E_tot−E_i). Expanding its entropy to first order gives S_b(E_tot−E_i)≈S_b(E_tot)−E_i/T, hence the Boltzmann factor. The approximation requires negligible interaction energy for this separation and a bath whose temperature changes negligibly.

The partition function $Z=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ normalizes the microstate weights. If a distinct energy level has degeneracy g, it contributes g e⁻ᵝᴱ. Counting both degeneracy and each microstate separately would double count. For independent subsystems the partition functions multiply when the Hamiltonian and state space factorize; interactions generally prevent that simple product.

At fixed Hamiltonian and volume, $U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z$ and $\operatorname{Var}(E)=\partial_\beta^2\ln Z$. Thus C_V=Var(E)/(k_BT²) is nonnegative for this canonical setup. Helmholtz free energy is F=−k_BT lnZ and S=−k_B∑p_i lnp_i. If energy levels themselves have explicit temperature dependence through an effective model, thermodynamic derivatives require additional care.

For two nondegenerate levels 0 and Δ, Z=1+e⁻ᵝΔ and the excitation probability is 1/(1+eᵝΔ). It approaches one half at high temperature, not one, because both levels retain equal asymptotic weight. Finite-system relative fluctuations need not be small. A canonical formula is not a proof that a strongly coupled subsystem or a nonequilibrium distribution is thermal.

### Derive the fluctuation identity

Hold the spectrum and volume fixed. Differentiate the normalized sum explicitly:

$$\partial_\beta Z=-\sum_i E_i e^{-\beta E_i},\qquad \partial_\beta^2 Z=\sum_i E_i^2e^{-\beta E_i}.$$

Consequently $\partial_\beta^2\ln Z=\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2$. Since $d\beta/dT=-1/(k_BT^2)$, the chain rule gives $C_V=\operatorname{Var}(E)/(k_BT^2)$. The units are energy per temperature: energy squared divided by energy times temperature. For an infinite state space, differentiating the sum requires convergence in the temperature range used.

### A finite reservoir is a counterexample to exact exponential weights

The next entropy term is $-E_i^2/(2T^2C_b)$, since $d^2S_b/dE_b^2=-1/(T^2C_b)$. Dividing entropy by $k_B$ gives a correction $-E_i^2/(2k_BT^2C_b)$ to the exponent. A reservoir with appreciable temperature change therefore produces weights that are not exactly canonical. The large-bath approximation must be checked over the occupied energy range, not just for the ground state.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

With Δ/(k_BT)=ln3, the excited-state probability is 1/(1+3)=1/4. In units Δ=1, U=1/4 and Var(E)=p(1−p)=3/16. Direct averaging agrees with derivatives of ln(1+e⁻ᵝ).

For an excited level of degeneracy three and a nondegenerate ground level, choose $\beta\Delta=\ln 3$. The excited level has total weight $3e^{-\ln 3}=1$, equal to the ground weight, so its total probability is $1/2$. Each of its three microstates has probability $1/6$. Thus $U=\Delta/2$, $\operatorname{Var}(E)=\Delta^2/4$, and $C_V/k_B=(\ln 3)^2/4$. At high temperature the excited *level* probability approaches $3/4$, while each of the four microstates approaches $1/4$.

</LessonSection>

**Core derivation.** For degeneracy ratio $g$ and $x=\Delta/(k_BT)$, derive $C_V/k_B=x^2g e^{-x}/(1+g e^{-x})^2$. Explain why the heat capacity vanishes as both $T\to0$ and $T\to\infty$ for this finite spectrum. The low-temperature exponential beats $x^2$; at high temperature the variance is bounded while $T^{-2}$ vanishes.

**Counterexample.** Replace the finite spectrum by a classical harmonic oscillator. Equipartition gives constant heat capacity $k_B$, so the finite-spectrum high-temperature conclusion does not extend to an unbounded spectrum.
