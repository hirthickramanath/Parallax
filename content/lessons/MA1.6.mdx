<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Derivatives become useful beyond local slopes because the mean value theorem connects a whole interval change to a slope at some interior point. Its endpoint and continuity hypotheses prevent false conclusions from local-looking formulas.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A differentiable curve joining two points must somewhere match the secant slope. Subtract the secant line: the resulting curve returns to the same height, forcing a flat interior extremum unless it is constant.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The derivative at an interior point is the limit of its difference quotient. Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If the derivative exists at a, write $f(a+h)-f(a)=h[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h$. The quotient stays bounded as h→0, so the product tends to zero, proving continuity. For |x| at zero the one-sided quotients are 1 and −1, so continuity is insufficient for differentiability.

Rolle's theorem assumes continuity on [a,b], differentiability on (a,b), a&lt;b, and equal endpoint values. Compactness supplies attained extrema. If f is not constant, at least one relevant extremum is interior, and the two-sided difference quotients force its derivative to zero. If f is constant every interior derivative is zero.

For the mean value theorem subtract the line joining the endpoint values. Applying Rolle gives some c in (a,b) with $f'(c)=[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a)$. Existence does not mean c is unique or directly computable from endpoint measurements. The theorem can fail if differentiability is missing: |x| on [−1,1] has secant slope zero but no zero derivative at a differentiable interior point.

If f′≥0 throughout an interval, every ordered pair of points has nonnegative secant slope, so f is nondecreasing. If f′>0, it is strictly increasing. The converse strict implication is false: x³ is strictly increasing but has derivative zero at 0. If |f′|≤M, the theorem gives the Lipschitz bound $|f(x)-f(y)|\le M|x-y|$.

Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder states, under the requisite continuity and differentiability through order n+1 between a and x, $f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!+f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{n+1}/(n+1)!$. A finite Taylor approximation does not claim an infinite series converges to f. Bound the derivative on the whole interval before quoting an error estimate.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

Subtract the secant line from $f$: $g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-(x-a)[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a)$. Then $g(a)=g(b)=0$. Rolle's theorem gives $g'(c)=0$, hence $f'(c)=[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a)$ when continuity on $[a,b]$ and differentiability on $(a,b)$ hold. Without these hypotheses the conclusion can fail.

If $|f'|\leq M$, the same equality bounds $|f(b)-f(a)|\leq M|b-a|$. A derivative lower bound gives a corresponding lower increment bound. A Taylor remainder estimate uses additional smoothness: with a second derivative bounded by $M_2$ on the relevant interval, the first-order error is at most $M_2h^2/2$. A small numerical residual alone does not establish the derivative bound needed for that conclusion.

</LessonSection>
