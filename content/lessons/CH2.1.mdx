<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

In a nonorthogonal basis, orbital coefficients solve Hc=ESc with overlap matrix S and normalization c†Sc=1.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The variational orbital equation is $H\mathbf c=ES\mathbf c$. If S is positive definite on the independent basis, a suitable orthogonalization transforms it to an ordinary Hermitian problem. Linear dependencies or tiny overlap eigenvalues cause numerical conditioning issues rather than new physical states.

For two equivalent functions with diagonal energy a, off-diagonal b and overlap s, symmetric/antisymmetric energies are (a+b)/(1+s) and (a−b)/(1−s) in the simple normalized real model. Ignoring overlap changes both the normalization and energy prediction.

Orbital occupations and a many-electron energy functional are further steps; a one-electron two-centre matrix is not the exact electronic energy of every molecule. Nuclear repulsion, electron interaction, exchange and correlation require their specified treatment.

Basis enlargement can improve a well-defined variational space but does not repair an incorrect Hamiltonian or state. Record symmetry, charge, spin, geometry and convergence before interpreting coefficients as chemical evidence. Population/charge partitions depend on conventions and are not uniquely observed atomic electron counts.


### Derive, test and transfer

For two orthonormal equivalent atomic basis functions with diagonal energy ε and real off-diagonal coupling t, the eigenvalues are ε±|t|. The splitting is 2|t|, and the lower combination is bonding within this ideal two-state model. The coefficient signs depend on the phase convention chosen for the basis.

Real atomic functions can overlap. Then the coefficient problem is Hc=ESc rather than an ordinary eigenproblem with S silently replaced by identity. Bond order from simple occupation bookkeeping is a model indicator, not a complete measured bond strength for every molecule. Correlation, multiple orbitals and nuclear geometry can change quantitative predictions.

</LessonSection>
