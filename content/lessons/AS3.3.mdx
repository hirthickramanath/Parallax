<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Reaction rates combine microscopic cross sections, particle distributions and composition, while stellar feedback depends on whether expansion can regulate the temperature.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a thermal two-body reaction, the rate density is n₁n₂⟨σv⟩ with a factor 1/2 for identical reactants counted as unordered pairs.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For identical species the pair correction gives $r=n^2\langle\sigma v\rangle/2$; for distinct species it is n₁n₂⟨σv⟩. Multiplying by deposited energy per reaction gives local heating, after accounting for energy carried away by neutrinos or other escaping products. A nuclear Q value alone is not necessarily all local heat.

Charged reactants must penetrate a Coulomb barrier. The product of the Maxwell thermal tail and tunnelling probability creates the Gamow-window concept in a nonresonant approximation. Resonances, screening and nonthermal distributions alter the simple estimate. Rates are temperature-dependent integrals over cross section, not one collision energy chosen arbitrarily.

The proton–proton chain and CNO cycles both convert hydrogen toward helium through different reaction networks and bottlenecks. CNO abundances act catalytically in the elementary cycle but can evolve in broader networks. Helium burning includes the triple-alpha pathway, whose effective density dependence differs from a single two-body reaction.

A local power-law ε∝ρT^ν summarizes a limited regime, not a universal exact exponent across stellar temperatures. Expansion/cooling can stabilize burning in a nondegenerate core; a degenerate region responds differently and can become thermally unstable. Reaction-network conservation, composition evolution, neutrino losses and transport must be coupled before predicting an evolutionary outcome.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a local power-law approximation $\epsilon\propto\rho^aT^\nu$, logarithmic differentiation gives $d\ln\epsilon=a\,d\ln\rho+\nu\,d\ln T$ at fixed composition. Thus ν is the fractional temperature sensitivity, not an energy-release efficiency. A large ν makes a small temperature perturbation important locally, but structural expansion can feed back and regulate the star.

Nuclear reactions must conserve charge, baryon bookkeeping and energy including rest-mass differences. A net energy budget can be written $E=\Delta m c^2$, with neutrino losses reducing thermal energy deposited in the star. Screening, composition and reaction-network bottlenecks change a fitted local exponent. Extrapolating a Tν toy curve across orders of magnitude is not a valid substitute for a reaction rate table or a self-consistent stellar model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If identical reactants have n=4 and ⟨σv⟩=2 in consistent units, the rate density is 16. A local T⁴ fit predicts a factor 16 after doubling T only if both temperatures remain inside that fit regime.



### Worked investigation: Energy generation versus mass consumption

The net conversion of hydrogen into helium releases approximately 0.007 of the participating rest mass as energy; neutrino losses reduce what heats the star. With the pedagogical approximation c=3.0×10^8 m/s, a luminosity 6.3×10^26 W supplied entirely by this conversion requires participating mass rate L/(0.007c²)=10^12 kg/s. That is consumed hydrogen, not the mass-equivalent loss L/c²=7×10^9 kg/s.

A fuel lifetime estimate eta*f*M*c²/L also requires a specified accessible fuel fraction f and assumes roughly steady L. It is not the age of every star: mixing, composition and a changing luminosity matter. Reaction-rate temperature sensitivity and the available reaction chain are separate from the mass-energy budget.
