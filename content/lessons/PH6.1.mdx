<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A dispersion relation ω(k) links frequency and wavenumber for a chosen wave branch. A packet superposes modes with a distribution of k.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For the homogeneous nondispersive equation $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$, Fourier components satisfy ω²=c²k². A single-direction packet can translate without distortion in this ideal one-dimensional model because ω is linear in k on that branch. Boundary reflections and multidimensional geometry can still change a local signal.

Expand ω(k) near carrier k₀: ω≈ω₀+ω′₀δk+ω″₀δk²/2. The first derivative gives a narrowband envelope speed and the second produces dispersion at leading order. Neglecting the quadratic term requires a propagation distance/time and bandwidth for which its phase remains small.

Phase velocity is ω/k; group velocity is dω/dk when the envelope approximation applies. Neither alone is a universal definition of a causal signal front in an absorbing or strongly distorted medium. Complex k or ω introduces attenuation/growth and requires a sign convention and stability analysis.

A medium can have multiple branches, anisotropy and polarization-dependent propagation. An effective wave equation must be derived from its constitutive regime. Finite-difference numerical dispersion is a separate source of phase error, so a distorted computed pulse does not automatically demonstrate physical material dispersion.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a narrow wavepacket, neighboring Fourier phases produce an envelope moving at dω/dk, while an individual phase moves at ω/k. For the illustrative dimensionless dispersion ω=2k+k³, phase speed is 2+k² and group speed is 2+3k². Their difference grows with spectral scale.

A local group-speed expression describes a sufficiently narrow packet in a regime where the dispersion approximation is valid; it is not an unrestricted information-velocity theorem. Boundary reflections, attenuation and broad spectra can change the observed envelope. A nondispersive wave equation has equal phase/group speeds, but adding dispersion changes the relation rather than merely renaming one speed.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For ω=2k+k² and k₀=1 in normalized units, phase velocity is 3 and group velocity is 4. The curvature is 2, so a finite-bandwidth packet generally disperses. Quoting one of these speeds without specifying the measured feature is ambiguous.
