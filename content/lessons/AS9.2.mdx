<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

FLRW symmetry simplifies general relativity to expansion equations, but homogeneity is a large-scale approximation rather than a claim of a featureless local universe.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

FLRW spacetime uses a scale factor a(t) for homogeneous isotropic spatial slices. H=ȧ/a describes expansion, not a force acting on every bound system.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For mass-equivalent density ρ and pressure p, one convention gives $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3-kc^2/a^2+\Lambda c^2/3$, where the normalization/units of k follow the chosen comoving coordinates. The acceleration equation contains ρ+3p/c². Thus pressure contributes gravitationally, not only through a Newtonian pressure gradient.

Local energy conservation gives $\dot\rho+3H(\rho+p/c^2)=0$ for a separately conserved perfect fluid. For constant w=p/(ρc²), density scales as a⁻³⁽¹⁺ʷ⁾. Pressureless matter gives a⁻³, radiation a⁻⁴ and a cosmological constant remains fixed in this description.

Critical density is 3H²/(8πG), and density parameters compare components with it at a specified epoch. Flatness is a spatial-curvature condition, not a declaration that gravity or expansion vanishes. The Hubble radius c/H is not automatically every definition of particle/event horizon.

Bound structures can decouple from the simple background expansion; a local laboratory is not generally stretched by inserting H into every length equation. Perturbations, local dynamics and observational light-cone relations extend the background model. The equations require content, parameters and initial conditions before predicting a(t).


### Derive, test and transfer

For a homogeneous isotropic background, the Friedmann equation relates H² to energy density, curvature and the cosmological constant. Conserved pressureless matter dilutes as a^−3, while a cosmological constant has constant density. In a flat matter-plus-Λ toy model, $H^2/H_0^2=\Omega_m a^{-3}+1-\Omega_m$.

The formula is normalized to one at a=1, but it omits radiation and assumes constant Λ. A realistic early-Universe calculation adds radiation, species evolution and the appropriate units. A background expansion curve also does not by itself determine perturbation growth or every observable distance; those require evolution and light propagation.
