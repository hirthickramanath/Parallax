Why can a larger telescope collect more light without beating the atmosphere’s blur? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Telescope performance combines collecting area, angular resolution, optical quality and detector noise. Increasing magnification alone cannot create information absent from the recorded image.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Aperture controls photon collection and a diffraction scale. The atmosphere, optics and detector add separate filters and noise, so the narrowest ideal pattern is not the whole instrument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a circular unobscured aperture the diffraction first-minimum scale is about 1.22λ/D. A detector records photoelectrons with quantum efficiency and additional noise sources.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For equal throughput and exposure, a point source supplies counts proportional to collecting area, πD²/4, and incident photon flux. A larger aperture can reduce the diffraction scale while increasing photons, but central obstruction, aberration, atmosphere and optical alignment alter the point-spread function. The Rayleigh scale is a conventional comparison criterion, not a universal hard inference limit.

A focal length f maps a small field angle θ to image displacement approximately fθ. Detector pixel pitch p gives an angular pixel scale p/f in radians. Sampling too coarsely loses recoverable image structure; making pixels smaller cannot undo the physical point-spread function and changes per-pixel signal and read noise.

In a simplified extracted-aperture count budget with known background, $\mathrm{SNR}=N_s/\sqrt{N_s+N_b+n_{\rm pix}dt+n_{\rm pix}r^2}$. Here source and background counts are Poisson means, d is dark electrons per pixel per second, t is exposure, and r is rms read noise per pixel for one read. Background-estimation uncertainty, correlated pixels, calibration error and source variability require additional terms.

In source-shot-noise domination SNR≈√Ns and typically rises as √t. In background domination the aperture used for extraction and the seeing profile affect noise; simply multiplying D by a universal sensitivity exponent can be misleading. Saturation, nonlinearity and cosmic-ray contamination limit arbitrarily long single exposures.

Bias, dark and flat-field calibrations address different effects. A flat normalizes relative response but its own noise and illumination pattern matter. Quantum efficiency converts incoming photons to detected electrons probabilistically; it is not identical to overall telescope throughput. Instrument performance should be expressed with an explicit source, band, extraction and detection criterion.


### Derive, test and transfer

A circular aperture produces an Airy diffraction pattern whose first minimum is at approximately $1.22\lambda/D$ radians. This is a property of the aperture's wave response, not a statement that a pixel is that angular size. Detector sampling must also resolve the image, and atmospheric turbulence can broaden it far beyond the diffraction limit.

Collected photon rate scales roughly as $D^2$ at fixed throughput and source spectrum, while the diffraction angle scales as 1/D. Doubling the diameter therefore quadruples counts but halves the ideal diffraction scale. An interferometer can obtain a small angular scale from a long baseline without the collecting area of a filled aperture that size. Distinguish resolution, sensitivity, field of view and calibration stability before selecting an instrument for a scientific question.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For 100 source electrons and negligible background, dark current and read noise, expected SNR is √100=10. Doubling the exposure gives 200 expected electrons and SNR≈14.14, not 20. Doubling diameter at fixed conditions gives four times source counts, but its final SNR regime must still be specified.
