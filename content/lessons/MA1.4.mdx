<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why can rearranging some convergent sums change their answer? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Series represent accumulated contributions in approximations and perturbation calculations. Finite-sum algebra cannot automatically be applied to infinitely many terms: convergence and summation order are part of the statement.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Follow partial sums. Absolute convergence controls the total size of contributions; conditional convergence relies on cancellation whose order matters.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A series ∑aₙ converges if its partial sums converge. Absolute convergence means ∑|aₙ| converges; conditional convergence means convergence without absolute convergence.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Convergence forces aₙ→0 because aₙ is the difference between consecutive partial sums. The converse fails for the harmonic series. Grouping terms from $2^{k-1}+1$ through $2^k$ yields at least 1/2 per group, so the partial sums are unbounded.

For nonnegative decreasing f, compare rectangles with $\int f(x)\,dx$. This proves the p-series ∑1/nᵖ converges exactly when p>1. Direct comparison bounds positive partial sums by a convergent series; limit comparison applies when the term ratio tends to a finite positive constant. If signs vary, apply a valid absolute test or analyze cancellation separately.

For nonzero terms with a ratio limit $\lim|a_{n+1}/a_n|=r$, convergence is absolute when r&lt;1 and the terms fail to approach zero when r>1. At r=1 the test is inconclusive: both ∑1/n and ∑1/n² have that ratio limit. The root test has the analogous threshold using limsup |aₙ|^(1/n). An inconclusive test is not evidence of divergence.

If bₙ decreases to zero, the alternating series ∑(−1)ⁿ⁻¹bₙ converges and the remainder after N terms has magnitude at most bN₊₁. Pairing and interleaving the even and odd partial sums proves that they approach the same boundary. Monotonicity and vanishing terms are substantive conditions, not optional labels.

Absolute convergence implies convergence through a Cauchy tail bound: the magnitude of any signed tail is no larger than its absolute tail sum. It also permits rearrangement without changing the sum. A real conditionally convergent series has divergent positive and negative subseries; suitable reorderings can produce different sums or divergence. This statement concerns a new order of infinitely many terms, not a violation of finite addition.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

The alternating harmonic series is conditionally convergent: the alternating test applies, while its absolute series is harmonic. After N terms its error is at most 1/(N+1). Taking N=100 bounds the absolute error by 1/101, independently of a graph of the running total.

</LessonSection>
