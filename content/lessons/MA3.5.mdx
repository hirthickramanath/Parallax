<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Inner products add geometry to vector spaces. Orthogonal projection explains approximation, regression and expansion coefficients, provided the chosen inner product matches the intended weighting.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

If a residual had a component along an allowed fitting direction, moving slightly in that direction could shorten it. At the best projection, no such component remains.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Use an inner product conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second. Its norm is the square root of the inner product of a vector with itself.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In complex coordinates the standard inner product is $\langle x,y\rangle=x^*y$, using conjugate transpose. It satisfies conjugate symmetry and positive definiteness. Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\|y\|$; it follows by requiring the squared norm of x−αy to remain nonnegative at the minimizing α. The triangle inequality then follows by expanding a norm square and applying that bound.

For an orthonormal list q₁,...,qₖ, the projection onto its span is $p=\sum_i q_i\langle q_i,x\rangle$. The residual r=x−p is orthogonal to every qᵢ. For any other s in the span, orthogonality gives $\|x-s\|^2=\|r\|^2+\|p-s\|^2$, establishing the unique best approximation in that finite-dimensional subspace.

Gram–Schmidt subtracts these projections from each new independent vector and normalizes the remainder. In exact arithmetic it builds an orthonormal basis of the same span. In floating arithmetic nearly dependent inputs can lose orthogonality; modified Gram–Schmidt, reorthogonalization or Householder QR can be preferable.

For a full-column-rank real matrix A, least-squares stationarity gives $A^T(Ax-b)=0$. The residual is orthogonal to the column space. This is a geometric characterization, not an instruction to form the normal equations as the best numerical method: QR and SVD avoid the squared condition number of AᵀA.

A weighted inner product x*Wy requires Hermitian positive-definite W to retain the stated norm geometry. If data errors have covariance Σ, weighting by Σ⁻¹ can be appropriate under the model, but estimated or singular covariance needs treatment. In infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, projection onto a closed subspace extends this result; nonclosed subspaces can fail to attain the infimum.

</LessonSection>
