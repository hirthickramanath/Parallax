<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Conservation laws arise from continuous action symmetries and can survive nonobvious coordinate descriptions. The mechanical and canonical meaning of a charge depends on the full Lagrangian and its boundary terms.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A symmetry is a direction in path space along which the action changes only by an allowed endpoint contribution. Its conserved charge can be recognized in different coordinates.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a time-dependent point symmetry with infinitesimal parameters τ and η and boundary term B, the on-solution Noether charge is J=p·η−Hτ−B under the chosen convention.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a cyclic coordinate qj with no explicit appearance in L, Euler–Lagrange gives $d p_j/dt=0$. This elementary case is a coordinate-translation variational symmetry. A coordinate can be cyclic while its generalized velocity still appears in L; removing both would describe a different problem.

For no explicit time dependence, define $H=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L$. Differentiating along a solution gives dH/dt=−∂L/∂t, so H is conserved in the autonomous case. Identifying H with T+V uses additional assumptions, such as ordinary quadratic velocity kinetic energy and suitable time-independent coordinates.

For a rotationally invariant point-particle system, an infinitesimal rotation η=a×r gives J=a·(r×p), implying angular-momentum conservation. A fixed external field or support can break that symmetry for the subsystem even if the complete apparatus conserves total momentum including omitted degrees of freedom.

If the Lagrangian changes by a total derivative, retain B in the charge. A change of L by a total derivative shifts canonical definitions consistently and should not create a new physical nonconservation. Symmetry of the differential equations alone is not automatically the variational symmetry required by this theorem.

A useful derivation checks the transformation of Ldt, writes the resulting charge and differentiates it using the actual equations. Discrete parity or time-reversal symmetry does not yield the same one-parameter infinitesimal charge through this simple theorem, though those symmetries have other physical consequences.


### Variational derivation and transfer case

For an infinitesimal transformation $\delta q=\varepsilon\eta$, suppose $\delta L=\varepsilon\,dB/dt$. On solutions, integration by parts reduces the variation to a total derivative and gives $d(p\eta-B)/dt=0$. A strict symmetry has $B=0$, but a symmetry up to a boundary term generally does not.

For $L=m\dot q^2/2+Fq$ and constant translation, $\eta=1$ and $B=Ft$. Thus $J=p-Ft$ is conserved even though ordinary momentum increases at rate F. Rotational invariance similarly gives the angular-momentum generator. The symmetry must apply to the complete stated system, including prescribed external fields; a fixed apparatus can break a symmetry that an isolated subsystem would have had.

</LessonSection>
