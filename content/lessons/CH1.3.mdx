<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Kinetic laws describe data in a regime; mechanisms propose the elementary routes that produce them. An effective order does not uniquely reconstruct every microscopic step.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A short-lived intermediate can be formed and consumed so quickly that only a simpler net time dependence is visible.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Reaction rate is defined using a specified species change and stoichiometric normalization. Elementary-step rate laws and overall observed rate laws are different claims.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For A→products with first-order disappearance, $-d[A]/dt=k[A]$ gives [A]=[A]₀e⁻ᵏᵗ. For bimolecular A+B disappearance, a simple elementary mass-action model gives k[A][B]. Stoichiometric rate normalization matters: a 2A→product event can give a factor of two between event rate and A disappearance.

For a sequence A→I→P with rates k₁ and k₂, the exact linear equations determine a transient intermediate. If I relaxes quickly and its buildup transient is unimportant, d[I]/dt≈0 gives [I]≈k₁[A]/k₂. This quasi-steady-state approximation requires a timescale and concentration argument; it is not identical to equilibrium.

For reversible A+B⇌I followed by I→P, a fast pre-equilibrium approximation relates [I] to an equilibrium ratio before the slow product step. Steady-state and pre-equilibrium assumptions can yield different rate expressions, and both can fail outside their intended parameter regime.

Temperature-dependent rates often fit $k=Ae^{-E_a/(RT)}$ over a limited range. An Arrhenius plot slope estimates an effective activation energy if the prefactor and mechanism do not change materially. The activation energy is not the reaction enthalpy. Curvature may indicate changing mechanisms, transport limitation or other effects rather than bad plotting alone.

A kinetic fit should compare concentration profiles, reaction orders and residuals across several initial conditions. Identifiability can be weak: different mechanisms or parameter combinations can produce almost indistinguishable observed curves. An accurate fit of one trace is insufficient to prove a unique mechanism.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a first-order elementary or effective decay dc/dt=−kc with constant k, integration gives c=c0 exp(−kt) and half-life ln2/k. Constancy of half-life is a diagnostic of this model, not a universal property of every reaction. Reaction order is determined by the rate law, not automatically by overall stoichiometric coefficients.

A mechanism consists of elementary steps and intermediates; eliminating short-lived species requires justified steady-state or pre-equilibrium approximations. The same observed order can arise from different mechanisms over a restricted range. Temperature, solvent and catalyst can change an effective rate constant, so a fitted exponential alone does not uniquely identify microscopic steps.

</LessonSection>
