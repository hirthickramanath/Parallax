<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Accretion converts binding energy through angular-momentum transport, dissipation and radiation. Disc approximations must distinguish geometrically thin, thick and advective regimes.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A circular Newtonian Kepler orbit has specific energy −GM/(2r) and angular speed √(GM/r³). A disc must transport angular momentum for material to drift inward.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Moving between circular orbits releases binding energy as angular momentum is redistributed. A test particle cannot simply spiral inward on unchanged conservative Kepler orbits without a torque. Turbulent/magnetic stress, not ordinary molecular viscosity alone, often supplies the effective transport.

A radiatively efficient steady thin-disc approximation gives $F(r)=3GM\dot M[1-\sqrt{r_{in}/r}]/(8\pi r^3)$ per face for the standard zero-inner-torque Newtonian boundary. The bracket and normalization depend on that boundary condition. Local blackbody emission is an additional spectral approximation, not a consequence of angular-momentum conservation alone.

Integrated luminosity is of order GMdotM/(2r_in) for this model. Writing L=ηdotM c² defines an efficiency whose value depends on potential, inner boundary, spin and energy carried away. It is not universally ten percent or the same for a material surface and a black hole.

At high or low accretion rates, advection, outflows, opacity and cooling can invalidate the thin radiatively efficient picture. The Eddington luminosity follows a gravity/radiation-force balance with a specified opacity and geometry, not an absolute ban on every anisotropic or transient super-Eddington flow. Timing and spectra require a model of emission and propagation beyond one luminosity estimate.


### Derive, test and transfer

A mass inflow rate carries rest-energy rate $\dot M c^2$. If a fraction η escapes as radiation, $L=\eta\dot M c^2$. The efficiency must come from a physical flow model: a thin Newtonian disc with an inner boundary releases binding energy of order $GM/(2R_{in})$ per unit accreted mass, whereas black-hole spin, stress and relativistic geometry change the conversion.

Angular momentum transport, not simply radial free fall, allows an orbiting disc to accrete. Viscous or magnetic stresses redistribute angular momentum while dissipating energy. Some flows advect energy inward or lose mass in winds, so measured luminosity alone cannot determine a unique mass supply rate. The interactive budget holds η fixed while varying inflow, making this degeneracy visible without pretending to solve the disc temperature and surface-density equations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In normalized Newtonian units GM=12,dotM=2 and r_in=3, the ideal zero-torque disc luminosity is 4. Doubling dotM doubles this estimate only if the same steady efficient regime remains valid.



### Worked investigation: Accretion energy is not fusion efficiency

A circular Newtonian orbit has specific kinetic energy GM/(2R), potential energy -GM/R and total energy -GM/(2R). Material settling from a very large radius to a circular orbit R can therefore release roughly GM/(2R) per kilogram if the difference is radiated. A steady toy disc has L≈GM*dot(M)/(2R).

With GM=1.2×10^20 m³/s², dot(M)=2×10^12 kg/s and R=10^7 m, this gives L=1.2×10^25 W. A boundary layer can release additional energy for a material surface; a black hole has no such surface, and relativistic binding and the inner edge matter. Angular-momentum transport and advected energy determine how much is actually radiated, so this expression is not a universal luminosity guarantee.
