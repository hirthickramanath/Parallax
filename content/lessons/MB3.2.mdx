<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="A slope that keeps changing" layer="puzzle">

A secant between two points gives an average rate. If the points are extremely close, is that rate exactly the derivative?

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="From motion to local change" layer="story">

The need to describe instantaneous motion motivated methods of calculus. The rigorous language of limits makes the claim precise without relying on an indivisible time interval. Detailed historical attribution remains under review.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Zoom, but do not confuse the picture with proof" layer="intuition">

A differentiable graph is locally approximated by a straight line. Zooming suggests this structure; proving it requires controlling the error for every sufficiently small displacement.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="The derivative and its domain" layer="concept">

For a real-valued function defined near a, the derivative is $\lim_{h\to0}[f(a+h)-f(a)]/h$, if this finite two-sided limit exists. Nonzero h is required in the quotient. Continuity alone does not guarantee differentiability.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="A complete proof for a square" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For $f(x)=x^2$ and $h\ne0$,

$$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h=\frac{a^2+2ah+h^2-a^2}h=2a+h.$$

Each equality is exact. The difference between this quotient and the proposed limit is $|h|$. Given any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon$. Then

$$0<|h|<\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left|\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h-2a\right|=|h|<\varepsilon.$$

This is precisely the definition of a two-sided limit; it holds for every real a. Thus $f'(a)=2a$.

For comparison, at zero the quotient for $|x|$ equals $|h|/h$, which is 1 on the positive side and −1 on the negative side. The two-sided limit fails, although the function is continuous. A finite set of positive-h samples would miss that obstruction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Position and velocity" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If $s(t)=ct^2$ with c in m/s², then $s\prime(t)=2ct$ in m/s. At c = 2 m/s² and t = 3 s, velocity is 12 m/s. This parameter c is half the constant acceleration.

<LessonSection id="connections" title="From a tangent to dynamics" layer="connections">

Instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. Those definitions turn Newton’s force law into a differential equation.
