<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A molecular-orbital approximation assigns electrons to delocalized one-electron functions combined into an antisymmetric many-electron state; bonding and antibonding labels describe energetic/phase character in a model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For two comparable atomic functions φ_A,φ_B, normalized combinations involve φ_A±φ_B and overlap S. The symmetric combination can accumulate density between nuclei, but its energy depends on the Hamiltonian, not merely the sign drawn on a picture. Orthogonalizing and normalizing the basis are distinct steps.

In an elementary closed-shell occupation model, $b=(N_b-N_a)/2$ estimates bond order from bonding and antibonding electron counts. Nonbonding electrons contribute neither term in this formula. Fractional occupancies, correlation and ambiguous orbital partitions limit its interpretation as a directly observed quantity.

For O₂, the qualitative valence MO filling leaves two parallel-spin electrons in degenerate π* orbitals in the triplet ground state. This accounts for paramagnetism that a simple all-paired Lewis model misses. Orbital order changes across the second-row diatomics because mixing between same-symmetry states changes energies; one universal memorized ordering is unsafe.

Heteronuclear orbitals can be polarized toward one atom and need not have equal atomic coefficients. Electron removal changes bonding differently depending on which orbital loses occupancy. Bond lengths, vibrational frequencies, magnetism and photoelectron spectra provide complementary tests; no single pictorial bond-order count is the entire electronic structure.


### Derive, test and transfer

Bond order in a simple MO occupation picture is half bonding minus antibonding occupancy. Adding two electrons to an antibonding level lowers that count by one; it does not simply add another stabilizing bond because more electrons are present. Pauli and Hund occupations must be applied to defined orbitals.

The sequence of orbital energies depends on molecule, symmetry and mixing. A single ordering should not be reused blindly across all diatomic species. Magnetic behavior depends on unpaired electrons, while a formal bond order is only one structural descriptor. The display isolates occupancy bookkeeping and does not calculate orbital energies or full dissociation curves.

</LessonSection>

For eight bonding and four antibonding electrons, the elementary bond order is (8−4)/2=2. Removing one antibonding electron raises that model value to (8−3)/2=2.5, whereas removing a bonding electron would lower it.
