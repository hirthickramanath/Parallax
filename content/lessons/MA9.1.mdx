<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Variational methods search over functions rather than a finite list of parameters. They derive equations of motion and equilibrium conditions from a functional, but stationarity and minimization are separate questions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Perturb an entire candidate path by a small test function. At a stationary path the first-order functional change vanishes for every allowed perturbation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For S[q]=∫L(t,q,q′)dt with fixed endpoint values, admissible variations q+εη have η zero at both endpoints. A classical derivation assumes enough smoothness to differentiate and integrate by parts.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Differentiate with respect to ε at zero to get $\delta S=\int_a^b(L_q\eta+L_{q'}\eta')dt$. Integration by parts yields $\delta S=[L_{q'}\eta]_a^b+\int_a^b[L_q-d(L_{q'})/dt]\eta\,dt$. Fixed endpoints remove the boundary term. The fundamental lemma says a continuous integrand whose integral against every suitable compactly supported test function vanishes must itself vanish. Thus $d(L_{q'})/dt-L_q=0$.

The same argument holds componentwise for several coordinates. The resulting Euler–Lagrange equation is a necessary stationarity condition under the admitted variation class. It is not sufficient to establish a minimum. The second variation or a more global convexity argument is needed for that classification.

For a free particle L=mq′²/2, the equation is mq″=0, giving a straight path. With fixed endpoints the quadratic kinetic functional is minimized: write q=qstationary+η and the cross term vanishes, leaving a positive integral mη′²/2. This positive result depends on that L; an arbitrary action can have saddle directions.

If an endpoint coordinate is free while endpoint time is fixed, the boundary variation is not zero there and supplies a natural condition, such as Lq′=0 for a free scalar endpoint with no additional boundary functional. Moving endpoint times or constrained endpoint manifolds require a transversality analysis rather than reusing the fixed-endpoint formula.

Adding a total derivative dF(t,q)/dt to L changes the action by an endpoint quantity. For fixed endpoint data it leaves the interior Euler–Lagrange equation unchanged, though canonical momenta and boundary terms can change. Smoothness, endpoint conditions and the admissible class therefore belong to the mathematical problem, not to optional presentation details.


### Derivation and transfer

For a functional ∫L(q,q′,t)dt, vary q by εη with fixed endpoint η=0. Integration by parts gives δS=∫[∂L/∂q−d(∂L/∂q′)/dt]ηdt. Stationarity for all admissible η yields Euler–Lagrange, not necessarily a minimum.

For L=(q′²−ω²q²)/2 this gives q″=−ω²q. The display probes the acceleration law at a point, not the full space of variations. Endpoint conditions and regularity are part of the derivation; free endpoints add boundary conditions.

</LessonSection>
