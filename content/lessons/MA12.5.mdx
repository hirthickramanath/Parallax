<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The spectrum consists of values where A−λI lacks the required bounded everywhere-defined inverse on the stated space; point and continuous spectral behaviour must be distinguished.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a finite Hermitian matrix, an orthonormal eigenbasis diagonalizes A and functions f(A) act on its real eigenvalues. In infinite-dimensional settings, a self-adjoint operator has a projection-valued spectral measure and $A=\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)$ with an appropriate domain condition.

A continuous-spectrum label need not correspond to an ordinary Hilbert-space eigenvector. Plane waves in full-line free-particle descriptions use generalized normalization, whereas normalizable packets are superpositions in the physical Hilbert space. One must not insert a Dirac-normalized wave as if its L² norm were one.

Compact self-adjoint operators have a discrete nonzero eigenvalue structure with possible accumulation at zero under the theorem’s hypotheses. General self-adjoint operators need not be compact or have a purely discrete spectrum. Boundary conditions, domain size and confining potentials change spectral type.

The functional calculus defines projectors, evolution and measurement probabilities, but unbounded functions of A restrict domains. Approximate numerical matrix eigenvalues are a discretization result, not automatically a proof of the continuum spectrum; check refinement, boundary truncation and possible spectral artifacts.


### Derivation and transfer

A self-adjoint operator has a real spectrum, but its spectral resolution need not be a finite sum of ordinary eigenvectors. Multiplication by x on L²([0,1]) has continuous spectrum, illustrating why finite-matrix intuition is incomplete.

For the finite diagonal example diag(−1,2), the resolvent eigenvalues are 1/(λ−z) away from the spectrum. The display probes one real resolvent branch without crossing its pole. It is not a complete spectral measure or a classification of non-self-adjoint operators.

</LessonSection>
