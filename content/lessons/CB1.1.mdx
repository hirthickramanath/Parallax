<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Atomic spectra and the repeating chemical behaviour of elements demanded a model beyond indivisible solid particles. Quantum states and electron exclusion explain recurring valence patterns, while electron interactions explain why simple hydrogen formulas cannot be copied unchanged to every atom.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

An outer electron feels attraction from the nucleus partly screened by other electrons. An orbital that penetrates nearer the nucleus can feel a different effective attraction from another orbital with the same principal quantum number.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

Z is the proton count; a neutral atom has Z electrons. The quantum numbers satisfy n≥1, 0≤l≤n−1, −l≤m≤l and spin projection ±1/2. Each spatial orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spin. Orbital energies and filling orders in many-electron atoms require care; simple rules are not universal theorems.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a fixed n, each l has 2l+1 magnetic substates. Summing the odd integers gives

$$\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2.$$

Including two spin states per spatial orbital yields a shell capacity of 2n². This counts allowed states, not a universal order in which all shells fill.

In a one-electron, nonrelativistic Coulomb model, energy depends primarily on n and scales as $E_n\propto-\mu_r Z^2/n^2$, where μ_r is the reduced mass. Transitions produce photons with hν=E_high−E_low. Electron-electron repulsion, shielding, fine structure and other effects alter this simple spectrum in many-electron atoms.

A useful qualitative screened attraction is −Z_eff e²/(4πε₀r). Increasing effective attraction tends to contract a comparable valence orbital and raise the energy required for removal. Across a period, rising nuclear charge with incomplete additional shielding often decreases atomic radius. Down a group, additional shells often increase radius.

These statements do not produce exact ionization energies. Pairing, subshell changes and configuration stability create exceptions to simple monotonic rules. Ionization is defined as the energy change for removal from a specified gaseous state; electron affinity has different sign conventions that must be stated.

For a neutral atom the electron count is Z. A cation with charge +q has Z−q electrons; an anion with charge −q has Z+q. Isotopes change neutron count and mass without changing Z. Chemical trends depend chiefly on electronic structure, while isotope effects can matter through nuclear mass and spin.


### Derivation and transfer checkpoint

For a specified orbital angular momentum $l$, the magnetic quantum number takes $2l+1$ integer values. With two allowed electron spin projections, a subshell can accommodate $2(2l+1)$ electrons. Summing over $l=0,\ldots,n-1$ gives shell capacity $2n^2$. Capacity does not by itself specify the energetic order in which subshells fill in interacting atoms.

For a one-electron Coulomb model, bound energies are negative relative to the ionization threshold. Ionizing hydrogen from $n=2$ therefore costs 3.4 eV, not 13.6 eV. In many-electron atoms, screening and electron-electron interactions invalidate an unchanged one-electron formula. Periodic trends are useful model-based tendencies with structural exceptions, not exceptionless monotonic laws.

</LessonSection>
