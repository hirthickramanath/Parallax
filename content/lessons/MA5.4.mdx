<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Diffusion models thermal transport and smoothing. Its conservation law, boundary data and numerical stability must match before a temperature animation is meaningful.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Neighbouring regions exchange energy down temperature gradients. Sharp spatial variation drives stronger flux and high-frequency modes decay faster.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The constant-coefficient heat equation uₜ=κuₓₓ has diffusivity κ>0 with dimensions length²/time. It follows from local conservation plus a linear conductive flux in a uniform medium without sources.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Let volumetric heat capacity be constant C and heat flux j=−K uₓ. Conservation C uₜ=−∂ₓj gives $u_t=\kappa u_{xx}$ with κ=K/C. Variable conductivity instead belongs inside the divergence; sources add a source term with consistent heat-capacity scaling. Temperature itself is not generally a conserved integral when heat capacity or boundary exchange varies.

On 0&lt;x&lt;L with fixed zero boundary values, separation u=X(x)T(t) gives X″+λX=0 and T′=−κλT. Nonzero Dirichlet modes are sin(nπx/L) with λₙ=(nπ/L)². For suitable initial data, $u(x,t)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)e^{-\kappa(n\pi/L)^2t}$. The initial match can be L² even when pointwise compatibility fails at a boundary corner. For positive time the solution smooths under the stated model.

For insulated endpoints, uₓ=0, the constant mode survives and the mean is conserved. With zero Dirichlet boundaries, heat can leave the interval. Integration by parts for a sufficiently regular homogeneous-boundary solution gives $\frac d{dt}\frac12\int u^2dx=-\kappa\int u_x^2dx$ when boundary terms vanish. This dissipates the squared norm; it is not a claim that every point decreases monotonically.

On the infinite line, the fundamental solution is $G(x,t)=(4\pi\kappa t)^{-1/2}\exp[-x^2/(4\kappa t)]$ for t>0. It has unit integral and variance 2κt; convolving with initial data solves the initial-value problem under appropriate hypotheses. The classical equation has nonzero tails arbitrarily far away immediately. This is a continuum diffusion approximation, not a relativistic finite-signal-speed law.

For a uniform spatial grid, forward Euler plus a centered second difference gives uⱼⁿ⁺¹=uⱼⁿ+r(uⱼ₊₁ⁿ−2uⱼⁿ+uⱼ₋₁ⁿ), r=κΔt/Δx². Fourier amplification is 1−4r sin²(θ/2), so the standard one-dimensional linear stability condition is 0≤r≤1/2. Stability alone is not accuracy: refine space/time and check boundaries, conservation where appropriate and convergence. Backward heat recovery amplifies short-scale errors and is ill-conditioned.


### Derive, test and transfer

For u_t=κu_xx with zero endpoints on [0,L], insert u=A(t)sin(nπx/L). The amplitude obeys A′=−κ(nπ/L)²A and decays exponentially. Higher-frequency spatial modes decay faster because two derivatives produce the square of their wavenumber.

Heat evolution smooths forward in time but reversing it amplifies high-frequency errors, making backward inference ill-conditioned. A finite-difference scheme also needs a stability condition; for explicit Euler in one dimension κΔt/Δx²≤1/2 is the standard interior bound. A smooth analytic mode display is not evidence that an arbitrary numerical time step is stable.

</LessonSection>
