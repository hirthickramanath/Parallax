<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Chemical equilibrium connects energy, entropy and composition. The same thermodynamic constraints apply in planetary atmospheres, laboratory mixtures and chemical models of astronomical environments, though equilibrium may not be reached.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Reaction direction is a downhill direction in the appropriate free energy, not necessarily in enthalpy alone. Equilibrium is dynamic balance, not molecular inactivity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

At constant temperature and pressure, reaction spontaneity is governed by Gibbs free energy. Equilibrium requires the reaction Gibbs change to vanish.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For enthalpy H=U+pV and Gibbs energy G=H−TS, a closed system at fixed temperature and pressure tends toward lower G subject to its constraints. Reaction enthalpy can be assembled by Hess's law because enthalpy is a state function: sum formation enthalpies of products minus reactants with stoichiometric coefficients. The chosen states and temperature must match.

For reaction stoichiometry νᵢ positive for products and negative for reactants, $\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q$, with $Q=\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}$ formed from dimensionless activities. For ideal gases aᵢ=pᵢ/p°, and dilute solutes use concentration relative to a standard state as an approximation. Pure solid or liquid activity is approximately one in the specified standard-state model.

At equilibrium Q=K and $\Delta_rG^\circ=-RT\ln K$. A large K favours products at equilibrium under the definition used; it says nothing about how quickly equilibrium is reached. At a given composition, $Q<K$ implies a negative forward reaction Gibbs change and a thermodynamic tendency toward products.

For a weak acid HA in an ideal dilute solution, $K_a=a_Ha_A/a_{HA}$. Together with mass balance and charge balance this determines composition. The shortcut x≈√(KₐC) requires x≪C and negligible water autoionization; verify both assumptions. pH is defined with hydrogen-ion activity, not simply a dimensional concentration.

Changing temperature alters K. Adding a catalyst changes rates but not the equilibrium constant of the same reaction at the same temperature. Pressure and concentration perturbations change Q and the constrained equilibrium composition; slogan-based shifts should be checked against the actual reaction quotient.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

A catalyst does not change the equilibrium thermodynamic states and therefore cannot change $K$ at fixed temperature. It can change the time needed to approach equilibrium. Dynamic equilibrium has nonzero balanced forward and reverse microscopic rates, not immobile molecules.

### Derive, test and transfer

For an ideal interconversion A⇌B at fixed temperature, ΔrG=ΔrG°+RT ln(aB/aA). At equilibrium the ratio is K=exp(−ΔrG°/(RT)). If the two activities track amounts in the same dilute volume and total amount is conserved, the product amount is N K/(1+K).

This inference combines thermodynamics with mass conservation; a favorable standard reaction does not mean complete conversion. Real activity coefficients, coupled reactions and heat exchange require further equations. The investigation varies a dimensionless standard-energy change, not a measured kinetic barrier. Equilibrium composition and the time needed to reach it remain distinct questions.

</LessonSection>
