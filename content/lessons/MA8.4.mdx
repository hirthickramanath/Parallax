<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Estimation and testing require distinguishing unknown parameters from random data. Frequentist error rates describe repeated procedures under a model, not automatic posterior probabilities for one claim.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A confidence procedure is a machine that builds an interval from random data. Its coverage is a statement about the machine across repeated datasets, with the parameter held fixed.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

An estimator is a function of data. Bias compares its mean with the parameter; a confidence interval has a specified repeated-sampling coverage under its model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For estimator T of θ, bias is Eθ[T]−θ and mean squared error decomposes as $E_\theta[(T-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)+\operatorname{Bias}_\theta(T)^2$. An unbiased estimator can have larger MSE than a slightly biased one. Maximum likelihood maximizes the data likelihood over the parameter domain, but does not automatically guarantee unbiasedness or calibrated intervals in finite samples.

For iid Gaussian observations with known σ, a two-sided interval for μ is $\bar x\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$. Its nominal coverage follows from the sampling distribution of the standardized mean. Replacing known σ by a sample estimate changes the pivot; under normal assumptions a Student-t construction accounts for that uncertainty. Model misspecification or dependence can invalidate either simple formula.

A p-value is the probability, under the specified null and test construction, of a result at least as extreme as the observed test statistic. It is not P(null|data), not the probability that the result occurred by chance in a model-free sense, and not the size of the effect. A composite null may need nuisance-parameter handling.

Type-I error rejects a true null and type-II error fails to reject a specified alternative. Power depends on that alternative, sample size and test rule. A large p-value does not prove the null; low power can hide substantial effects. Searching many outcomes or stopping selectively changes the sampling procedure and can inflate false-positive rates without correction.

Report effect sizes, uncertainty, model assumptions and search choices. A detection threshold alone is not a calibrated physical measurement. Coverage simulations can test an implementation under declared synthetic models, but they do not prove that the real data-generating mechanism matches those models.


### Derivation and transfer

For a known-variance Gaussian mean, z=(xbar−μ0)/(σ/√n). A two-sided p-value is computed under the null sampling model; it is not the probability that the null is true. Confidence coverage refers to repeated sampling of the interval procedure.

The display varies a standardized mean displacement at fixed sample size assumptions. Multiple testing, sequential stopping, model selection and unknown variance can require different calibration. Statistical significance alone does not establish a large or useful effect.

</LessonSection>
