<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Curvature measures failure of derivatives/transport to commute and underlies tidal gravity rather than merely nonzero coordinate connection.

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a torsion-free connection and a vector, $[\nabla_\mu,\nabla_\nu]v^\rho=R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}v^\sigma$ in the stated convention. Parallel transport around an infinitesimal loop differs from identity by curvature times area to leading order. Finite-loop holonomy can also reflect topology, so local and global conclusions must be separated.

For a Levi–Civita connection, the fully lowered Riemann tensor has antisymmetries, pair interchange symmetry and Bianchi identities. Ricci contraction and scalar curvature are selected contractions, not the complete tensor in every dimension. Vanishing scalar curvature alone does not prove the whole geometry flat.

For a round two-sphere of radius R, Gaussian curvature is 1/R² and scalar curvature is $\mathcal R=2/R^2$. Flat polar coordinates instead have zero curvature even though their Christoffel coefficients are nonzero. Dimensional and sign conventions matter when comparing references.

Curvature controls relative geodesic motion under an appropriately signed geodesic-deviation equation. One observer can choose locally inertial coordinates at a point, but cannot eliminate finite tidal effects over a region with nonzero curvature. Physical singularity diagnosis needs invariant/extendibility analysis, not merely one divergent chart component.

### Compute a sphere component rather than infer curvature from a picture

For $ds^2=R^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ away from the polar chart singularities, the nonzero connection coefficients are $\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi}=\Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi\theta}=\cot\theta$. Substitution in the stated convention gives

$$R^\theta{}_{\varphi\theta\varphi}=\partial_\theta(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)-(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)\cot\theta=\sin^2\theta.$$

Lowering the first index and dividing by the metric area factor gives Gaussian curvature $K=R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi}/(g_{\theta\theta}g_{\varphi\varphi})=1/R^2$. Contraction gives scalar curvature $2/R^2$. A mixed coordinate component can therefore be independent of radius while the invariant sectional curvature is not.

### A vanishing contraction can conceal curvature

Take the product of a two-sphere of curvature $+1/R^2$ and a hyperbolic plane of curvature $-1/R^2$, with the product Riemannian metric. Their scalar curvatures add to zero, but planes tangent to either factor still have nonzero sectional curvature. The four-dimensional product is not flat. In two dimensions alone the scalar determines the full Riemann tensor; dimensional assumptions change this conclusion.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a round sphere radius 2, Gaussian curvature is 1/4 and scalar curvature is 1/2. Doubling radius reduces both by four. A zero scalar in another manifold would not by itself certify zero Riemann curvature.

At radius 2 and $\theta=\pi/2$, the mixed component above is 1, the lowered component is 4 and the metric area factor is 16. Thus $K=4/16=1/4$. Comparing the component 1 directly to curvature in inverse-length-squared units would miss the metric conversion.

</LessonSection>
