<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Waves and cascades connect scales in fluids. Spectral laws apply to particular regimes rather than every visually disordered flow.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Fluid waves arise from a restoring mechanism and background state. Turbulence is a multiscale dynamical regime with fluctuations and transport, not simply any irregular motion.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Linearize compressible flow around a uniform static equilibrium and use δp=c_s²δρ with $c_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s$ to obtain acoustic waves. Isothermal and adiabatic sound speeds differ when heat exchange changes the perturbation closure. A stable background and sufficiently small disturbances belong to the approximation.

For inviscid surface waves of depth h without surface tension, ω²=gk tanh(kh). Shallow-water kh≪1 gives ω≈√(gh)k; deep water gives ω²≈gk. Capillary effects add another restoring term at sufficiently short wavelengths. A single nondispersive wave speed cannot cover all these limits.

In an idealized high-Reynolds-number three-dimensional turbulent inertial range, dimensional reasoning gives $E(k)\sim C\epsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}$, where ε is energy transfer/dissipation rate per mass. This requires an appropriate scale separation and statistical assumptions. The law is not an exact spectrum for anisotropic, compressible, magnetized or two-dimensional turbulence.

A forward cascade transfers kinetic energy toward smaller scales where gradients enable viscous dissipation. Resolving only large eddies misses that dissipative scale and requires a validated subgrid model or a qualified approximation. Intermittency, finite domain and forcing affect statistics. Label synthetic spectra and quantify averaging windows rather than inferring a cascade from a straight segment alone.


### Derive, test and transfer

Linear gravity waves on an inviscid incompressible fluid of uniform depth h satisfy ω²=gk tanh(kh) when capillarity is negligible. For kh small, tanh(kh)≈kh, giving shallow-water phase speed sqrt(gh). For kh large, ω²≈gk and phase speed depends on wavelength.

A dispersive wavepacket has group speed dω/dk rather than ω/k. Surface tension introduces an additional high-k term and viscosity damps motion. Turbulent spectra describe interacting fluctuating flows, not simply one linear surface mode. The investigation varies depth and wavenumber within the ideal gravity-wave relation so the shallow/deep limits can be checked quantitatively.

</LessonSection>
