<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Kinetics and electrochemistry ask complementary questions: how fast composition changes, and how chemical potential differences can drive electrical work. Rate barriers and free-energy differences are distinct.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

A high hill can separate two valleys even if the destination valley is lower. A catalyst lowers a route’s barrier without changing the valley depths.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

A rate law relates reaction rate to the state of a system through experimentally determined kinetics. A reversible cell voltage relates chemical free-energy change to charge transfer.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a simple first-order disappearance, $-d[A]/dt=k[A]$ gives $[A](t)=[A]_0e^{-kt}$ and half-life ln2/k. For a second-order law k[A]² the integrated relation is $1/[A]-1/[A]_0=kt$. Units of k depend on reaction order. Orders are not generally inferred from the overall balanced reaction; an elementary-step model needs justification.

The Arrhenius approximation $k=Ae^{-E_a/(RT)}$ gives a straight-line ln k versus 1/T slope −Eₐ/R when A and activation energy are effectively constant over the interval. Catalysts offer alternative mechanisms and affect both forward and reverse kinetics consistently with the same equilibrium constant. Diffusion or transport can instead control an observed rate.

For a reversible electrochemical cell with n moles of electrons transferred per mole of reaction, $\Delta_rG=-nFE$ and $E=E^\circ-(RT/nF)\ln Q$. E is the equilibrium open-circuit emf under the defined reaction direction, not automatically the terminal voltage under load. Activities enter Q. Changing reaction direction reverses E; multiplying reaction stoichiometry multiplies ΔG and n but leaves E unchanged.

Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode in both galvanic and electrolytic devices; their electrical signs depend on operation. Faraday's law relates charge It to electron amount It/F. For deposition, deposited mass is $m=MIt/(nF)$ at 100% current efficiency.

Ohmic loss, activation overpotential, concentration gradients and side reactions alter practical cells. Standard potential is an intensive quantity, so standard electrode potentials cannot be added as if they were extensive energies.

</LessonSection>
