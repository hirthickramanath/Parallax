<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Matrices encode coupled linear equations and transformations of coordinates or signals. Singular systems occur when measurements contain repeated information rather than independent constraints.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

The columns show where the basis arrows go. If those transformed arrows collapse into one direction, an area becomes zero and information is lost.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

A matrix represents a linear map after bases are chosen. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its kernel contains only zero.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A linear map obeys $T(\alpha u+\beta v)=\alpha T(u)+\beta T(v)$. Its columns are the images of basis vectors, so multiplying a matrix by a column vector forms their weighted sum. Matrix multiplication represents composition, with the rightmost map applied first. In general AB≠BA; dimensions must match.

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, the determinant is ad−bc. It is the signed area scale. If it is nonzero, $A^{-1}=(ad-bc)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$. If it is zero, the two columns are dependent and no unique inverse exists. An absolute determinant measures area scaling; the sign tracks orientation.

To solve Ax=b, row operations preserve the solution set when performed on the augmented matrix. A contradictory row 0=nonzero means no solution. A consistent system with free variables has multiple solutions. A square singular system need not have no solutions: it can have infinitely many.

The determinant of a product is the product of determinants. An eigenvector is a nonzero vector with Av=λv; an eigenvalue can be zero for a singular map. Eigenvectors describe invariant directions, not necessarily every possible direction. Repeated eigenvalues do not guarantee a full basis of eigenvectors.

Numerical conditioning is distinct from invertibility. A nearly singular matrix may amplify measurement errors strongly even though its determinant is not exactly zero. In scientific computation solve the system with a stable factorization rather than explicitly forming an inverse, and propagate uncertainty.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Solve x+y=3 and x−y=1. Adding gives x=2, then y=1. The coefficient determinant is −2, so the solution is unique. Replacing the second equation by 2x+2y=6 creates dependence and infinitely many solutions, not a contradiction.

</LessonSection>
