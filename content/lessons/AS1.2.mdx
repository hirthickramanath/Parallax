<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

If distance from the Sun caused seasons, why do the hemispheres have opposite summers? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Calendars, navigation and eclipse observations force us to distinguish several motions: daily rotation, annual revolution, and slower changes in orientation. Explaining them with one vague idea of closeness obscures the different geometries and timescales.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A tilted hemisphere faces the Sun more directly and for more hours during its summer. Its opposite hemisphere receives less direct illumination and shorter days at the same moment, even though both share almost the same Earth–Sun distance.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

The obliquity ε is the angle between Earth’s spin axis and its orbital normal. In a simplified circular-orbit model, solar declination varies with orbital longitude λ through sin δ = sin ε sin λ. Eclipse alignment additionally depends on the Moon’s inclined orbital plane and its nodes.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A surface patch receiving parallel sunlight has projected collecting area A cos z, where z is the solar zenith angle. Its instantaneous incident power is $P=F A\cos z$ for the illuminated face. Thus local flux is F cos z, not merely the orbital flux F.

For latitude φ and solar declination δ, the coordinate transformation gives

$$\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H.$$

At geometric sunrise/sunset, h=0, so

$$\cos H_0=-\tan\phi\tan\delta.$$

When a crossing exists, day length in solar-hour-angle units is $D=24H_0/\pi$ hours with H₀ in radians. If the right side lies outside [−1,1], interpret polar day/night rather than taking an impossible arccos. Refraction and the finite solar disk shift actual observed sunrise times.

At an equinox δ≈0 in this model, H₀=π/2 and D=12 h. At northern summer δ>0, a positive northern latitude generally has H₀>π/2 and longer daylight; the southern latitude has the opposite trend.

Orbital flux also scales as $F=L/(4\pi r^2)$ for an isotropic source. That shared distance factor cannot on its own produce opposite seasonal phases between the hemispheres.

For eclipses, conjunction/opposition is necessary but not sufficient: the Moon must also be near a node so the three-dimensional alignment is close enough. Axial precession arises from torques on Earth’s nonspherical mass distribution; a full rate calculation requires rigid-body dynamics, not simply this geometric day-length model.


### Derive, test and transfer

Resolve the Sun's ecliptic position along the equatorial north axis. For obliquity $\epsilon$ and solar ecliptic longitude $\lambda$, $\sin\delta=\sin\epsilon\sin\lambda$. Substitution into the transit-altitude formula gives the seasonal solar-noon height. Declination changes sign at the equinoxes and reaches approximately ±23.44 degrees at the solstices. Obliquity, rather than the small annual distance variation, explains opposite seasons in the two hemispheres.

The longitude parameter in the investigation is an angle, not a uniform date scale: eccentric orbital motion makes angular speed nonuniform. An eclipse additionally requires alignment near a node of the Moon's inclined orbit; a new or full Moon alone is insufficient. Axial precession changes the direction of Earth's axis over long times, while the annual changing solar direction occurs around that slowly moving reference. Keep these three geometries separate when predicting an observation.

</LessonSection>
