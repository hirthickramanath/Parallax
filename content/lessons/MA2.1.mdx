<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Multivariable models depend on simultaneous changes of several inputs. Testing one coordinate direction at a time cannot establish that every nearby direction behaves consistently.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A partial derivative samples one line. Total differentiability requires one linear approximation that controls errors from every direction together.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

At a point a, differentiability means f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)h+r(h), where the remainder divided by the norm of h tends to zero. The derivative is a linear map.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The partial derivative with respect to coordinate j is the directional limit along the corresponding basis vector. If a total derivative exists, these partials are its matrix columns. The converse is false: define f(x,y)=xy/(x²+y²) away from the origin and f(0,0)=0. Along either coordinate axis the value is zero and both partials at zero are zero. Along y=x the value is 1/2, so f is not even continuous there.

A sufficient theorem says that if all first partial derivatives exist in a neighbourhood of a and are continuous at a, then f is differentiable at a. To see the mechanism, telescope the increment by changing one coordinate at a time and use a one-variable mean value estimate on each segment. Continuity of the partials makes their coefficients approach one common linear map. These are sufficient conditions, not necessary conditions for every differentiable example.

For differentiable maps g from Rⁿ to Rᵐ and f from Rᵐ to Rᵖ, the chain rule is $D(f\circ g)(a)=Df(g(a))Dg(a)$. Substituting both linear approximations gives the product, while their remainders remain small relative to the original displacement. Matrix order follows the order of the maps; incompatible dimensions reveal an invalid composition.

A directional derivative along v, when total differentiability holds, is Df(a)v. Without total differentiability even the existence of every directional derivative need not produce one linear dependence on v. For example f(x,y)=x³/(x²+y²) with value zero at the origin has directional values v₁³/(v₁²+v₂²), not a linear function of v.

For a scalar field sampled along a differentiable trajectory r(t), $df(r(t))/dt=\nabla f\cdot\dot r$ when f is differentiable. This separates the spatial model from the path that probes it. Merely collecting coordinate partials without checking the model regularity is not a proof that the chain rule applies.


### Derive, test and transfer

For $f(x,y)=x^2y$ along x=t and y=t², direct substitution gives f=t⁴ and df/dt=4t³. The chain rule gives the same result: $f_x\dot x+f_y\dot y=(2xy)(1)+x^2(2t)$. Comparing both calculations checks that the partial derivative holds one coordinate fixed while the total derivative follows the path.

Existence of coordinate partial derivatives alone does not prove differentiability. For example, defining $g(x,y)=xy/(x^2+y^2)$ away from the origin and g(0,0)=0 gives zero coordinate partial derivatives at the origin but a nonzero limit along y=x. A valid linear approximation requires a remainder small compared with displacement norm, not merely derivatives along selected lines.

</LessonSection>
