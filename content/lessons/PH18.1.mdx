<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Research reading requires exact versions, evidence extraction and source checking rather than copying confidence from presentation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Start with the concrete worked example below. Track which quantities belong to the system, which are imposed by its surroundings, and which conclusions require a limiting approximation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A literature review maps claims to data, methods and assumptions, retaining bibliographic identifiers, versions, corrections and evidence status.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Start from one quantitative claim and trace its derivation/observations. A standardized residual $r=(x-x_{model})/\sigma$ is meaningful only under its measurement and uncertainty model. A claimed discovery significance may include additional trials/covariance not captured by that simple expression.

Use arXiv and INSPIRE as discovery/bibliographic tools, then inspect exact paper versions, journal status, errata, supplementary data/code and subsequent critique. A preprint, review and primary experiment serve different roles; citation counts are not validation.

Read parameter definitions, units, priors, selection and excluded regimes. Recompute one small check before trusting a broad interpretation. Distinguish a result actually reproduced from a proposal merely understood at the abstract level.

Maintain a claim-evidence matrix and label unread/unverified sections. Do not fabricate citations, quotations or page numbers to make a report appear complete. If a needed source is inaccessible, record the gap and continue independent checks without inventing its contents.

### Turn a claim into an auditable record

For each quantitative claim, retain the exact source identifier and version, the equation/table/figure used, definitions and units, data selection, uncertainty model and a status such as inspected, recalculated, replicated or unresolved. Record the command and software version for a computational check. An inspected equation is weaker evidence than reproducing its numerical result, and reproducing one result is weaker than independently validating its physical assumptions.

### Correlated errors change the check

For a residual vector $\mathbf r=\mathbf x-\mathbf m$ with known positive-definite covariance $C$, the quadratic statistic is $\chi^2=\mathbf r^TC^{-1}\mathbf r$. Summing individually squared standardized residuals assumes diagonal covariance. Even with a valid Gaussian model, estimating parameters changes the reference distribution and effective degrees of freedom; selection and multiple searches require their own treatment.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Create a reading note for one exact versioned source and extract definitions for a single equation. Separate observations, assumptions, deductions and open questions before planning an extension.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For hypothetical data 12, model 10 and standard uncertainty 2, the simple residual is 1. That does not by itself define a discovery p-value for a search over many targets.

Use a deliberately synthetic two-measurement example: residuals $(1,1)$, unit variances and correlation $\rho$. Inverting the two-by-two covariance gives $\chi^2=2/(1+\rho)$. At $\rho=1/2$ this is $4/3$, rather than the independent-error value 2. Positive correlation makes a shared residual less surprising in this specified model. At $\rho=1$ the matrix is singular, so the inverse formula cannot be used. No observational dataset or published discovery has been reproduced by this calculation.

</LessonSection>

**Core.** Repeat the calculation for residuals $(1,-1)$. Obtain $\chi^2=2/(1-\rho)$ and explain why the effect of positive correlation reverses.
