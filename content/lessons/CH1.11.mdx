<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Transport connects local gradients with material and energy flow. Constitutive laws and conservation equations are separate ingredients; one without the other does not determine evolution.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A gradient supplies a local imbalance, while conservation tracks whether the resulting inflow and outflow accumulate material.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Fick, Fourier and Newtonian-viscosity laws are linear constitutive models in their stated regimes. Their transport coefficients depend on material and conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For simple isotropic diffusion, molar flux is $J=-D\nabla c$. Conservation with no reaction gives ∂c/∂t=−∇·J. Constant D then yields $\partial_tc=D\nabla^2c$. Spatially varying D must remain inside the divergence. Advection and reaction add terms; the diffusion equation is not a complete model of every moving mixture.

In one-dimensional free diffusion, an initially localized distribution develops mean-square displacement 2Dt under the ideal Brownian model. In d Cartesian dimensions it is 2dDt. This scaling refers to ensemble displacements, not a deterministic distance every particle travels. Boundaries and anomalous transport change the long-time behavior.

Fourier's law is heat flux q=−k∇T with thermal conductivity k. Constant coefficients and energy conservation give a heat equation involving thermal diffusivity k/(ρcp) in a suitable simple setting. Thermal conductivity and diffusivity have different units and meanings.

For a Newtonian fluid in simple shear, stress is proportional to the velocity gradient with viscosity η. In general the tensor relation and incompressibility assumptions must be stated. Viscosity transports momentum rather than being a universal force opposing every motion at a fixed value.

Boundary conditions set concentrations, temperatures or fluxes and are essential for a solved transport problem. A no-flux boundary is not a zero-concentration boundary. Diffusion coefficients inferred from a finite experiment can be confounded with geometry, adsorption or reaction unless those processes are modeled.


### Derive, test and transfer

Fick's first law J=−D grad c gives flux down a concentration gradient for a simple isotropic dilute diffusion model. Combining it with local conservation yields the diffusion equation when D is constant and no sources or advection are present. The negative sign is a directional statement, not a negative diffusion coefficient.

For a one-dimensional free diffusion process, mean-square displacement is 2Dt; in three independent dimensions the total squared displacement is 6Dt. Boundaries, spatially varying diffusivity, flow and reactions change the problem. Viscosity and thermal conductivity describe different transported quantities, so their constitutive coefficients cannot be swapped merely because their equations have gradient forms.

</LessonSection>
