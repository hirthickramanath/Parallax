<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Systems of particles require separating overall translation from internal motion. Rigid-body kinematics describes how points move under constraints, before torque and inertia determine why they move that way.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A rigid body carries a fixed internal shape through space. At one instant its motion is a common translation plus rotation, so different points can have different velocities.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a fixed particle system. Rigidity preserves all pair separations and permits an instantaneous angular velocity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For constant particle masses and total mass M, $\mathbf R=M^{-1}\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i$ and total momentum $\mathbf P=M\dot{\mathbf R}$. Summing Newton's laws cancels internal forces when they occur in equal-and-opposite pairs, giving $M\ddot R=F_{\rm ext}$. This does not imply every internal torque cancels unless the interactions meet the appropriate torque condition.

Write ri=R+ρi and vi=V+ρ̇i. Since ∑miρi=0 and ∑miρ̇i=0, the kinetic energy splits into $K=MV^2/2+\sum_i m_i\dot\rho_i^2/2$. Angular momentum about a fixed inertial origin splits into R×MV plus angular momentum relative to the centre of mass. Reference points and their motion must be specified when applying a torque equation.

For a rigid body, velocities relative to two points satisfy $\mathbf v_B=\mathbf v_A+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A)$. For a continuously rotating body this follows by differentiating a length-preserving rotation matrix; its instantaneous generator is antisymmetric and corresponds to a cross product in 3D.

Differentiate again to get $\mathbf a_B=\mathbf a_A+\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf r_{BA}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{BA})$. There is no Coriolis term for two fixed points of the same rigid body because their relative rotating-frame velocity is zero. Sliding motion inside rotating coordinates would add one.

For a wheel rolling without slip on a stationary flat surface, the instantaneous contact point has zero velocity, while the centre moves at v=ωR. The contact point's acceleration need not be zero and the material contact point changes over time. An instantaneous centre is a kinematic construction, not necessarily a permanently fixed pivot or a valid shortcut for torque dynamics about an accelerating origin.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

For a closed system with constant particle masses, $\mathbf R=\sum m_i\mathbf r_i/M$ and $\mathbf P=M\dot{\mathbf R}$. Write $\mathbf r_i=\mathbf R+\boldsymbol\rho_i$ and $\mathbf v_i=\mathbf V+\mathbf v_i'$. Since $\sum m_i\boldsymbol\rho_i=\sum m_i\mathbf v_i'=0$, cross terms vanish, giving $T=P^2/(2M)+\sum m_i|\mathbf v_i'|^2/2$. Zero total momentum therefore does not imply zero energy.

The same decomposition gives angular momentum about a fixed origin as $\mathbf L=\mathbf R\times\mathbf P+\mathbf L_{cm}$. For a rigid body, $\mathbf v_i'=\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\rho_i$; different points need not have equal velocity. A rolling contact point can have zero instantaneous velocity without being a permanently fixed pivot or having zero acceleration. Those distinctions matter when choosing torque and acceleration equations.

</LessonSection>
