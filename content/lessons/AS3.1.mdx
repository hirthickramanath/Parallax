<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a spherical nonrotating Newtonian star in hydrostatic equilibrium, dp/dr=−Gm(r)ρ/r² and dm/dr=4πr²ρ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Integrating local gravity and pressure forces yields $dp/dr=-Gm\rho/r^2$. Pressure decreases outward while its gradient supports the fluid; the star does not need an outward force called heat separate from pressure. Rotation, magnetic stress, dynamical acceleration and general relativity modify this equation.

For an isolated stationary configuration with negligible surface pressure and the appropriate kinetic/internal-energy bookkeeping, the scalar virial relation is 2K+W=0 for nonrelativistic particles with inverse-distance gravity. More generally pressure integrals, surface terms, bulk motion and additional stresses enter. For an ideal monatomic gas, thermal U=K and total E=U+W=W/2=−U.

In that ideal regime, losing total energy makes E more negative and U larger: contraction can heat the star. This negative effective gravothermal response is not the fixed-volume canonical heat capacity of a gas in a rigid box. Nuclear energy, ionization, degeneracy and radiation pressure can alter the relation between temperature and contraction.

Hydrostatic balance is an instantaneous structural approximation when the evolution time is long compared with the dynamical time $t_{dyn}\sim\sqrt{R^3/(GM)}$. It does not ensure thermal equilibrium or nuclear stability. A model can be hydrostatic while slowly contracting or changing composition; violent collapse requires acceleration rather than a hydrostatic shortcut.


### Derive, test and transfer

A uniform-density sphere has enclosed mass $m(r)=4\pi\rho r^3/3$. Substitute into hydrostatic balance to obtain $dP/dr=-(4\pi/3)G\rho^2r$. Integration from r to the surface gives $P(r)-P(R)=(2\pi/3)G\rho^2(R^2-r^2)$. The center has a finite maximum pressure and the gradient vanishes there by symmetry.

The uniform density is an imposed toy assumption, not an equation of state. An actual stellar model simultaneously solves mass, pressure, energy generation and transport with boundary data. Its dynamical time can be short even while thermal adjustment is slow. Comparing a computed pressure profile with a plausible equation of state is a consistency check; matching one central pressure value does not establish a stable or thermally equilibrated star.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For an ideal virialized configuration with W=−20 in energy units, U=10 and total E=−10. If it loses total energy 2 and re-equilibrates in the same regime, E=−12 and U=12. The heating follows a changed gravitational configuration, not energy creation.



### Worked investigation: A contracting ideal star

For a uniform-density sphere of mass M and radius R, a shell contributes dW=-Gm(r)dm/r. With m(r)=M(r/R)^3 and dm=3Mr²dr/R³, integration gives W=-3GM²/(5R). In the isolated, nonrotating, monatomic ideal-gas regime with negligible surface pressure, 2U+W=0 and E=W/2. If R halves at fixed M and the star re-equilibrates, W, U and the magnitude of E double. The extra thermal energy comes from gravitational contraction; half the released gravitational energy is radiated.

For initial W=-120 J, U=60 J and E=-60 J. At half radius these are -240 J, 120 J and -120 J, so the radiated energy is 60 J. These tiny numbers are an energy-bookkeeping model, not a realistic stellar energy scale. Radiation pressure, degeneracy, mass loss and rapid collapse can invalidate the ideal relation.
