<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Gauss’s law connects charge to net field flux. Its symmetry-based applications are powerful precisely because the law alone does not specify every local field value.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Field can enter one part of a closed surface and leave another. Only the signed net crossing counts the enclosed charge.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

In vacuum electrostatics, electric field obeys Coulomb superposition, Gauss’s law and zero curl. Surface normals are outward and electric charge can have either sign.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A point charge q at r′ produces $E(r)=q(r-r')/[4\pi\epsilon_0|r-r'|^3]$ away from the source. Integrating against a charge density gives the continuum field where that integral exists. Self-fields of point charges require separate treatment; substituting r=r′ into the point formula is undefined.

For a point inside a closed surface, the oriented solid angle of the surface is 4π. Coulomb flux therefore gives $\oint E\cdot dA=Q_{\rm enc}/\epsilon_0$. Charges outside contribute zero net flux but can give nonzero field everywhere on parts of the surface. For sufficiently smooth fields and densities, the divergence theorem converts this to $\nabla\cdot E=\rho/\epsilon_0$. A point source instead needs a distributional divergence, not a zero-source inference from the formula away from its singularity.

Spherical symmetry makes E radial and its magnitude constant on a sphere. For a uniform solid sphere of radius R and total charge Q, enclosed charge at r&lt;R is Qr³/R³, giving $E_r=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0R^3)$. Outside, $E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$. The field is continuous at R because no additional ideal surface-charge sheet was specified.

For an infinite plane sheet with surface charge σ, a pillbox yields the normal-field jump σ/ε₀. Symmetry in identical vacuum on both sides gives magnitudes |σ|/(2ε₀) pointing away for positive charge. The infinite-sheet idealization has no natural zero of potential at infinity and differs from a finite disk at large distance.

A chosen Gaussian surface simplifies a calculation only when actual source symmetry fixes enough of the field. Drawing a sphere around an arbitrary charge arrangement does not make its field spherically symmetric. Evaluate signed normal components and state the reference charge distribution before using a one-number field estimate.


### Derive, test and transfer

Gauss' integral law fixes net outward flux through a closed surface from enclosed charge. Charges outside can contribute a nonzero field on the surface while their total flux through it cancels. The law is not an assertion that the electric field is zero whenever enclosed charge is zero.

To extract a local field magnitude from total flux, symmetry must justify a constant normal component or a known angular dependence. A charge pair generally lacks the spherical symmetry needed to divide flux by sphere area pointwise. The field probe illustrates superposition and lets you compare a zero-potential location with a nonzero field. In differential form the charge density is related to the full three-dimensional divergence; a two-dimensional slice plot must not be mistaken for all three spatial derivatives.

</LessonSection>
