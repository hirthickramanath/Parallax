<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Interference and diffraction show where ray optics loses information. Astronomical resolution depends on wavelength, aperture and propagation through the atmosphere, not simply on making the image bigger.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Add amplitudes with phase before converting to intensity. Equal waves can cancel at one place and reinforce elsewhere while redistributing energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

Coherent wave superposition gives interference. Diffraction arises when contributions from an extended wavefront are limited by an aperture or obstacle.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For two coherent contributions with individual intensities I₁ and I₂ and relative phase δ, $I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta$ for matched polarization. Time-varying random relative phase averages the cross term away. Coherence is about a stable correlation over the measurement, not merely identical advertised colours.

For two narrow slits separated by d and a distant screen, path difference is approximately d sin θ. Constructive interference occurs at d sin θ=mλ. For small angles at distance L, fringe spacing is $\Delta y=\lambda L/d$. Finite slit widths multiply this by a diffraction envelope; the two-slit formula alone does not describe all brightness variation.

A single slit of width a in the far field produces amplitude proportional to $\sin\beta/\beta$, with $\beta=\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda$. Noncentral minima satisfy a sin θ=mλ for nonzero integer m. As a shrinks, the central pattern broadens. This is not evidence that photons took individually measured trajectories through every point of the slit.

For a circular aperture the first dark ring lies at angle approximately $1.22\lambda/D$. The Rayleigh criterion is a conventional separation scale for two incoherent point sources, not a universal hard limit for every estimation problem. At λ=500 nm and D=0.1 m it gives 6.1×10⁻⁶ rad, about 1.26 arcseconds. Atmospheric seeing can dominate a ground-based telescope.

Polarization, bandwidth and near-field geometry can alter these scalar far-field expressions. State coherence, angular approximation and screen-distance assumptions before fitting a real pattern.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

For equal-amplitude coherent waves, adding fields and then squaring yields $I=2I_0[1+\cos\delta]$, where $\delta=2\pi\Delta/\lambda$ includes path and initial-phase differences. Maxima require integer $2\pi$ phase and minima odd $\pi$. The small-angle geometry $\Delta\simeq dy/L$ then gives fringe spacing $\lambda L/d$. If relative phase changes randomly during exposure, the averaged cross term tends toward zero; intensities remain but stable fringes do not. Finite slit width adds a diffraction envelope and unequal amplitudes prevent perfectly dark minima.


### Derivation and transfer

For two coherent equal-amplitude waves, intensity is proportional to 2I0(1+cosδ), ranging from zero to 4I0. Path difference converts to phase through δ=2πΔ/λ. Incoherent sources instead lose the stable interference cross term under averaging.

The display varies phase and single-source intensity. It does not include a diffraction envelope, finite coherence length or a resolved detector model. Amplitudes add before squaring; adding intensities alone misses coherent cancellation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

With λ=500 nm, L=2 m and slit separation d=0.5 mm, fringe spacing is 0.002 m=2 mm. Doubling slit separation halves the spacing; doubling wavelength doubles it. This does not change a detector’s physical pixel size.
