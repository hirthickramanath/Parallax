<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A group has associative composition, an identity and an inverse for every element. A homomorphism satisfies φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b).

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The group axioms do not require commutativity. A homomorphism sends identity to identity and inverses to inverses by applying the composition law. Its kernel is the elements mapped to identity; closure and inverse properties make it a subgroup.

For k in the kernel, $\phi(gkg^{-1})=\phi(g)\phi(k)\phi(g)^{-1}=e$, so the kernel is normal. Normality makes coset multiplication well defined in a quotient group. Arbitrary subgroups need not admit that quotient multiplication.

The first isomorphism theorem identifies G/kerφ with imφ through gkerφ→φ(g). Well-definedness follows because elements in the same coset differ by kernel information. Injectivity of this induced map and surjectivity onto the image complete the argument.

For finite groups, subgroup cosets partition G and give Lagrange’s divisibility relation. It does not claim that every divisor of group order occurs as a subgroup order in every group. Symmetry in physics also needs an action on states/observables; an abstract group alone does not specify what is transformed.


### Derivation and transfer

A homomorphism preserves the operation: φ(ab)=φ(a)φ(b). Its kernel is a normal subgroup, and two elements have the same image exactly when their quotient lies in the kernel. For finite groups, |G|=|kerφ| |imφ|.

The modular map from integers to residues illustrates a quotient; it does not prove that an arbitrary subset is a subgroup. The display uses addition modulo n with integer controls. Changing n changes the target group, not merely the labeling of the same map.

</LessonSection>
