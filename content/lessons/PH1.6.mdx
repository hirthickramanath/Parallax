<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

General rigid-body dynamics requires an inertia tensor rather than one scalar. It explains why tumbling spacecraft and rotating asymmetric bodies behave differently from a wheel about a fixed principal axis.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A mass element contributes according to its perpendicular displacement from the rotation. For an asymmetric body, these weighted contributions can tilt the total angular momentum away from angular velocity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

About the centre of mass, a rigid body has L=Iω with inertia tensor I. Principal axes diagonalize this real symmetric tensor; the principal moments are its eigenvalues.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For body-fixed coordinates ρi, the inertia tensor has components $I_{ab}=\sum_i m_i(\rho_i^2\delta_{ab}-\rho_{ia}\rho_{ib})$. Substituting the rigid velocity ω×ρ into angular momentum gives L=Iω. Rotational kinetic energy is $K_{\rm rot}=\omega\cdot I\omega/2$. Symmetry makes the tensor diagonalizable by orthonormal principal axes. Only rotation about a principal axis generally makes L parallel to ω.

About the centre of mass, inertial torque satisfies τ=(dL/dt)I. In body axes, the rotating derivative adds Ω×L. With fixed principal moments I1,I2,I3 and body angular velocity components ωi, Euler's first equation is $I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=\tau_1$, with cyclic companions. Dropping the cross terms is valid only in a special motion or symmetry, not because the tensor is diagonal.

For torque-free rotation, both inertial L and rotational energy are conserved, while ω and the body-axis components of L can vary. Linearize near rotation about axis 3: $\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\Omega\omega_2/I_1$ and $\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\Omega\omega_1/I_2$. Thus $\ddot\omega_1=\Omega^2(I_2-I_3)(I_3-I_1)\omega_1/(I_1I_2)$. Distinct principal moments give oscillatory perturbations for maximum- or minimum-moment rotation and growing perturbations for the intermediate axis. Degenerate moments need a separate interpretation.

For a rapidly spinning symmetric top with an approximately steady tilt and spin-dominated L≈I3ωs, balancing the slowly rotating angular-momentum vector gives precession rate approximately $\Omega_p=Mgr/(I_3\omega_s)$. This is a slow-precession approximation, not an exact formula for every launch condition; nutation and transient motion can occur.

An inertia tensor depends on reference point and axis convention. The parallel-axis theorem adds $M[(d\cdot d)\mathbf1-dd^T]$ when shifting from the centre of mass by vector d. Material deformation or fuel redistribution changes I and takes the system outside a fixed-rigid-body model.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

In principal body axes, $\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega$ and the transport equation is $\boldsymbol\tau=(d\mathbf L/dt)_{body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L$. With zero torque this yields $I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3$ and cyclic companions. The body components can change while inertial angular momentum and kinetic energy remain constant. The scalar formula $L=I\omega$ cannot replace a tensor relation away from a principal direction.

For $I_1<I_2<I_3$, perturb a spin $\Omega$ about axis 2 by small components $a=\omega_1$ and $c=\omega_3$. Linearization gives $I_1\dot a=(I_2-I_3)\Omega c$ and $I_3\dot c=(I_1-I_2)\Omega a$. Consequently $\ddot a=\lambda^2a$ with $\lambda=|\Omega|\sqrt{(I_2-I_1)(I_3-I_2)/(I_1I_3)}$, producing exponential growth. This is a local small-perturbation result, not a claim that the linear approximation stays valid after large tumbling.

</LessonSection>
