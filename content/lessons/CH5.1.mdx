<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Periodic trends describe families of neutral/ionic properties with specified charge and state; effective nuclear charge and shell occupancy are qualitative organising ideas.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Ionization energy is an energy difference, $I=E(N-1)-E(N)$ with a consistent reference for the removed electron. Electron affinity has competing sign conventions, so its definition must be stated. Neither property is identical to an atom’s partial charge inside a compound.

Across periods and down groups, shielding, radial extent and subshell occupancy compete. Pairing and half/filled-subshell effects produce exceptions to simple monotonic heuristics. An effective charge is a model parameter, not every electron experiencing one exact unscreened Coulomb atom.

Oxidation state is a formal convention; electronegativity scales differ and can depend on chemical context. Inert-pair and relativistic effects influence heavier p-block behaviour, but a label alone is not a full bonding calculation.

Use trends to propose comparisons, then verify with measured/modelled species under consistent conditions. Neutral atoms, gas-phase ions and solid compounds need not share one radius or reactivity ranking. This selected unit supports bonding and astrochemical materials rather than cataloguing every block reaction.


### Derive, test and transfer

A hydrogenic scale gives orbital size proportional to n²/Z and binding energy proportional to −Z²/n². Replacing Z by an effective charge can organize selected periodic trends, but it is an approximation that hides shielding, penetration and electron correlation.

Real multi-electron ionization energies and radii do not follow one exact universal hydrogenic curve. Oxidation state, orbital occupancy and the measurement definition matter. The display tests a stated effective-charge scaling; it is not a fitted table of every s/p-block element or a substitute for measured chemical behavior.

</LessonSection>
