<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A monochromatic plane wave uses a declared complex convention; take physical fields as real parts of E₀exp[i(k·r−ωt)]. Linear homogeneous media can have frequency-dependent ε and μ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In source-free vacuum, taking a curl of Faraday and using ∇·E=0 gives $\nabla^2\mathbf E-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0$. A plane wave has k·E=0 and B=(k×E)/ω, so ω=ck. Longitudinal electrostatic fields are not an extra transverse vacuum radiation polarization.

For a homogeneous isotropic nondissipative medium with constant positive ε,μ, k²=εμω² and impedance Z=√(μ/ε). Phase velocity is ω/k. In a dispersive medium group velocity dω/dk describes a narrow packet only under suitable weak-distortion assumptions. Front propagation and causal information are more restrictive notions, so a phase/group speed by itself cannot establish superluminal signalling.

With conductivity σ and e⁻ⁱωᵗ time convention, an effective permittivity is ε+iσ/ω for the elementary local Ohmic model. A physical attenuating branch has the appropriate positive spatial decay for the propagation direction. In a good conductor, skin depth is approximately √(2/(μσω)) when the good-conductor assumptions hold. Frequency dependence, anisotropy and nonlocal response can invalidate that simple formula.

Polarization specifies relative component amplitudes and phases: equal orthogonal amplitudes with quadrature phase give circular polarization under a stated viewing convention. Linear superposition is a property of the linear medium model. Absorption and scattering are different mechanisms of beam attenuation; an extinction coefficient alone need not identify which energy-transfer process occurred.


### Derive, test and transfer

In homogeneous lossless nondispersive matter with simple positive ε and μ, a plane wave has phase speed 1/sqrt(εμ). The vacuum expression c=1/sqrt(ε₀μ₀) is a special case. Frequency is fixed across a stationary interface while wavelength changes with phase speed.

Dispersive matter has frequency-dependent response, so phase and group velocities differ. A wavepacket envelope description requires a sufficiently narrow spectral band and a meaningful propagation regime. Loss makes propagation constants complex; substituting one real index into every formula can hide attenuation and causality. The investigation isolates the lossless scaling and does not model all material response.

</LessonSection>
