<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

Why can a star keep nearly fixed celestial coordinates yet move across your sky? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Predicting where to point a telescope requires both a sky catalogue and an observer-dependent transformation. A catalogue direction can remain nearly fixed over a short observation while the horizon rotates relative to it as Earth turns. Precision work additionally accounts for precession, proper motion and other effects.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

Imagine marking directions on a distant celestial sphere. Rotate the observer rather than the stars: the marks cross the local horizon even though their equatorial labels barely change. Time supplies the rotation angle.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

Declination δ is measured north or south of the celestial equator. Right ascension α is measured eastward along it, conventionally in hours where 24 h corresponds to 360°. Local hour angle H = local sidereal time − α, with a consistent angular unit and wrap convention. Altitude h is above the local horizon.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Use equatorial Cartesian axes with the celestial pole as z. A unit source direction is

$\mathbf s=(\cos\delta\cos\alpha,\cos\delta\sin\alpha,\sin\delta).$

At observer latitude φ and local sidereal angle θ, the local zenith direction is

$\mathbf z=(\cos\phi\cos\theta,\cos\phi\sin\theta,\sin\phi).$

Their dot product is cos of the zenith distance. Since altitude is 90° minus that distance, the same dot product equals sin h. Applying the cosine difference identity gives

$\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H,\quad H=\theta-\alpha.$

This is a geometric transformation, not a change in the star’s intrinsic position. Use radians consistently inside trigonometric calculations, even if catalogue angles are presented in hours or degrees.

At upper meridian transit H=0, the formula becomes sin h = cos(φ−δ), giving altitude $h=90^\circ-|\phi-\delta|$ when interpreted on the usual altitude branch. A source can be circumpolar or never rise depending on latitude and declination; those cases follow by checking the altitude extrema as cos H ranges from −1 to 1.

Sidereal time tracks Earth’s orientation relative to the sky, whereas civil/solar time tracks the Sun and administrative conventions. A sidereal day is slightly shorter than a mean solar day because Earth also advances around the Sun. Precision conversion needs a date/time standard and Earth-orientation model; a fixed universal subtraction is not adequate for astrometry.


### Derive, test and transfer

At upper meridian transit the spherical altitude relation reduces to $\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta=\cos(\phi-\delta)$. Choosing the physical altitude in $[-90^\circ,90^\circ]$ gives $h=90^\circ-|\phi-\delta|$. This is a maximum altitude, not the altitude throughout the day. More generally $\sin h=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H$, with hour angle H measured from the local meridian.

To test visibility, compare upper and lower culminations rather than treating right ascension as a clock time. At a northern latitude, a northern source is circumpolar when its declination exceeds $90^\circ-\phi$, ignoring refraction and horizon obstruction. Sidereal time advances relative to civil solar time because Earth also moves around the Sun. Precession changes the reference axes slowly; proper motion changes the star's direction. Neither is represented by a nightly rotation of the local horizon.



### Worked inference: Separate observer latitude from source declination

1. In the ideal geometric sky, an observer at latitude +30° sees a source of declination +20° reach upper culmination at altitude h=90°−|30°−20°|=80°.

2. Upper culmination means the upper meridian crossing; it does not mean the object is overhead. Overhead would require declination equal to latitude.

3. A negative computed altitude places that upper crossing below the geometric horizon. Atmospheric refraction, local terrain and the distinction between upper and lower culmination require additional treatment.

</LessonSection>
