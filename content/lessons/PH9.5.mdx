<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Identical fermion states are antisymmetric under particle exchange and boson states symmetric in the usual three-dimensional setting.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For two fermions, antisymmetrizing one-particle states gives $[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)-\phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)]/\sqrt2$ when the states are orthonormal. If a and b are the same complete state the expression vanishes. The complete spin-space labels matter, not only a spatial drawing.

Two spin-half states form a symmetric triplet and antisymmetric singlet. To make the total electronic state antisymmetric, a symmetric spatial factor pairs with the singlet and an antisymmetric spatial factor with triplet spin in the simple product construction.

For helium, nuclear attraction and electron–electron repulsion require a correlated two-electron problem. Independent-orbital/Hartree–Fock approximations capture some exchange and screening but do not include all electron correlation. Exchange integrals arise from antisymmetry, not a new classical force law added by hand.

Spin–orbit coupling and additional interactions can make separate spin/spatial labels approximate. State counting and energies must use consistent symmetry and Hamiltonian assumptions. Do not identify distinguishable labels used in calculations with physically distinguishable identical electrons.


### Derivation and transfer

Identical-fermion states are antisymmetric under exchange of all particle labels, including spin. Two electrons may share a spatial orbital only in an antisymmetric spin state. A Slater determinant with two identical spin-orbitals vanishes.

For K distinct one-particle spin-orbitals, N identical fermions have choose(K,N) occupation configurations. The display fixes N=2. It is a counting model, not a helium energy calculation or proof that electron interactions can be ignored.

</LessonSection>
