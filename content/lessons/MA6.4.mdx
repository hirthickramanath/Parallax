<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Local singularity structure helps classify response functions and evaluate contour integrals. An expansion is incomplete without its annulus of convergence.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A Laurent series is a local description on a ring. Expanding inward or outward relative to a nearby singularity changes which geometric series is valid.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A Laurent expansion ∑aₙ(z−a)ⁿ includes integer powers of both signs and represents a holomorphic function on an annulus. Isolated singularities are classified by the negative-power part near the centre.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If f is holomorphic on r&lt;|z−a|&lt;R, it has a unique Laurent series on that annulus, converging uniformly on compact subannuli. The coefficients satisfy $a_n=(2\pi i)^{-1}\oint f(\zeta)/(\zeta-a)^{n+1}\,d\zeta$ on any admissible positively oriented circle inside the annulus. Different annuli separated by singularities can require different expansions of the same function.

Near an isolated singularity a, no negative powers means a removable singularity; finitely many negative powers with a₋ₘ≠0 means a pole of order m; infinitely many nonzero negative powers means an essential singularity. A bounded holomorphic function on a punctured disk has a removable singularity. A residue of zero does not imply removability: 1/(z−a)² has a double pole but no (z−a)⁻¹ coefficient.

For $f(z)=1/[z(1-z)]$, on 0&lt;|z|&lt;1 the geometric expansion is z⁻¹+1+z+z²+…. For |z|>1, rewrite it as $-z^{-2}(1-z^{-1})^{-1}=-\sum_{j\ge0}z^{-j-2}$. The outer series has no z⁻¹ term. This does not contradict uniqueness because the annuli differ; a circle outside the pole at one encloses contributions from both finite poles.

At zero, e¹ᐟᶻ has infinitely many negative powers and an essential singularity; sin z/z extends holomorphically with value one. Essential singularity theorems describe highly variable local behaviour, but do not make every value attained exactly at every nearby point. When invoking a stronger theorem about exceptional values, state its precise hypotheses and conclusion rather than replacing it with a picture.

To study infinity, set w=1/z and classify f(1/w) at w=0. The residue at infinity includes the differential: $\operatorname{Res}_\infty f=-\operatorname{Res}_{w=0}[f(1/w)/w^2]$. For a meromorphic function on the sphere, the finite residues plus the residue at infinity sum to zero. Confusing the singularity of f at infinity with the residue of f(z)dz misses the Jacobian and sign.


### Derivation and transfer

In an annulus, a Laurent expansion has both positive and negative powers; its coefficients depend on the annular region. For 1/[z(z−1)] near zero with 0 &lt; |z| &lt; 1, partial fractions give −1/z−1−z−z²−… . The residue at zero is −1.

A finite principal part indicates a pole, whereas infinitely many negative powers indicate an essential isolated singularity. Convergence regions must accompany a series; crossing another singularity changes the expansion. The explorer compares a finite truncation only on its stated positive-radius interval, not at the puncture.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>
