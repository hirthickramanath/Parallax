<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Tides arise from differential gravity and a body’s response to it. They affect planetary deformation, satellite survival and stellar encounters; a force evaluated only at a body’s centre misses the essential gradient.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Compare the external acceleration at a displaced point with the acceleration of the body’s centre. On the near side attraction is stronger; on the far side it is weaker, producing opposite relative accelerations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

The leading tidal field is the spatial gradient of external gravitational acceleration in a freely falling frame. Its effects depend on material strength, self-gravity, rotation and time-dependent response.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a distant point mass M at separation R, let the local x axis point toward it. Expand the external potential about the centre and remove the constant and uniform-acceleration terms. The leading tidal acceleration is $\delta\mathbf g=(GM/R^3)(2x,-y,-z)$. It stretches along the line of centres and compresses transverse directions. The approximation requires a local displacement much smaller than R.

The characteristic differential acceleration across radius r is $2GMr/R^3$. It scales as inverse separation cubed, unlike the inverse-square centre acceleration. Comparing with self-gravity Gm/r² gives a rough disruption scale proportional to $r(M/m)^{1/3}$. This order-of-magnitude balance does not include an actual fluid equilibrium shape, rotation or strength.

For an ideal synchronously rotating incompressible fluid satellite, a commonly quoted Roche separation is approximately $2.44R_p(\rho_p/\rho_s)^{1/3}$ from the primary centre. The coefficient is model-specific. A rigid or strength-supported object can survive closer; a weak rubble pile has other failure conditions. The Roche limit is not a universal radius at which every object instantly disintegrates.

A tidal bulge with a phase lag dissipates mechanical energy. Angular momentum can transfer between spin and orbit while energy becomes heat. Whether a satellite migrates inward or outward depends on the relation between orbital angular speed and the primary's spin, not solely on its presence inside or outside a guessed diagrammatic bulge.

Ocean tide amplitudes are not the static equilibrium bulge alone: coastlines, bathymetry, resonances, rotation and weather matter. Likewise solid-body tidal response requires rheology. A simple tidal tensor correctly explains the leading differential forcing but cannot forecast a local high tide or a material rupture time.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

For radial gravity $a_r=-GM/r^2$, a small radial displacement changes acceleration by $\Delta a_r\simeq(2GM/r^3)\ell$. This is a first derivative of the field and explains the inverse-cube tide law. In an orthonormal local frame, point-source Newtonian tidal eigenvalues are proportional to $(2,-1,-1)GM/r^3$: stretching in one direction and compression in two. Their sum vanishes in vacuum even though each is nonzero. Roche estimates additionally compare tides with self-gravity and depend on rotation, structure and material strength; a fluid numerical coefficient cannot be universally applied to a cohesive solid.


### Derive, test and transfer

A differential gravitational acceleration is found by subtracting the center-of-mass acceleration before expanding the perturber's field. Along the radial direction, $g(r-R)-g(r)\simeq 2GMR/r^3$ for $R/r\ll1$. The leading tide is proportional to body size and to the inverse cube of separation, whereas the common acceleration of the whole body is proportional to the inverse square.

Compare the tide to the body's own surface gravity, $Gm/R^2$. Equating orders gives a disruption separation proportional to $R(M/m)^{1/3}$, or to the cube root of a density ratio. The numerical Roche coefficient depends on fluid response, rotation and geometry; the simple differential-force balance does not supply it. Rigid material strength can support small objects beyond a fluid estimate. The investigation is therefore a local tide model, not a simulation of nonlinear disruption or ocean response.

</LessonSection>
