<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Geometrical optics models light as rays where wavelength is small relative to relevant structures. Telescopes use it for image placement, but diffraction later sets the resolution limit.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Follow several rays from one object point. A real image is where outgoing rays meet; a virtual image is where their backward extensions meet.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

Reflection preserves the angle to a surface normal. Refraction obeys n₁ sin θ₁=n₂ sin θ₂ for isotropic media at a stationary interface.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Fermat's stationary optical-path principle produces the law of reflection and Snell's law under the ray approximation. Angles are measured from the normal, not the surface. For light going from higher index n₁ to lower n₂, a transmitted propagating ray ceases when $\sin\theta_1>n_2/n_1$: total internal reflection occurs above the critical angle. An evanescent field may still exist outside the boundary.

Use a thin converging lens of positive focal length f and real object distance u>0 on the incoming side. With image distance v positive on the outgoing side, $1/f=1/u+1/v$. The transverse magnification is m=−v/u. This equation follows from paraxial rays and similar triangles; large apertures, thick lenses and strong off-axis rays require more detailed models.

For u>f, v>0 gives a real inverted image that can be projected. For $0<u<f$, $v<0$ and $m>0$ give an upright virtual image. At u=f, outgoing paraxial rays are parallel and no finite image plane exists. A real image can be magnified or reduced, so real does not mean larger.

For a distant source a telescope objective forms an image near its focal plane. A small field angle θ corresponds to height approximately fθ in radians. An eyepiece can magnify the angular view, while a detector can record the focal plane directly. More magnification cannot recover spatial detail destroyed by aberration, atmospheric blur or finite-aperture diffraction.

Energy is not determined by ray intersection alone. Reflection, absorption and finite aperture affect throughput. Chromatic refractive index and nonparaxial aberrations shift image location and shape.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Apparatus and investigation" layer="interactive">

With a classroom converging lens and a safe illuminated object, move a screen to find a sharp image for u>f. Measure u and v from the approximate lens plane and plot 1/v against 1/u. Never point an optical system at the Sun; use a lamp or printed target.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For f=10 cm and u=30 cm, 1/v=1/10−1/30=1/15 cm⁻¹, so v=15 cm and m=−0.5. Moving the object to u=5 cm yields v=−10 cm and m=2, an upright virtual image.

</LessonSection>
