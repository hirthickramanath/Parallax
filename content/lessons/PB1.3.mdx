<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

Why can a rolling object have zero velocity at its contact point while its centre moves? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Wheels turn a local contact constraint into global motion. Treating a wheel only as a translating point loses the rotation, while treating it as rotating about a fixed centre loses the moving centre. Combining both descriptions resolves the apparent contradiction at contact.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

At the bottom of a rolling wheel, the rotational velocity relative to the centre opposes the centre’s translational velocity. Equal magnitudes cancel at that instant. That particular material point then moves away from contact; it is not permanently fixed to the ground.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

For a rigid body, v_P = v_cm + ω×r_P/cm. The rotational inertia about an axis is ∫r_perp² dm. For rolling without slip on a fixed plane, the tangential velocity of the contact point is zero. Static friction can enforce this constraint without dissipative work in the ideal fixed-surface model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The kinetic energy of rigid motion is the sum over particles:

$$K=\frac12\sum_i m_i|\mathbf V+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_i|^2.$$

Choose the centre of mass so ∑m_i𝐫_i=0. The cross term vanishes. For rotation about a fixed direction this leaves

$$K=\frac12MV_{cm}^2+\frac12I_{cm}\omega^2.$$

At contact on a stationary plane, the tangential velocities cancel: V_cm−Rω=0, hence V_cm=Rω. A slipping object need not satisfy this equality.

For a uniform thin disk, divide it into annuli: dm=2πσr dr. Then $I=\int_0^R r^2(2\pi\sigma r)dr=\pi\sigma R^4/2=MR^2/2$. A thin hoop has all its mass at radius R, so I=MR².

For a body released from rest rolling down a vertical drop h without losses,

$$Mgh=\frac12Mv^2+\frac12I\frac{v^2}{R^2},\qquad v^2=\frac{2gh}{1+I/(MR^2)}.$$

A disk therefore has v²=4gh/3; a hoop has v²=gh. Shape matters through mass distribution, even though total mass cancels.

One can also derive acceleration down an incline. With friction f up the slope, Mg sinα−f=Ma and fR=I(a/R). Eliminating f gives $a=g\sin\alpha/[1+I/(MR^2)]$. This solution must be checked against the maximum available static friction; otherwise the assumed no-slip motion is inconsistent.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the assumptions" layer="interactive">

Compare an ideal disk and hoop released through the same height. Predict which arrives faster, compute v²/(gh), and then ask what happens if the surface cannot provide enough static friction. The pure rolling formula is not a model for sliding.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a uniform disk descending h=1 m at g=9.81 m/s², v=√(4gh/3)≈3.62 m/s. The corresponding hoop reaches √(gh)≈3.13 m/s. The disk has less rotational inertia relative to MR².

</LessonSection>
