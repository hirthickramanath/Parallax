<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Nonlinear systems can change qualitative behaviour as a parameter varies. Linearization is powerful near hyperbolic equilibria, but its borderline cases require nonlinear information.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Approximate the vector field by its tangent map near an equilibrium. If that map has no decisive growth or decay in one direction, higher-order terms can dominate it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Lyapunov stability means sufficiently small initial perturbations remain small for future time. Asymptotic stability additionally requires nearby states to approach the equilibrium.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a continuously differentiable vector field with equilibrium x*, write x=x*+ξ. The linearized equation is $\xi'=DF(x_*)\xi$. If every eigenvalue has strictly negative real part, the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable under the standard linearization theorem. A positive-real-part eigenvalue implies instability. Zero real parts do not permit either conclusion from that criterion alone.

The scalar systems x′=−x³ and x′=x³ both have zero derivative at x=0. The first attracts nearby states; the second repels them and can blow up forward in time. Their identical zero linearizations therefore miss the decisive sign.

A Lyapunov function V near an equilibrium is positive away from the equilibrium and zero there. Along trajectories $\dot V=\nabla V\cdot F$. A nonpositive derivative supports stability under suitable local hypotheses; a negative-definite derivative gives a standard asymptotic-stability result. A derivative that is only semidefinite does not by itself prove attraction: an undamped oscillator conserves its energy and does not settle to the origin.

For x′=−x³, choose V=x²/2. Then V̇=−x⁴&lt;0 away from zero, establishing local asymptotic stability, with algebraic rather than exponential decay. For a damped mechanical system an energy function often decreases, but to prove convergence when its derivative vanishes on a larger set one needs an invariance argument such as LaSalle's under its compactness assumptions.

A saddle-node example is x′=μ−x². For μ>0 the equilibria ±√μ have derivative −2x, making the positive one stable and negative one unstable. At μ=0 they merge into a nonhyperbolic equilibrium; for μ&lt;0 there are none. This is a local normal-form illustration, not a universal description of every parameter-dependent system. Periodic forcing, higher dimensions and global basins require further analysis.


### Derive, test and transfer

Linearization of x′=f(x) at an equilibrium x* gives δx′≈f′(x*)δx. A negative derivative gives local exponential attraction in the one-dimensional smooth hyperbolic case. If the derivative is zero, higher-order terms decide behavior: x′=−x³ attracts while x′=x³ repels despite identical zero linearizations.

A Lyapunov function can settle such cases. For V=x²/2 and x′=−x³, its derivative along motion is −x⁴, negative away from equilibrium, while the exact decay is algebraic rather than exponential. Stability, attraction and rate of convergence must not be conflated. Nonlinear or finite-amplitude behavior can differ from what a truncated linear test suggests.

</LessonSection>
