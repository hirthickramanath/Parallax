An equation of state supplies pressure and internal energy from density, temperature and composition in a stated thermodynamic regime.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a fully ionized nondegenerate ideal gas, $p_g=\rho k_BT/(\mu m_u)$, where μ counts particles per mass including ions and electrons. In ionization zones μ and internal-energy storage depend on temperature and density, so a fixed fully ionized value is not universal.

Equilibrium radiation contributes $p_r=aT^4/3$. Gas and radiation can be added in local thermodynamic equilibrium when their coupling and constitutive assumptions justify it. Radiation pressure scales with temperature differently from gas pressure and can alter stability and transport.

A cold ideal degenerate electron gas has pressure proportional to (ρ/μ_e)⁵ᐟ³ in the nonrelativistic regime and to (ρ/μ_e)⁴ᐟ³ in the ultrarelativistic regime. Electron number per mass sets μ_e. These are different limits of Fermi statistics, not interchangeable polytropic choices for arbitrary density or finite temperature.

Coulomb corrections, partial degeneracy, electron–positron pairs, magnetic fields and nuclear interactions can matter in extreme regimes. Pressure derivatives and adiabatic exponents must come from the same thermodynamically consistent model as internal energy. A guessed interpolation can violate stability or thermodynamic identities even if its pressure curve looks smooth.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a nonrelativistic degenerate electron gas, the occupied momentum-space volume implies $p_F\propto n_e^{1/3}$. Since kinetic energy per electron scales as $p_F^2/(2m_e)$, the energy density and pressure scale as $n_e^{5/3}$. In the ultrarelativistic limit particle energy scales as p_Fc, producing pressure proportional to $n_e^{4/3}$ instead. Composition determines the conversion from mass density to electron density.

An ideal monatomic adiabatic gas also has a 5/3 exponent, but its entropy and temperature dependence differ from cold degeneracy. Radiation pressure scales as T⁴ rather than a single universal density power. A polytropic law P=Kρ^γ is useful only when K and γ capture the stated regime; ionization zones, mixtures and degeneracy transitions can defeat a fixed exponent. The model lets you compare compression slopes without pretending to determine an entire equation of state.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In normalized units k_B/m_u=1, ρ=6,T=4 and μ=2 give gas pressure 12. If a=3 at T=2, radiation pressure is aT⁴/3=16. These are separate example states, not one combined pressure at inconsistent temperatures.



### Worked investigation: When radiation pressure becomes important

Compare P_gas=rho*k_B*T/(mu*m_u) with P_rad=a_rad*T^4/3. Their ratio is a_rad*mu*m_u*T³/(3*rho*k_B): at fixed composition it grows as T³/rho, rather than merely with temperature. If the initial ratio is 0.10, doubling T at fixed density makes it 0.80. Doubling both T and rho makes it 0.40. This comparison assumes local thermal equilibrium and a classical nondegenerate gas.

Nonrelativistic electron degeneracy gives P proportional to rho^(5/3) at fixed composition, while the ultrarelativistic limit gives rho^(4/3). Neither is obtained by setting an ideal-gas temperature to zero. A real equation of state must connect regimes and include composition and interactions.
