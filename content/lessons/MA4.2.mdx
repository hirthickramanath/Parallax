Why can an impulse response predict the response to an arbitrary forcing? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Linear ODEs admit superposition, allowing a general input to be decomposed into simpler contributions. Boundary conditions and causality determine which Green function performs the reconstruction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A small pulse launches a response. Adding delayed, weighted copies builds the response to an extended signal when the system is linear.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a linear differential operator L with specified initial or boundary conditions, a Green function solves LₜG(t,s)=δ(t−s) with the associated homogeneous conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a second-order equation $y''+p(t)y'+q(t)y=f(t)$ with continuous coefficients, two independent homogeneous solutions span the homogeneous solution space. Their Wronskian W=y₁y₂′−y₁′y₂ is nonzero on an interval when independence holds; Abel's identity W′=−pW explains why a nonzero W cannot vanish at an isolated interior point under these assumptions.

Variation of parameters seeks a particular solution u₁y₁+u₂y₂ and imposes u₁′y₁+u₂′y₂=0 to remove redundant freedom. Solving the resulting two-by-two system yields $u_1'=-y_2f/W$ and $u_2'=y_1f/W$. The integration constants contribute homogeneous terms and are fixed with the actual conditions.

For $y''+\omega_0^2y=f(t)$ with zero initial displacement and velocity at t=0, the retarded Green function is $G(t,s)=H(t-s)\sin[\omega_0(t-s)]/\omega_0$. It vanishes before the impulse and has a unit jump in its first t derivative at t=s. That jump produces the delta forcing. Then $y(t)=\int_0^tG(t,s)f(s)ds$; nonzero initial data adds a homogeneous solution.

For a general leading coefficient a(t), the derivative jump is 1/a(s), not universally 1. For boundary-value problems, the Green function may be noncausal because endpoint conditions differ from initial data. At resonant boundary eigenvalues an inverse can fail to exist or need compatibility conditions; a formal integral does not bypass that obstruction.

Time-translation invariance makes a Green kernel depend on t−s, giving convolution. A time-varying linear system can still have a two-time Green function but not a stationary convolution. Nonlinear equations generally do not obey this superposition rule. Distributional impulses describe an ideal limit of short forcing, not an infinite measured force applied for a finite duration.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For y″+4y=1 with zero initial conditions, integrate sin[2(t−s)]/2 from s=0 to t. The result is y=(1−cos2t)/4. Its value and first derivative vanish at zero, and y″+4y=cos2t+(1−cos2t)=1.
