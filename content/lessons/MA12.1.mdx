<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A metric defines distances and an induced topology; a topology specifies open sets without necessarily choosing one distance function.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A metric satisfies nonnegativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry and the triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$. Its open balls generate open sets. A sequence converges when eventually inside every neighbourhood of its limit; a metric Cauchy sequence instead controls pairwise late distances.

Completeness means every Cauchy sequence converges within the space. The interval (0,1) with the usual metric is not complete because 1/n for n≥2 tends toward a missing endpoint. Completeness depends on the metric, while topological equivalence alone need not preserve it for a specific distance choice.

Compactness is the finite-subcover property. In Euclidean Rⁿ, Heine–Borel identifies compact sets with closed bounded sets, but that equivalence is not universal in arbitrary metric or infinite-dimensional spaces. A continuous real function on a compact domain attains extrema.

Connectedness, path connectedness and simple connectedness are different properties. A hole can obstruct global primitives without preventing ordinary connectedness. Physical spaces also need a specified topology and admissible boundary conditions; drawing one coordinate patch does not settle their global structure.


### Derivation and transfer

A metric supplies nonnegative distance, symmetry, identity of indiscernibles and the triangle inequality. It induces open balls and a topology, but different metrics can induce the same topology. Convergence is defined through neighborhoods, not a particular drawing.

For vectors (x,1), the p-norm is (|x|^p+1)^(1/p), with p≥1. The display compares finite-dimensional norms. Equivalent finite-dimensional norms do not imply identical distances or extend automatically to every infinite-dimensional space.

</LessonSection>
