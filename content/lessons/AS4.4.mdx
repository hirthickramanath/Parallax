Why can a star’s brightness oscillation reveal something about its interior? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Pulsation modes respond to internal sound speed, buoyancy and rotation, but mode identification and driving must be separated from a mere periodic light curve.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Start with the concrete worked example below. Track which quantities belong to the system, which are imposed by its surroundings, and which conclusions require a limiting approximation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Stellar oscillations are perturbations of a background structure. Pressure and buoyancy provide restoring forces for different mode families.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A characteristic acoustic crossing time is R/c_s, while a radial dynamical period scales with $\sqrt{R^3/(GM)}$. Thus similar-structure pulsators have an approximate period–mean-density relation P∝ρ_bar⁻¹ᐟ². The coefficient depends on the mode and stellar model.

Acoustic p modes and buoyancy g modes sample different structures. A positive squared Brunt–Väisälä frequency indicates stable buoyancy oscillations in the stated convention, while an unstable stratification changes the interpretation. Rotation and magnetic fields split or couple modes.

Mode driving includes opacity-related heat-engine mechanisms and stochastic forcing by convection in different classes. A structural eigenfrequency is not by itself a proof that a mode is excited to observable amplitude. Damping, nonlinear saturation and surface visibility influence the light curve.

Asteroseismic inference compares mode frequencies, spacings and splittings with models. Cadence, gaps, aliases, finite duration and blended companions affect observed spectra. A peak can be an instrumental artifact or rotation signal, so mode identification needs diagnostics. Synthetic oscillation curves must not be relabelled as actual telescope observations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Generate a synthetic multi-frequency light curve with recorded cadence and gaps, then compare its periodogram to the known inputs. Change the window and show aliases. Use model frequency ratios separately from an excitation/amplitude claim.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If two similar-structure pulsators differ in mean density by a factor of four, the denser one has half the characteristic period. A sampled 2-cycle-per-day signal needs cadence and a long enough baseline to distinguish aliases; a frequency label alone does not establish a radial mode.

</LessonSection>
