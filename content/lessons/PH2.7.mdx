<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Normal modes diagonalize the linearized motion near a stable equilibrium. General initial conditions superpose modes, producing mixed patterns and beat-like energy exchange without dissipation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Choose combinations of coordinates that stretch the springs independently. In-phase and opposite-phase displacements are natural patterns for a symmetric two-mass system.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a stable small-oscillation model, quadratic kinetic and potential forms give Mq̈+Kq=0 with positive-definite mass matrix. Normal frequencies solve Ku=ω²Mu.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Expand the potential around an equilibrium q₀ with zero first derivative. To quadratic order, V≈V₀+ξᵀKξ/2, where K is the symmetric Hessian. The corresponding kinetic form is ξ̇ᵀMξ̇/2 under the usual equilibrium small-motion assumptions. Stability requires the relevant potential form to be nonnegative; zero modes need separate interpretation.

Seeking ξ=u cosωt gives the generalized eigenproblem $Ku=\omega^2Mu$. For positive-definite M, mass-weighting converts it into a symmetric eigenproblem for M⁻¹ᐟ²KM⁻¹ᐟ². Distinct modes are orthogonal in the M-weighted inner product, not necessarily in the naive coordinate dot product.

For two identical masses m connected to walls by springs k and to one another by spring kc, $K=\begin{pmatrix}k+k_c&-k_c\\-k_c&k+k_c\end{pmatrix}$. Symmetric and antisymmetric coordinates q±=(x₁±x₂)/√2 have frequencies $\omega_+=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\omega_-=\sqrt{(k+2k_c)/m}$. In-phase motion does not stretch the coupling spring; opposite-phase motion does.

For zero initial velocities, each normal coordinate evolves as q±(t)=q±(0)cos(ω±t). Recombining gives the two mass displacements. A one-mass release excites both modes. The resulting envelopes need not be a single oscillator frequency, and the elementary close-frequency beat description is only appropriate when the two frequencies are sufficiently close.

Conserved energy is m(v₁²+v₂²)/2+k(x₁²+x₂²)/2+kc(x₂−x₁)²/2 in this ideal model. Real damping, large-amplitude nonlinear springs and unequal masses require extensions. The mode decomposition is an approximation to the actual system when derived from a small-displacement expansion.


### Variational derivation and transfer case

Expand near a stable equilibrium as $T=\dot q^T M\dot q/2$ and $V=V_0+q^T Kq/2$, with real symmetric M positive definite. A mode $q=v e^{i\omega t}$ obeys $Kv=\omega^2Mv$, not an ordinary stiffness-only eigenproblem when M is nontrivial. Multiplication by $M^{-1/2}$ gives the symmetric mass-weighted problem.

For distinct eigenvalues, symmetry gives $(\omega_i^2-\omega_j^2)v_i^TMv_j=0$, so modes are orthogonal in the mass inner product. In a degenerate eigenspace that subtraction is uninformative, although an M-orthonormal basis can be constructed. General initial data are expanded in the chosen mode basis; observing one coordinate oscillate is not proof that only one mode is excited.

</LessonSection>
