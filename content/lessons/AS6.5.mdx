<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Dynamo theory couples flow geometry, diffusion and nonlinear feedback; a kinematic growth result does not specify a saturated galactic field.

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A dynamo is sustained magnetic-field amplification through conducting-fluid motion under a model that includes transport and feedback conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The induction equation contains advection/stretching and magnetic diffusion. In an incompressible ideal limit the material field evolution includes $(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v$, showing how velocity gradients can stretch and amplify field strength while respecting ∇·B=0. Amplification does not mean magnetic monopoles or arbitrary net flux creation.

A kinematic calculation prescribes the flow and neglects Lorentz-force backreaction. It can estimate growth rates and mode structures but cannot determine final saturation once magnetic stresses alter the fluid. Energy growth must be supplied by flow/forcing rather than appearing without an energy source.

Mean-field models split fields into averages and fluctuations; their electromotive-force closure can involve α and turbulent-diffusion effects under scale/separation assumptions. Averaging definition, helicity, boundaries and shear matter. Anti-dynamo constraints show that not every simple flow geometry sustains growth.

Faraday rotation probes a signed electron-density-weighted line-of-sight field integral, while synchrotron polarization and dust polarization have different weights and directional ambiguities. Combining observables needs transport, electron and foreground models. A field-strength estimate is conditional inference, not a direct three-dimensional field map from one line of sight.


### Derive, test and transfer

With uniform magnetic diffusivity, the induction equation contains $\eta\nabla^2B$. A spatial Fourier mode with wavenumber k decays as $B_k(t)=B_k(0)e^{-\eta k^2t}$, so its timescale is $1/(\eta k^2)$. A structure of scale L therefore has diffusion time of order $L^2/\eta$, with geometry-dependent factors.

Advection and stretching add the $\nabla\times(v\times B)$ term. A long decay time preserves a seed field but does not explain its amplification; a dynamo requires suitable flow and an energy source. The magnetic Reynolds number compares advection and diffusion, yet a large value alone does not prove a sustained dynamo. The investigation controls the decay scale while deliberately omitting the flow-generation problem.

</LessonSection>
