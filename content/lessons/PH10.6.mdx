<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

One convention is $\dot\rho=-i[H,\rho]/\hbar+\sum_k(L_k\rho L_k^\dagger-\tfrac12\{L_k^\dagger L_k,\rho\})$. The jump operators include rates by convention; adding another rate factor without checking dimensions can double count.

Trace preservation follows by cyclicity, but an arbitrary trace-preserving matrix equation need not preserve positivity. Complete positivity also ensures a valid map when an untouched ancillary system is included. This is stricter than checking one chosen initial system state.

For L=√γ|0⟩⟨1|, the simple amplitude-damping excited population decays as p₁(t)=p₁(0)e⁻γt. A dephasing jump can suppress coherences without the same population decay. Environment temperature and upward transitions change the stationary state.

Deriving a Markovian generator from a bath commonly uses weak-coupling, time-scale and sometimes secular approximations. Structured environments and initial correlations can require memory/non-Markovian treatment. A fitted Lindblad equation is a controlled model, not proof that every environment has no memory.


### Derivation and transfer

A Markovian amplitude-damping model has excited population ρ11(t)=ρ11(0)exp(−γt) and coherence decaying at γ/2 when no additional dephasing is present. A Lindblad generator with appropriate nonnegative rates preserves trace and complete positivity.

The display isolates the excited population from an initially excited state. It does not establish the Markov approximation for every environment; memory, strong coupling or time-dependent modeling can require different equations. Population relaxation and pure dephasing need not have the same rate.

</LessonSection>
