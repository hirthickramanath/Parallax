<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Molecular identification combines orthogonal constraints. Formula, connectivity and three-dimensional environment influence different measurements, and one peak rarely determines an entire structure.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Each technique supplies part of a puzzle: mass constrains composition, vibrational bands constrain bonding, and magnetic resonance constrains local environments and connectivity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Organic identification uses a forward model from candidate structure to observable spectra, with instrumental response, mixture and environmental assumptions made explicit.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A molecular formula provides an atom inventory and a DBE constraint under the standard valence assumptions. Exact mass and isotope patterns can narrow candidate formulas, but isomers can have the same composition and mass. Fragmentation depends on ionization and rearrangement pathways; a fragment label is not automatically a bond broken in a unique direct step.

Infrared absorption reflects allowed vibrational transitions with changing dipole moment. Functional-group regions are useful guides, but overlap, coupling, hydrogen bonding and environment shift bands. A missing weak feature does not prove that a particular bond is absent.

NMR chemical shifts report local magnetic shielding relative to a reference. Integrals can relate proton populations under suitable acquisition and relaxation conditions, while coupling patterns encode interactions with neighbouring spins. The introductory n+1 rule has restricted applicability to suitable simple first-order couplings; chemically equivalent or strongly coupled systems need a fuller spin treatment.

For a simple synthetic first-order example, an ethyl group can show a three-proton triplet and two-proton quartet with a common coupling scale, but this pattern alone is not a unique complete molecular assignment. Two-dimensional correlations and complementary spectra can test connectivity hypotheses.

For an ideal absorbance calibration, $A=-\log_{10}(I/I_0)=\varepsilon c\ell$ in the Beer–Lambert regime. Scattering, saturation, concentration-dependent association and background errors can break the linear concentration model. Spectral lines in astronomical gas additionally require rotational or vibrational assignments, excitation, velocity and line-confusion checks. A terrestrial functional-group guide is not an independently verified interstellar detection.


### Derive, test and transfer

Beer–Lambert absorbance is A=εcl under its defined decadic convention, and transmittance is 10^−A. Absorbance adds for independent absorbers in an appropriate dilute linear regime; transmittances multiply instead of adding in the same way.

Organic identification combines IR, NMR, mass and electronic spectral constraints with a molecular formula and plausible structure. Concentration, path length, baseline and overlapping bands affect inference. The display illustrates a controlled absorbance relation, not a unique structure solver or a full synthetic spectrum.

</LessonSection>
