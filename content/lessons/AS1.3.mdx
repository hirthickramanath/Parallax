<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="The chase that starts with braking" layer="puzzle">

A spacecraft is ahead in the same circular orbit. Firing backward can place you on a lower transfer orbit that gains orbital phase. Does this mean “always brake to catch up”? Predict, then inspect the assumptions: phasing, collision avoidance and a later burn all matter.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="From observed patterns to dynamics" layer="story">

Kepler described regularities of planetary motion; Newtonian gravitation provides a dynamical account in an ideal two-body setting. We use the modern derivation rather than claiming to reproduce either scientist’s historical reasoning. Detailed historical references await verification.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Falling sideways" layer="intuition">

A circular orbit balances continually changing velocity direction with inward acceleration. A brief tangential burn changes energy and angular momentum. The new trajectory is not generally a new circle at the same radius.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="What the elements describe" layer="concept">

For an ideal two-body relative orbit let μ = G(M + m). Shape and size use eccentricity e and semimajor axis a; orientation uses inclination, longitude of ascending node and argument of periapsis; phase can be described by true anomaly or time of periapsis passage. Circular/equatorial cases make some angles undefined, so nonsingular alternatives are needed. The explorer is planar.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive the conic from a central force" layer="theory" fullOnly>

<Symbol name="μ" definition="Gravitational parameter G(M + m), with SI units m³/s²." /> and <Symbol name="h" definition="Specific angular momentum, with SI units m²/s; conserved for a central force." /> are used below.

Work in the orbital plane with specific angular momentum $h=r^2\dot\phi$. Central force gives zero torque, so h is constant. Assume h ≠ 0; radial collision orbits need separate treatment. Radial acceleration obeys

$$\ddot r-r\dot\phi^2=-\frac\mu{r^2}.$$

Set $u=1/r$. Since $\dot\phi=hu^2$, chain differentiation gives $\dot r=-hu'$ and $\ddot r=-h^2u^2u''$, where primes denote differentiation with respect to φ. Substitute into the radial equation and divide by $-h^2u^2$:

$$u''+u=\frac\mu{h^2}.$$

The constant particular solution plus the homogeneous sinusoid gives

$$u=\frac\mu{h^2}[1+e\cos(\phi-\omega)],\qquad r=\frac p{1+e\cos\nu},\quad p=\frac{h^2}\mu.$$

Here ν = φ − ω measures angle from periapsis and e ≥ 0 sets the shape. Specific energy is

$$\epsilon=\frac12(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)-\frac\mu r.$$

Use $\dot r=(\mu e/h)\sin\nu$ and $r\dot\phi=(\mu/h)(1+e\cos\nu)$. Squaring and adding, then subtracting μ/r, cancels the cosine terms:

$$\epsilon=\frac{\mu^2}{2h^2}(e^2-1),\qquad e^2=1+\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu^2}.$$

Thus ε < 0 gives an ellipse (e < 1), ε = 0 a parabola, and ε > 0 a hyperbola. For the circular special case e = 0. For nonparabolic orbits define $a=-\mu/(2\epsilon)$ and obtain the vis-viva equation

$$v^2=\mu\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).$$

For a bound ellipse, $h^2=\mu a(1-e^2)$ and its area is $\pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}$. Areal velocity is h/2, hence

$$T=\frac{2\pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}}h=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}\mu}.$$

This two-body result neglects drag, additional bodies, finite-size perturbations and relativistic corrections.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Two speeds at the same radius" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Set μ = 1 and r = 1 in normalized units. A circular launch needs v = 1, ε = −1/2, a = 1, e = 0. A tangential launch at √2 has ε = 0 and e = 1: the parabolic boundary. A faster launch has positive energy and is unbound. These are normalized examples, not spacecraft mission parameters.
