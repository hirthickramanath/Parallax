<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Collective charge response creates dispersion and cutoff; energy loss and point-charge self-force require separate, qualified models.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A cold unmagnetized collisionless electron plasma with a fixed neutralizing ion background has ε_r(ω)=1−ω_p²/ω², where ω_p²=n_e e²/(ε₀m_e).

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Linearize m_e dv/dt=−eE and J=−n_e e v for a harmonic e⁻ⁱωᵗ perturbation, neglecting pressure and collisions. Combining this current with Maxwell’s equations gives $\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2$ for transverse waves. Below ω_p the ideal bulk wavenumber is imaginary; above it the phase velocity exceeds c while group velocity is below c for this dispersion. This does not enable superluminal information.

Electron thermal motion, collisions, density gradients and a background magnetic field change the dielectric response. Longitudinal plasma oscillations and transverse waves are different branches. Landau damping is a kinetic resonant effect, not simply binary collisional friction; a cold-fluid formula cannot represent its full dependence on the velocity distribution.

Causal linear response links real and imaginary parts of frequency-dependent susceptibility through appropriately subtracted dispersion relations under their analyticity/decay assumptions. An arbitrary independently drawn refractive-index and absorption curve may violate causality or passivity. An index alone must specify its complex and frequency conventions.

For a classical point charge, radiation reaction introduces self-force subtleties. The Abraham–Lorentz equation contains a higher time derivative and admits unphysical runaway/preacceleration solutions outside naive interpretation. Order-reduced approximations such as Landau–Lifshitz are used only in an appropriate weak-reaction regime. Do not solve a truncated self-force equation and call every mathematical branch a physical particle trajectory. Strong fields, quantum emission and finite-size effects require further theory.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For ω_p=3 and c=2, a wave with ω=5 has k=√(25−9)/2=2. Its phase velocity is 5/2=2.5, larger than c=2, while group velocity c²k/ω=8/5=1.6. The comparison explicitly separates phase and packet speeds.

</LessonSection>
