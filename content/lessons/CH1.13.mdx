<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Photochemistry follows excited-state dynamics and competing pathways after absorption. Photon energy makes a pathway possible, while branching and quantum yield determine what the exposure produces.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Absorption starts a race among relaxation, emission, transfer, dissociation and reaction. Not every excitation reaches the chosen product channel.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A quantum yield is the number of a specified counted events divided by absorbed photons under a declared measurement convention. Absorbed and incident photons are different denominators.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Photon energy is $E=h\nu=hc/\lambda$. Absorbing a suitable photon populates an excited state or continuum according to the transition strength and spectrum. The subsequent path depends on the electronic states, environment and internal-energy redistribution, not just the photon energy compared with one bond-energy number.

For a simple excited population with independent first-order decay channels ki, total lifetime is $\tau=1/\sum_i k_i$ and channel branching fraction is ki/∑kj. This model assumes a single relevant population and constant rates; multistate transfer or nonexponential dynamics requires a fuller kinetic network.

A product quantum yield can be below one because competing decay removes excitations. It can exceed one when a photon initiates a chain generating multiple counted products, depending on the definition. That does not violate energy conservation: chemical energy and reactant consumption supply additional energy. A yield above one is not a universal claim of multiple fluorescence photons per one excitation in a passive simple two-level model.

Photochemical reaction rates integrate spectral photon flux, absorption cross section and channel yield over wavelength under the appropriate geometry. Attenuation and self-shielding alter the local field. A single monochromatic yield cannot always predict broadband irradiation, and a laboratory flux can drive chemistry outside a low-intensity astronomical regime.

Gas-phase reaction dynamics may include collision energy, orientation and radiative stabilization; ice or surface environments can accept excess energy differently. Track whether a reported abundance change is formation, destruction, desorption or a redistribution of existing species before attributing it to a unique reaction.


### Derive, test and transfer

A photochemical quantum yield counts specified molecular events per absorbed photon under a defined measurement. The denominator is absorbed photons, not every incident photon; incomplete absorption changes the inference. Chain chemistry can yield more than one molecular event per absorbed photon when stored chemical energy feeds subsequent steps.

Photon energy is hc/λ, while a rate depends additionally on flux, cross section, excited-state branching and competing relaxation. A quantum yield by itself is not a complete reaction mechanism or an energy-conversion efficiency. The display is an expected count budget, not a time-resolved excited-state simulation or a claim that every outcome count is deterministic.

</LessonSection>
