How can two molecules with the same connections fail to fit onto their mirror images? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Stereochemistry records three-dimensional information that a molecular formula or flat bond list omits. Conformation changes must be separated from configurational changes and from the chirality of the whole molecule.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A model can rotate in space without changing identity. A mirror model may or may not be superimposable after every such rotation; that is a whole-object question.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Constitutional isomers differ in connectivity. Stereoisomers share connectivity but differ spatially; conformers interconvert through allowed motions such as single-bond rotation in the applicable timescale.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A tetrahedral centre with four distinct substituents is a common stereogenic centre. Assigning R/S requires the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules and a declared viewing convention. R/S does not predict the sign of optical rotation. Likewise a molecule can be chiral without that simplest tetrahedral-centre pattern.

With n independent binary stereocentres, $2^n$ is an upper bound on configurations, not an automatic count of distinct stereoisomers. Internal symmetry can identify configurations or create an achiral meso form despite local stereocentres. Enantiomers are nonsuperimposable mirror images; diastereomers are stereoisomers not related that way.

An alkene supports an E/Z distinction when each double-bond carbon has two different substituents. Compare higher-priority groups under the same priority convention. Restricted π-bond rotation differs from easy single-bond conformational change; thermal or photochemical isomerization requires an appropriate pathway rather than a free swivel of a double bond.

Conformational preferences arise from torsional, steric and electronic free-energy differences. In an equilibrated two-conformer model, populations satisfy $N_2/N_1=\exp[-(G_2-G_1)/(RT)]$ with degeneracies included consistently in the free energies or state counts. A lower-energy drawing is not the only populated conformer at finite temperature.

A racemic mixture can have zero net optical rotation even though each molecule is chiral. Zero rotation can also arise from other circumstances and is not a complete structure proof. Spectroscopic time resolution, exchange rate and environment determine which conformers are experimentally distinguishable; a static molecular picture does not answer that question alone.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Build a tetrahedral model and its mirror image, testing superposition by rigid rotations. Compare a symmetric two-centre model with the naive 2ⁿ count. Draw conformers separately from configurations and state whether the modeled motion breaks a bond or changes a double-bond arrangement.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Two binary stereocentres give at most four configurations. A symmetric case such as 2,3-dibromobutane instead has an enantiomeric pair plus one meso form, for three stereoisomers. For a synthetic two-conformer free-energy difference RT ln2, the higher-free-energy conformer has half the population of the lower one.

</LessonSection>
