<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Singular-value decomposition handles rectangular and rank-deficient maps without assuming an eigenbasis. It gives a geometric view of inverse problems and a principled way to expose noise amplification.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A linear map sends a unit sphere to an ellipsoid in its image. Singular values are its principal stretch factors, and zero stretches identify lost directions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Every real m×n matrix admits A=UΣVᵀ with orthogonal U and V and nonnegative diagonal singular values in rectangular Σ. Complex matrices use unitary factors and conjugate transpose.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix $A^TA$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis satisfying $A^TAv_i=\sigma_i^2v_i$ with $\sigma_i^2\ge0$. For each positive singular value define $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$; these uᵢ are orthonormal. Extending both orthonormal lists gives the SVD, including null directions. This construction establishes existence, though numerically forming AᵀA can lose accuracy for small singular values.

Write b in left-singular coordinates. For nonzero σᵢ, solving the corresponding direction divides by σᵢ. Small σᵢ amplify errors; zero values mark directions that cannot be inverted. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse replaces positive singular values by reciprocals and zeros by zeros. It produces the minimum-norm least-squares solution, not necessarily an exact solution when b has a component outside the image.

For a full-rank square matrix, the 2-norm condition number is σmax/σmin. Rank decisions in finite precision need a scale-dependent threshold. Declaring a small singular value zero changes the effective model and should be reported, not hidden behind an unqualified “exact rank.”

Truncating the SVD to k largest singular values gives a best rank-k approximation in spectral and Frobenius norms. The spectral error is σk₊₁. This is the Eckart–Young approximation theorem, with nonuniqueness possible at repeated cutoff singular values. Low-rank approximation compresses a map; it does not prove the discarded components are scientifically irrelevant.

Regularized inversion can truncate unstable directions or shrink them smoothly. The chosen threshold or penalty encodes a bias–variance tradeoff and should be checked against noise, validation data and physical prior information. A mathematically small residual can coexist with an unreliable inferred parameter when singular directions are weak.


### Derive, test and transfer

Least squares minimizes the squared residual norm, yielding A transpose A x=A transpose b when differentiating the quadratic objective. If A has full column rank, this gives a unique minimizer; rank deficiency leaves nonuniqueness unless an additional minimum-norm convention is imposed.

An SVD A=UΣV transpose separates orthonormal input/output directions and singular scaling. Small singular values amplify noise in inversion, even when the determinant is not exactly zero. Forming normal equations squares the condition number, so QR or SVD can be numerically preferable. A regularizer changes the inference problem and should be reported, rather than presented as an exact inverse of the original map.

</LessonSection>
