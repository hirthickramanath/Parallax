<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why can choosing the wrong basis inside a degenerate eigenspace hide a first-order splitting? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Degenerate perturbation selects symmetry-adapted combinations; fine structure combines several physical corrections consistently.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Start with the concrete worked example below. Track which quantities belong to the system, which are imposed by its surroundings, and which conclusions require a limiting approximation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Within a degenerate unperturbed eigenspace, first-order shifts are eigenvalues of the perturbation projected into that subspace.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Let P project onto the degenerate eigenspace. Solve $PVP|a\rangle=\epsilon_a|a\rangle$ there before using gap denominators for outside states. Arbitrary basis diagonals alone are not invariant predictions of the splitting. Remaining degeneracies may require higher-order or symmetry analysis.

A two-state matrix with equal diagonal a and off-diagonal b has eigenvectors proportional to symmetric/antisymmetric combinations and shifts a±|b| for the Hermitian real-coupling example. The basis rotation changes states while the eigenvalues are invariant.

Hydrogen fine structure includes relativistic kinetic, spin-orbit and Darwin-type contributions in an appropriate expansion. Counting only one term while calling the result the complete fine structure is inconsistent. Spin, angular coupling, reduced mass and domain qualifications belong to the model.

External electric/magnetic fields and hyperfine interactions select further bases/scales. Compare correction sizes before combining incompatible perturbative hierarchies. A degeneracy protected by symmetry differs from an accidental equality broken by a chosen perturbation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Rotate the basis inside a two-dimensional degenerate subspace and verify the same eigenvalues. Compare a diagonal entry before/after rotation to see why it is not an invariant energy prediction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For the projected perturbation matrix [[2,1],[1,2]], first-order shifts are 1 and 3. Merely reading both diagonal entries as 2 would miss the splitting. The shift sum remains 4, the trace.

</LessonSection>
