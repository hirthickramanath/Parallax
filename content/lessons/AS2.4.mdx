<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Spectral shifts compare an observed feature with a modeled rest wavelength. Relative motion, gravity and cosmic expansion can each contribute, and the same z symbol does not make their interpretations identical.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A shifted line is a comparison of wavelength scales. Turning that ratio into a speed requires a physical model for emitter, observer and propagation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Redshift is z=(λobs−λrest)/λrest, so 1+z=λobs/λrest=νrest/νobs for corresponding photons.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For inertial source/observer radial recession in special relativity, $1+z=\sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}$ with β=v/c positive for recession. Solving gives $\beta=[(1+z)^2-1]/[(1+z)^2+1]$. At |β|≪1, z≈β. Large-redshift speeds must not be inferred from the small-velocity approximation without its assumptions.

Transverse motion and angle require the full photon four-momentum transformation, not the radial expression. In a static weak gravitational field, the leading redshift for light climbing from emitter potential Φe to observer Φo is $z\approx(\Phi_o-\Phi_e)/c^2$ when the difference is small and kinematic effects are separately controlled.

In a homogeneous expanding-universe model, $1+z=a(t_{\rm obs})/a(t_{\rm emit})$. Converting that to distance needs an expansion history, and interpreting it as one global special-relativistic velocity is not generally valid. Peculiar motion and local gravitational shifts can be superposed only with a consistent propagation model; exact shift factors multiply rather than all z values simply adding.

Line identification precedes numerical redshift. Several transitions with one common wavelength factor are more convincing than one ambiguous feature. Calibration drift, air/vacuum wavelength conventions, observer motion and blending can bias a claimed shift. A continuum maximum does not supply a unique rest wavelength without a model.

For small line-of-sight velocity dispersion, a width scale satisfies Δλ/λ≈Δv/c, but one must distinguish standard deviation, full width at half maximum and instrumental resolution. A cosmological redshift of the whole spectrum is not the same observable as internal line broadening. A reliable inference reports both the model and the statistical/systematic uncertainties.


### Derive, test and transfer

For radial recession in flat spacetime, combine time dilation and the increasing separation of successive wavefronts to obtain $1+z=\sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}$. Squaring and solving gives $\beta=[(1+z)^2-1]/[(1+z)^2+1]$. The small-speed limit z≈β is an approximation, not a high-redshift conversion rule.

In a homogeneous expanding cosmology the wavelength ratio is instead $1+z=a(t_{obs})/a(t_{emit})$. Peculiar motion, gravitational shifts and expansion can all contribute to observations. At large distance there is no unique special-relativistic relative velocity between arbitrarily separated galaxies without a transport convention. The investigation intentionally isolates the local special-relativistic relation; a spectrum alone needs line identification and instrumental calibration before a physical shift can be interpreted.



### Worked inference: Label a low-speed Doppler estimate as an approximation

1. A line with rest wavelength 500 nm is observed at 501 nm. Its redshift is z=(501−500)/500=0.002.

2. For a low-speed radial Doppler estimate, v≈cz. Taking c=300000 km/s gives recession speed about 600 km/s.

3. This first-order estimate is not exact relativistic inversion. Cosmological redshift and peculiar motion require their own interpretation; a large redshift is not automatically a recession speed cz.
