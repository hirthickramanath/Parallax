<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Functional groups organize organic reactivity through bonding and electronic structure. Carbonyl polarization and the presence of a potential leaving group distinguish addition from acyl substitution pathways.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

The carbon–oxygen π bond is polarized. A donor can attack carbon while the π pair shifts toward oxygen, but what happens next depends on which groups are attached and the medium.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Carbonyl addition changes the π bond through a tetrahedral intermediate or corresponding pathway. Acyl substitution also replaces a group at an acyl centre when an appropriate leaving-group pathway exists.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A simple carbonyl Lewis description includes a polar C=O bond, and a charge-separated contributor emphasizes electrophilic carbon and electron-rich oxygen. These are contributors to one electronic state, not separate molecules in equilibrium. Protonation, Lewis-acid coordination and substituents alter the activation barriers and must be specified.

For aldehydes and ketones, a common nucleophilic addition picture transfers a donor pair to carbon and shifts the C=O π pair to oxygen. Subsequent proton transfer depends on the reagent and medium. A carboxylic-acid derivative with a leaving-group pathway can instead form and collapse a tetrahedral intermediate, giving nucleophilic acyl substitution. Leaving-group ability and reversibility govern which versions are plausible.

For an alkene, electrophilic addition often consumes the π bond and creates two new σ-bond connections through a mechanism dependent on the reagents. Carbocation, concerted or radical descriptions predict different stereochemistry and rearrangement possibilities. A Markovnikov orientation rule is not a substitute for choosing the actual mechanism and can fail when the pathway changes.

Degree of unsaturation for conventional neutral closed-shell C/H/N/halogen formulas is $\mathrm{DBE}=(2C+2+N-H-X)/2$, with the usual valence assumptions. A carbonyl contributes one, a ring one and a triple bond two. Oxygen does not enter this count. The result constrains possible structures but does not locate functional groups.

Chemoselectivity distinguishes reactions at different functional groups; regioselectivity distinguishes positions and stereoselectivity spatial products. These categories describe observations, while a mechanism attempts to explain them. Interstellar analogues need environment-specific activation and energy-removal pathways rather than copying a bulk-solution scheme unchanged.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a simple reversible carbonyl addition C+Nu⇌P with nucleophile activity held by a reservoir, mass action gives P/C=K aNu. Conserving carbonyl-derived material gives product fraction K aNu/(1+K aNu). The fraction is an equilibrium result, not an elementary kinetic rate law.

Acid/base catalysis, proton transfers and hydration/solvent effects can change the relevant pathway and species definitions. Functional-group reactivity depends on electronics and conditions rather than one universal activity parameter. The investigation makes the reservoir and ideality assumptions explicit so equilibrium conversion is not confused with mechanism or speed.

</LessonSection>
