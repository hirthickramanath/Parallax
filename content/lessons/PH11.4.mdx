Why can successive boosts rotate a carried spatial frame? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Acceleration links a sequence of instantaneous inertial frames. Nonparallel Lorentz boosts do not compose into a pure boost, producing Thomas–Wigner rotation; photon directions and frequencies also transform together.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

An accelerated observer keeps changing velocity. Comparing local axes by different boost sequences reveals a rotation, even without treating it as an ordinary torque on a point particle.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Proper acceleration is the magnitude of the four-acceleration measured in the instantaneous rest frame. Relativistic optics uses the Lorentz transformation of a photon four-momentum.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For one-dimensional motion, rapidity η is defined by β=tanhη and γ=coshη. Collinear boosts add rapidities. Proper acceleration α satisfies $d\eta/d\tau=\alpha/c$. With constant α, initial rest and initial position zero, integration gives $ct=(c^2/\alpha)\sinh(\alpha\tau/c)$ and $x=(c^2/\alpha)[\cosh(\alpha\tau/c)-1]$. Lab coordinate acceleration is not constant; it decreases as speed approaches c.

Using laboratory time, a standard Thomas precession angular velocity of a torque-free carried frame relative to the laboratory axes is $\Omega_T=\gamma^2(\mathbf a\times\mathbf v)/[(\gamma+1)c^2]$ under the stated frame convention. In uniform circular motion this opposes the orbital angular velocity and has magnitude (γ−1)ωorb. Quoting the opposite cross-product order without changing the frame convention reverses the sign incorrectly.

For a photon making angle θ with +x in S, a boost with S′ moving +v along x gives $\nu'=\gamma\nu(1-\beta\cos\theta)$ and $\cos\theta'=(\cos\theta-\beta)/(1-\beta\cos\theta)$. Frequency shift and aberration are two parts of the same four-vector transformation. At θ=0, ν′/ν=√[(1−β)/(1+β)], appropriate to an observer moving along the light's propagation.

A transverse direction must be defined in a particular frame: θ=π/2 in S gives ν′=γν, while a different source/observer geometry can yield a reciprocal factor. The phrase transverse Doppler effect without a frame and angle convention can therefore be misleading.

Specific intensity transforms with the invariant Iν/ν³ along the same ray in flat-spacetime Lorentz transformations. Apparent beaming additionally changes solid angle and arrival-time mapping. Cosmological or gravitational redshift needs geometric propagation beyond a global inertial boost; constant proper acceleration in flat spacetime does not itself introduce spacetime curvature.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

At β=0.6, γ=1.25. For a photon traveling along +x, ν′/ν=1.25(1−0.6)=0.5. In uniform circular motion at that speed, the Thomas precession magnitude is 0.25 times the orbital angular frequency and its direction is opposite under the stated convention.

</LessonSection>
