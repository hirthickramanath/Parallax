<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why do frequency and wavelength plots put the peak of one blackbody at different locations? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Continuum shapes and spectral lines constrain temperature, composition and motions. A plotted density depends on its horizontal variable; its peak is not invariant under changing that variable.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A spectrum reports energy per interval. Equal frequency bins and equal wavelength bins cover different portions of the same radiation, so their heights need a Jacobian.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A blackbody is an ideal equilibrium absorber and emitter with Planck specific intensity Bν or Bλ. A line transition involves an energy difference and additional populations and transport physics.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Planck's frequency form is $B_\nu(T)=2h\nu^3/[c^2(\exp(h\nu/kT)-1)]$. Converting the density gives $B_\lambda(T)=2hc^2/[\lambda^5(\exp(hc/(\lambda kT))-1)]$. These are specific intensities per solid angle per spectral interval. Multiplying by π gives the outward spectral flux from an isotropic blackbody surface hemisphere; it is not the total source luminosity.

Integration yields surface flux F=σT⁴. A spherical blackbody of radius R has L=4πR²σT⁴. The wavelength-density peak obeys λmaxT≈2.898×10⁻³ m K. The frequency-density peak does not map to that same λ by c/νmax, because the density Jacobian shifts the maximum. Always identify which spectral quantity is plotted.

At hν≪kT, expanding the exponential gives the Rayleigh–Jeans limit $B_\nu\approx2\nu^2kT/c^2$. At hν≫kT, the Wien exponential tail dominates. Extending the low-frequency approximation over all frequencies produces a divergent energy integral; each approximation has a regime.

For a transition with energy gap ΔE, photon frequency is ΔE/h. Whether it appears in emission or absorption depends on level populations, illumination, opacity and source function, not only the presence of an element. Line strengths depend on oscillator strengths, excitation and ionization conditions. Missing lines do not by themselves prove an element is absent.

Thermal motion broadens lines through a line-of-sight velocity distribution; natural, pressure, rotational, turbulent and instrumental effects can contribute. A blackbody fit to a nonthermal source can be physically misleading even if a short wavelength range looks smooth. An inferred colour temperature need not equal every local gas or effective temperature.


### Derive, test and transfer

For equilibrium blackbody radiation, the wavelength spectral density is $B_\lambda=2hc^2/[\lambda^5(\exp(hc/(\lambda kT))-1)]$. At long wavelengths expand $e^x-1\simeq x$ to obtain the Rayleigh–Jeans form $B_\lambda\propto T/\lambda^4$. At short wavelengths the exponential suppresses the spectrum; extending the long-wavelength approximation there creates a false divergence.

Differentiating at fixed T gives the wavelength peak relation $\lambda_{max}T\simeq2898\,\mu\mathrm m\,K$. A density per frequency transforms with a Jacobian: $B_\nu d\nu=|B_\lambda d\lambda|$. Consequently the peak of Bν cannot be found by merely substituting c/λ at the Bλ peak. Real stellar spectra carry absorption lines and atmospheric continua; a fitted color temperature is model-dependent and is not always the effective temperature defined by integrated flux.



### Worked inference: Specify which spectral density has a peak

1. For the wavelength-density blackbody spectrum Bλ, adopt λmax T=2.90×10⁻³ m K. A temperature of 5800 K gives λmax=5.00×10⁻⁷ m=500 nm.

2. Halving temperature doubles this peak wavelength. At unchanged surface area, total blackbody luminosity also falls by a factor 16 because it scales as T⁴.

3. The peak of Bν is not obtained by simply replacing its frequency with c/λ at the Bλ maximum: spectral densities have different Jacobians. Real stellar spectra also contain absorption features and need not be perfect blackbodies.
