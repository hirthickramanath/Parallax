<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For two coherent equal contributions, $I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta$ in the elementary scalar model. Path difference and additional source/interface phases determine δ. Incoherent averaging suppresses the cross term; unequal intensities reduce fringe visibility even with perfect coherence.

A uniformly illuminated slit of width a gives an amplitude proportional to sinc(ka sinθ/2), where sinc u=sin u/u and its value at zero is one. Intensity is its squared modulus. The first minima obey a sinθ=±λ, when geometrically accessible. The small-angle width approximation λ/a has an admitted angular regime.

A circular aperture instead produces an Airy-type pattern involving J₁; the ideal first angular minimum is approximately 1.22λ/D for a small angle. A resolution criterion is a chosen detectability convention for a stated object/noise model, not a hard universal impossibility boundary.

Near-field Fresnel diffraction retains quadratic phase terms neglected in a far-field approximation. Polarization, vector fields, finite bandwidth and aperture structure can matter. A plotted intensity must be normalized if interpreted probabilistically, and pixel size plus detector response can limit an actual measurement.


### Derive, test and transfer

For two equal coherent narrow slits, phase difference δ gives an interference factor cos²(δ/2) relative to its central maximum. Finite slit width multiplies this by a single-slit diffraction envelope sinc²β, with β=πa sinθ/λ and δ/2=πd sinθ/λ. The widths and separation therefore control distinct structures.

At β=0 the sinc ratio is defined by its limit one, not a division error. An interference maximum can be suppressed when it lies on a diffraction-envelope zero. Partial coherence, unequal amplitudes and non-Fraunhofer geometry alter the ideal pattern. The display is an analytic far-field intensity relation, with its horizontal phase coordinate stated explicitly.

</LessonSection>
