<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Electrostatic potential reduces a curl-free vector field to a scalar, but source equations and boundary information are still needed. An arbitrary reference constant differs from missing physical boundary data.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Potential is an energy-per-charge landscape. Its slope determines the field, while charge determines its local curvature through Poisson’s equation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For an electrostatic field on a suitable domain, E=−∇V. In vacuum, Poisson’s equation is ∇²V=−ρ/ε₀ and its source-free version is Laplace’s equation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Because electrostatic circulation vanishes on admitted loops in a domain with a valid potential, $V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B E\cdot dl$. Adding a constant changes no field. Combining E=−∇V with Gauss’s differential law gives Poisson’s equation. It is not valid to ignore time-varying induction and still assume every electric field is globally a scalar gradient.

In a source-free region, harmonic V satisfies ∇²V=0. On suitable bounded connected domains its maximum and minimum occur on the boundary unless V is constant, under the usual regularity conditions. One proof uses the mean-value property of harmonic functions: an interior strict extreme cannot equal the average of neighbouring values unless they all match.

For two solutions with the same charge density and Dirichlet boundary potential, their difference u is harmonic and zero on the boundary. Green’s identity gives $\int|\nabla u|^2dV=\oint u\partial_nu\,dA-\int u\nabla^2u\,dV=0$. Hence u is constant, and its zero boundary value fixes that constant to zero. This uniqueness theorem explains why a solution satisfying all conditions is sufficient even if found by a clever construction.

Neumann data specify the outward normal derivative and determine a solution only up to a constant when the compatibility condition holds. Integrating Poisson requires $\oint\partial_nV\,dA=-Q/\epsilon_0$. Boundary values inconsistent with total charge cannot be repaired by an arbitrary additive constant.

For spherical symmetry, $\nabla^2V=r^{-2}d(r^2V')/dr$. Source-free solutions on a positive-r interval have V=A+B/r. Whether B is allowed at r=0 or A at infinity depends on the actual domain and conditions. A singular term excluded at the centre can still be correct outside a charged object.


### Derive, test and transfer

For electrostatics in a suitable domain, E=−grad V and work by the electric field on charge q is q(Vinitial−Vfinal). The additive potential constant changes neither field nor potential differences. Zero potential at a point therefore does not mean zero force there.

For a point charge, V=kq/r and differentiating gives the outward field for positive q. For multiple charges, scalar potentials add and the vector field is the negative gradient of their sum. Poisson's equation introduces the source density with a sign fixed by this convention. Boundary conditions matter for uniqueness; simply solving the homogeneous differential equation without matching boundaries does not solve the requested electrostatic problem.

</LessonSection>
