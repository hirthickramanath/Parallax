<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

Can two collisions conserve momentum but lose different amounts of kinetic energy? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Collisions and machines expose two distinct accounting systems. Momentum tracks directed motion through impulse; energy tracks transfers through work and other mechanisms. Separating them explains why sticking objects can conserve momentum while losing mechanical kinetic energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A force acting for longer can produce more impulse, while a force acting through a larger displacement can do more work. A perpendicular force can bend a trajectory without changing speed because its instantaneous power is zero.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

For constant mass p=mv and F_ext=dp/dt. Work is ∫F·dr, not simply force times distance for arbitrary directions. Conservative forces permit a potential energy; dissipative processes can move energy out of the resolved mechanical variables without destroying total energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Integrate $d\mathbf p/dt=\mathbf F_{ext}$ from t₁ to t₂:

$$\Delta\mathbf p=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F_{ext}\,dt.$$

For a collection of particles, internal forces that obey equal-and-opposite action along the interaction cancel in the total momentum balance. Zero net external impulse therefore preserves total momentum.

For constant mass, dot Newton’s law with velocity: $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v=m\dot{\mathbf v}\cdot\mathbf v=d(mv^2/2)/dt$. Integrating in time and using d𝐫=𝐯dt gives

$$W_{net}=\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=K_2-K_1.$$

A force is conservative on the considered domain when its work can be represented by −ΔU independently of path. Then W_cons = −ΔU. Separating nonconservative work yields Δ(K+U)=W_noncons. For inverse-square gravity F_r=−GMm/r², solving F_r=−dU/dr with U(∞)=0 gives U=−GMm/r.

For two bodies that stick in one dimension with negligible external impulse,

$$v_f=\frac{m_1v_1+m_2v_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$

Subtract the final kinetic energy from the initial value and simplify:

$$K_i-K_f=\frac12\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}(v_1-v_2)^2\ge0.$$

The missing resolved kinetic energy can appear as deformation, thermal energy and sound. Equality occurs for equal initial velocities, not for every momentum-conserving collision.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

A 2 kg body at 3 m/s sticks to a stationary 1 kg body. The final speed is 2 m/s from momentum balance. Initial K=9 J, final K=6 J, so 3 J leaves the resolved kinetic energy. An isolated total system still conserves energy.

</LessonSection>
