<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Gradients turn scalar models into local change predictions. Their geometric meaning assumes a metric: coordinates with incompatible scales or units cannot be compared by an unqualified Euclidean arrow.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Among unit steps in Euclidean space, the step aligned with the gradient gives the largest first-order increase. A Hessian describes how that local slope changes.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a differentiable real scalar field in orthonormal Cartesian coordinates, the gradient has its partial derivatives as components. The Hessian contains second partials when they exist.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a unit vector u, the directional derivative is $D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u$. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds its magnitude by |∇f|, with maximal increase along ∇f/|∇f| when the gradient is nonzero. At a critical point, every first-order directional change vanishes and higher order determines behaviour.

If f has continuous second partials near a, Taylor expansion along the line a+th gives $f(a+h)=f(a)+\nabla f(a)\cdot h+\tfrac12h^TH(a)h+o(|h|^2)$. Equality of mixed partials under these regularity assumptions makes the Hessian symmetric. Mere existence of both mixed partials at a point is not the general equality theorem.

For stronger smoothness, applying one-variable Taylor to the line function gives a higher-order remainder controlled by directional higher derivatives along the segment. A quadratic approximation is local; a large displacement can invalidate an apparently accurate curvature picture.

A differentiable level surface f=c has tangent displacements satisfying ∇f·h=0 at a regular point where ∇f≠0. Thus the gradient is normal to that surface. When ∇f=0 the implicit-surface conclusion needs further analysis: the zero set may cross, collapse or still be smooth for another defining function.

The identity “gradient equals partial-derivative components” uses an orthonormal Euclidean basis. With a general coordinate metric g, differential components ∂ᵢf are converted to gradient components by the inverse metric. Rescaling one coordinate changes a naive steepest-descent algorithm unless its notion of step size changes consistently. Physical optimization should state parameter scales and units rather than comparing one metre with one kelvin as equal coordinate lengths.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a differentiable scalar field, the directional derivative along unit u is $D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u$. Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|D_uf|\leq\|\nabla f\|$, with steepest ascent along the gradient when it is nonzero. This statement uses the specified Euclidean metric; coordinate components cannot be treated as physical lengths in an arbitrary chart.

For $f=x^2+2y^2$, gradient is (2x,4y) and Hessian diag(2,4). Its exact quadratic expansion has linear term gradient dot displacement and quadratic term one half displacement-Hessian-displacement. Omitting the factor one half doubles the curvature contribution. A positive-definite Hessian at a stationary point proves a strict local minimum under the smoothness hypotheses, not a global minimum for every nonquadratic function.

</LessonSection>
