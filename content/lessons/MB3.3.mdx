<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

If differentiation gives a rate, what exactly does integrating that rate recover? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Computing an area and recovering a trajectory once looked like different problems. The fundamental theorem of calculus connects them: local change and accumulated change are inverse operations under appropriate hypotheses. This connection turns many area problems into endpoint calculations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A thin strip contributes rate times a small interval. Summing strips adds signed contributions; refining a partition tests whether the sum approaches a stable value. Below an axis the contribution is negative, so a signed integral need not equal total geometric area.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

On a closed interval a continuous function is Riemann integrable. Define A(x) as the integral from a to x. The fundamental theorem states A′(x)=f(x) at interior points when f is continuous there. Indefinite integration asks for an antiderivative, with a free additive constant.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Let $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ with f continuous near x. For nonzero h,

$$\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}h=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt.$$

Subtract f(x). The absolute value of the difference is at most the largest $|f(t)-f(x)|$ between the endpoints. Continuity makes that bound tend to zero as h tends to zero, so $A'(x)=f(x)$.

If F is any antiderivative, then $(F-A)'=0$. The mean value theorem makes F−A constant on the interval. Hence

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).$$

For substitution, the chain rule gives $(F(g(x)))'=f(g(x))g'(x)$. Applying the endpoint formula yields

$$\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du,$$

under the usual differentiability/continuity assumptions. Bounds must change with the variable; one cannot replace dx by du without the derivative factor.

For integration by parts, start with $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$. Integrating and rearranging gives

$$\int_a^b u v'\,dx=[uv]_a^b-\int_a^b u'v\,dx.$$

As an example, take u=x and v′=eˣ. Then $\int xe^x dx=xe^x-e^x+C$. Differentiation checks the result exactly. Improper integrals require a further limit at singular or infinite endpoints; the theorem is not a licence to ignore divergence.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the assumptions" layer="interactive">

Approximate the integral of x² from 0 to 1 using n equal-width rectangles with right endpoints. For n = 1, 2, and 4 compare the sums with 1/3. Then derive the general sum using the formula for the sum of squares; do not infer equality from a finite table.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For ∫₀¹2x cos(x²) dx, set u = x² so du = 2x dx and bounds remain 0 to 1. The integral becomes ∫₀¹cos u du = sin 1. The angle is in radians. Differentiating sin(x²) returns the original integrand.

</LessonSection>
