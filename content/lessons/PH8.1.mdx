<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A wavefunction is a probability amplitude; in a position representation its squared modulus gives a probability density when normalized with the appropriate measure.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a one-particle nonrelativistic position state, $\int|\psi(x)|^2dx=1$ and a region probability is its density integral. The value |ψ(x)|² is a density, not the probability of a mathematical point in a continuous coordinate. A global phase changes no probabilities, while relative phases affect interference.

Photons carry energy hν and momentum h/λ in vacuum. Matter waves have de Broglie wavelength λ=h/p in the elementary momentum eigenstate description. These relations explain why energy transfer and spatial interference cannot be modeled by one naive classical particle trajectory. A normalizable localized packet contains a momentum range rather than one exact wavelength.

For two coherent alternatives, probability is |ψ₁+ψ₂|² and contains a cross term. If which-alternative information becomes encoded in distinguishable environmental states, the reduced interference term is suppressed by their overlap. A one-particle detection is localized, but repeated outcomes sample the predicted distribution; the amplitude need not be a literal mechanical fluid passing through a detector.

Fourier structure yields $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ for states with the required variances and domain conditions. This is not solely a claim of clumsy measurement or a universal energy-time operator formula. The elementary Schrödinger framework has nonrelativistic and fixed-particle-number limits; relativistic creation and annihilation require a broader theory. Interpretational proposals should be distinguished from the operational probabilities being tested.


### Derivation and transfer

Photon energy E=hf and momentum p=E/c connect wave frequency to discrete energy transfer. Matter waves instead use λ=h/p, so wavelength depends on momentum rather than on a universal particle speed. A classical trajectory and a probability amplitude describe different levels of a model.

The display uses h=1 to isolate the inverse momentum law. It is not a measured electron diffraction pattern; preparation, aperture geometry and detection statistics are required to predict an actual experiment.

</LessonSection>
