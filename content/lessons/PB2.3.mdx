<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Kinetic theory connects a statistical microscopic model with thermodynamic pressure and temperature. It also explains why astronomical line widths can reveal temperature without tracking individual particles.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Molecules move in every direction. Their mean vector velocity can be zero even while their mean squared speed is positive.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

For a dilute classical ideal gas in equilibrium, random translational motion produces pressure, and mean translational kinetic energy is 3kT/2 per particle.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A molecule with x velocity vₓ reverses that component in an elastic wall collision, delivering momentum $2mv_x$. Collision frequency with that wall is |vₓ|/(2L). Summing and dividing by wall area yields $p=Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/V$. Isotropy gives $\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3$, so $pV=Nm\langle v^2\rangle/3$.

Comparing with pV=NkT gives $\langle mv^2/2\rangle=3kT/2$ and $v_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{3kT/m}$. This is root-mean-square speed, not mean speed or most probable speed. For a Maxwell speed distribution those are $\sqrt{8kT/(\pi m)}$ and $\sqrt{2kT/m}$ respectively. Their differences come from weighting a distribution, not conflicting temperature definitions.

Equipartition assigns kT/2 to each independent quadratic energy term in the classical equilibrium limit. Monatomic gas has three translational terms. Rotational and vibrational modes can be frozen out quantum mechanically, so an unlimited classical count gives wrong heat capacities at low temperatures. Binding and ionization also alter energy accounting.

For hard-sphere particles of effective diameter d in a dilute gas, a common mean-free-path estimate is $\ell=1/(\sqrt2\,n\pi d^2)$, with number density n. Collision rate and transport require a distribution of relative velocities. A collisionless gas may not remain in local thermal equilibrium, so a single temperature or fluid pressure description requires justification.

Thermal Doppler broadening reflects the line-of-sight velocity distribution. Turbulence, instrumental response and natural broadening also contribute; assigning every observed width to thermal speed overestimates temperature when those contributions are ignored.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

In an isotropic gas, $\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3$. Momentum transfer to a wall gives $p=(N/V)m\langle v^2\rangle/3$; comparison with $p=(N/V)kT$ yields $v_{rms}=\sqrt{3kT/m}$. This is a root-mean-square speed, not the most probable speed or the mean of the speed distribution. Classical quadratic degrees of freedom each contribute $kT/2$ to the mean energy only in their classical regime. If level spacing is comparable to or exceeds $kT$, the classical heat-capacity count must be replaced with a quantum population calculation.


### Derivation and transfer

For a classical monatomic ideal gas, each translational quadratic degree contributes kT/2 to the mean energy. Thus mean kinetic energy is 3kT/2 and rms speed is sqrt(3kT/m). Rms speed differs from mean speed.

The explorer uses k=1 model units and a selected particle mass. Quantum degeneracy, internal molecular modes and interacting dense gases require more physics. A single representative speed is not the full Maxwell distribution.
