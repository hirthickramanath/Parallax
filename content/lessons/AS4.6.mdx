<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Compact remnants use degeneracy and strong gravity, with separate physics for white dwarfs and neutron stars.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

White dwarfs are supported largely by degenerate electrons; neutron-star structure requires dense-matter pressure and general-relativistic equilibrium, not a scaled white-dwarf model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For cold nonrelativistic electron degeneracy, the approximate white-dwarf mass–radius trend is $R\propto M^{-1/3}$ for fixed composition in the admitted polytropic regime. More mass can mean smaller radius. At relativistic electron momenta the EOS softens toward a 4/3 exponent, producing the idealized Chandrasekhar limiting-mass scale with composition dependence.

Actual white dwarfs have finite-temperature envelopes, composition stratification and possible magnetic/rotational effects. A cold ideal limit is not a universal exact observed maximum under all conditions. Cooling luminosity reflects thermal evolution rather than sustained ordinary core fusion.

Neutron stars require a high-density EOS and the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations in the nonrotating spherical relativistic model. Nuclear interactions, composition and causality/stability constrain the EOS; ideal neutron degeneracy alone is not a precision mass-radius theory. Rapid rotation and fields require extensions.

Pulsars are rotating magnetized compact stars whose beam geometry and spin evolution can produce pulses. Spin-down power scales with Iω|ω̇| when moment of inertia is treated constant, but magnetospheric torque and inclination models affect inferred field/age. A characteristic age P/(2Ṗ) relies on braking and initial-period assumptions, not an automatic true birth date.

</LessonSection>
