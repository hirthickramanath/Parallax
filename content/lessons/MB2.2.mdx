<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Conics connect geometric constraints to equations and later to inverse-square orbits. An orbital ellipse is not explained by drawing one: its force law must still produce that curve.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

A conic is a locus, a set of points meeting one geometric rule. The equation encodes the rule; axes and origins determine its simplest form.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

An ellipse has constant sum of distances to two foci; a hyperbola constant absolute difference; a parabola equal distance to one focus and a directrix.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For an ellipse centred at the origin with major axis on x, write $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1$, a≥b>0. Its foci lie at ±c with $c^2=a^2-b^2$; eccentricity e=c/a lies in [0,1). The sum of focal distances is 2a. Equal axes give a circle; the foci then coincide.

For a parabola with focus (p,0) and directrix x=−p, equate distances: $\sqrt{(x-p)^2+y^2}=|x+p|$. Squaring cancels x² and p² to yield $y^2=4px$. This derivation keeps the geometric meaning of p; it is not the coefficient of x itself. The vertex is halfway between focus and directrix.

For a hyperbola $x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$, the foci satisfy $c^2=a^2+b^2$ and e>1. At large |x| the constant is negligible and asymptotes are y=±(b/a)x. The branches never reach the asymptotes at a finite point.

Translations replace x and y with x−h and y−k. A rotation can remove a mixed xy term from a real quadratic when the axes are suitably chosen. The discriminant B²−4AC of $Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0$ suggests the conic type, but degenerate and empty loci still require checking.

For a focus at the polar origin a suitable orientation gives $r=\ell/(1+e\cos\theta)$ over its physical domain. This describes conic geometry. In gravity, a separate angular-momentum and force-law derivation determines ℓ and e from energy and initial conditions.

### Extended derivation and transfer checks

Place ellipse foci at $(\pm c,0)$ and impose $r_++r_-=2a$, where $0\leq c<a$. Isolate one radical, square, isolate the remaining radical and square again; simplifying gives $x^2/a^2+y^2/(a^2-c^2)=1$. More explicitly, let $d_R=\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}$ and $d_L=\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2}$. Since $d_L^2-d_R^2=4cx$ and $d_L+d_R=2a$, we obtain $d_L-d_R=2cx/a$ and $d_L=a+cx/a$. Squaring the last identity and cancelling $2cx$ yields $(1-c^2/a^2)x^2+y^2=a^2-c^2$, which gives the stated ellipse equation. Because squaring can introduce extraneous branches, substitute candidate points back into the positive-distance sum. Define $b^2=a^2-c^2$ and $e=c/a$. The circle is the regular $e=0$ limit.

For a hyperbola use $|r_+-r_-|=2a$ with $c>a$. The same elimination yields $x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$ with $b^2=c^2-a^2$. Its two branches and asymptotes $y=\pm(b/a)x$ distinguish it from an ellipse. For a parabola with focus $(f,0)$ and directrix $x=-f$, equality of distances gives $(x-f)^2+y^2=(x+f)^2$, hence $y^2=4fx$.

With the focus as origin and periapsis on the positive polar axis, the focus/directrix definition gives $r=\ell/(1+e\cos\nu)$. Here $\ell$ denotes the semilatus rectum, not the earlier parabola focus distance $p$. For an ellipse $\ell=a(1-e^2)$ and $r_p=a(1-e)$, $r_a=a(1+e)$. Do not put a center-based Cartesian coordinate into this focus-based radius without translating the origin. At $e=1$ the denominator vanishes only in the asymptotic direction; at $e>1$ only angles with positive radius belong to this branch.

Kepler motion in an attractive inverse-square field has this conic form with $\ell=h^2/\mu$. Geometry alone does not establish an orbit law or time dependence. The true anomaly slider samples position, not equal time intervals: equal areas, not equal angles, correspond to equal times. Bound orbits have $e<1$ in the point-mass Newtonian model; perturbations and relativistic precession need a more complete model.
