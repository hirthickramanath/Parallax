<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Wave equations in radial geometry lead to differential equations with singular coefficients. Series solutions reveal which singularities can be handled systematically and where local expansions are valid.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

An ordinary Taylor series assumes a regular leading behaviour. Factoring xʳ allows a controlled noninteger or negative leading power near a regular singular point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For y″+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0 near x=0, an ordinary point has analytic P and Q. A regular singular point has xP and x²Q analytic there.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

At an ordinary point seek $y=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nx^n$, differentiate termwise inside its convergence radius and match coefficients. A second-order homogeneous equation ordinarily leaves two independent constants. The radius is limited by analytic structure of the coefficients, and a formal recurrence is not by itself a proof of convergence for every equation.

At a regular singular point, Frobenius seeks $y=x^r\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nx^n$ with a₀≠0 on a chosen side or complex branch. If p₀=(xP)(0) and q₀=(x²Q)(0), the lowest power produces the indicial equation $r(r-1)+p_0r+q_0=0$. This equation determines candidate leading exponents, not all higher coefficients.

For distinct indicial roots whose difference is not an integer, a standard Frobenius theorem supplies two such local solutions under the analytic hypotheses. Repeated roots or integer-separated roots can produce a second solution containing a logarithm, though logarithms are not forced in every integer-separated case. A reduction-of-order formula can construct the missing independent solution where a known solution is nonzero.

For the Euler equation $x^2y''+axy'+by=0$, trying y=xʳ gives r(r−1)+ar+b=0 exactly. Distinct roots produce power solutions on x>0; a repeated root r gives xʳ and xʳln x. Complex roots yield real combinations involving cos(β ln x) and sin(β ln x) times a real power. Domain and branch must be stated before extending these forms across zero.

For an irregular singular point the Frobenius ansatz may fail to span the solutions; asymptotic or other methods can be needed. A physical boundary condition may remove a mathematically valid singular solution, but regularity requirements must come from the problem rather than from an aesthetic preference for finite-looking curves.


### Derive, test and transfer

For an Euler-type equation x²y″+a xy′+b y=0 away from zero, insert y=x^r on a positive-x interval. The indicial polynomial is r(r−1)+ar+b. Distinct roots give power solutions under the ordinary Euler assumptions; a repeated root introduces a logarithmic second solution.

For a general regular singular point, a Frobenius series x^rΣa_nx^n produces both an indicial equation and coefficient recurrence. Integer root differences can create subtleties, so two indicial roots alone do not guarantee two simple power-series branches. A solution valid on x>0 need not extend smoothly through the singular point. Keep interval and branch conventions explicit.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For x²y″−2y=0 on x>0, the indicial equation is r(r−1)−2=(r−2)(r+1)=0. Thus y=C₁x²+C₂x⁻¹. At a boundary including zero, boundedness would force C₂=0, but on an interval away from zero both solutions are valid.
