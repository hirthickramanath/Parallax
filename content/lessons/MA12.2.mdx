<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A Banach space is complete in its norm. A Hilbert space is complete in a norm induced by an inner product, with a stated real/complex convention.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A norm obeys homogeneity and the triangle inequality, while an inner product additionally defines angles/orthogonality. With conjugation convention chosen consistently, $\|x\|^2=\langle x,x\rangle$. Not every norm comes from an inner product; the parallelogram identity characterizes this property in the standard setting.

In L², functions differing on a set of measure zero represent the same element. Point evaluation is therefore not a generic well-defined bounded functional on bare L². Weighted spaces require an explicit measure, and changing weight changes orthogonality and completeness claims.

For a closed linear subspace of a Hilbert space, the nearest-point projection exists uniquely and the error is orthogonal to the subspace. Closedness is essential; a dense proper subspace may approximate a vector arbitrarily well without containing a minimizer. Bessel’s inequality becomes Parseval equality for a complete orthonormal basis.

The Riesz representation theorem identifies continuous linear functionals with an inner-product pairing in a Hilbert space under its convention. It does not say every algebraic functional or every distribution is an ordinary vector of the same L² space. Boundary traces and generalized eigenvectors need their own function-space framework.


### Derivation and transfer

A Banach space is complete in its norm; a Hilbert space additionally has an inner product inducing the norm. Cauchy sequences must converge inside the space, so an incomplete subspace cannot be repaired merely by naming a norm.

In an inner-product space, orthogonal projection decomposes a vector into parallel and perpendicular parts and obeys the Pythagorean identity. The display uses a two-dimensional Euclidean example. Infinite-dimensional projection requires a closed subspace for the standard closest-point theorem.

</LessonSection>
