<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Force is not speed" layer="intuition">

A larger mass experiences a larger gravitational force but also has greater inertia. A balanced force changes neither component of velocity. A nonzero net force changes velocity, including its direction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="The precise statement" layer="concept">

For constant mass in an inertial frame, the net external force equals mass times acceleration. Misconception: a moving body needs a forward force simply to keep moving. In an ideal force-free setting, constant velocity persists.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="From Newton’s law to a trajectory" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Take downward as positive. Distinguish gravitational mass $m_g$ from inertial mass $m_i$:

$$m_i\ddot y=m_g g.$$

Observed equivalence motivates the model assumption $m_i=m_g=m>0$. Divide by $m$:

$$\ddot y=g.$$

Integrate once with initial velocity $v_0$: $\dot y=v_0+gt$. Integrate again with initial position $y_0$:

$$y=y_0+v_0t+\frac12gt^2.$$

For a release from rest at $y_0=0$, ground contact at height $H$ occurs at $t=\sqrt{2H/g}$. This positive root is mass-independent. Dimensional check: $H/g$ has units s². Constant $g$ and negligible drag are hypotheses, not universal truths.

With linear drag, $m\dot v=mg-bv$. Let $\tau=m/b$ for $b>0$. Multiplication by $e^{t/\tau}$ gives $(e^{t/\tau}v)'=ge^{t/\tau}$. Integrating from rest yields

$$v=g\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau}),\qquad y=g\tau[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})].$$

Mass now appears through $\tau$. Expanding the exponential as $b\to0$ recovers $y=gt^2/2$.

### A trajectory requires force and initial conditions

For constant acceleration, integrate $dv/dt=a$ with $v(0)=v_0$ to get $v=v_0+at$. Integrating again gives $x=x_0+v_0t+at^2/2$. Acceleration has units of length per time squared; every term in the position equation has length units. At a turning point, velocity can vanish while acceleration remains nonzero. Constant-g free fall assumes a local region and negligible drag; it is not a complete orbit model.

With downward-positive linear drag, $m\dot v=mg-bv$. Starting from rest gives $v=(mg/b)(1-e^{-bt/m})$ for positive $b$. The time scale is $m/b$ and the terminal speed is $mg/b$. Both depend on mass for fixed $b$. The limit $b\to0$ recovers $v=gt$ by expanding the exponential. A shared drag coefficient is a modelling choice, not a universal description of differently shaped objects.

</LessonSection>
