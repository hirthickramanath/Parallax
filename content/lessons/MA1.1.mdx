<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Rigorous analysis starts by distinguishing the logical structure of claims. A limit proof and a scientific model comparison can look similar in prose yet differ because the order of “for every” and “there exists” changes what may depend on what.

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A proposition has a truth value. Quantifiers specify a domain and either all its elements or the existence of at least one. A function is a relation assigning exactly one output to every admitted input.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

An implication $P\Rightarrow Q$ is false precisely when P is true and Q false. Its contrapositive $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$ is logically equivalent; its converse is not. To establish a biconditional, prove both directions. Negating a quantified statement swaps quantifiers and negates the inner claim: $\neg(\forall x\in D\;P(x))$ means $\exists x\in D\;\neg P(x)$.

The statements $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\exists y\in\mathbb R\;(y>x)$ and $\exists y\in\mathbb R\;\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(y>x)$ differ. The first is true using y=x+1; the second is false by choosing x=y+1. Declare dependencies rather than treating quantifiers as movable decorations.

For sets A and B, A is contained in B if every element of A lies in B. Equality follows from mutual containment. De Morgan's law $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$ follows by testing one arbitrary element: it lies in neither A nor B exactly when it lies in both complements. Complements require a specified ambient set.

For a function f:D→E, an inverse on f(D) exists if f is injective. A preimage $f^{-1}(S)=\{x\in D:f(x)\in S\}$ is defined for any subset S of E, even if f has no inverse function. Preimages preserve unions, intersections and complements relative to the admitted domains. Images need not preserve intersections: for f(x)=x², images of the disjoint singleton sets containing −1 and 1 both contain 1.

A direct proof begins with an arbitrary element satisfying the hypotheses. A contradiction proof adds the negation of the desired conclusion and derives an impossibility. An existence proof must construct an admissible example or supply a valid theorem guaranteeing one. In analysis, hypotheses such as nonemptiness, boundedness and positive denominators often do real work; omit none silently.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

The implication $P\Rightarrow Q$ fails only when $P$ is true and $Q$ false. Its contrapositive $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$ excludes exactly the same assignment; its converse does not. A biconditional requires both implications. Equality of sets likewise requires showing both inclusions, rather than only counting examples.

Quantifier order controls the allowed dependence of witnesses. For every real $x$ there is a larger real $y=x+1$, but there is no single real $y$ larger than all $x$. Given a proposed universal $y$, the choice $x=y+1$ contradicts it. Negating a quantified claim reverses the quantifier and negates the predicate: the negation of $\forall x\exists y\,R(x,y)$ is $\exists x\forall y\,\neg R(x,y)$.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

To prove the preimage of A∩B equals the intersection of their preimages, take arbitrary x in the function domain. The statement f(x)∈A∩B is equivalent to both f(x)∈A and f(x)∈B. These are exactly the two preimage membership conditions. This establishes both containments at once without assuming f is one-to-one.

</LessonSection>
