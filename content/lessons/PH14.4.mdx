<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Band theory solves a periodic electronic problem with states labelled by crystal momentum and band index, often in an independent-quasiparticle approximation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Near a Bragg-plane crossing, a periodic potential couples plane-wave components differing by a reciprocal vector. A two-state model with equal unperturbed energy and coupling V_G gives a gap $\Delta=2|V_G|$. This is a local model, not every gap’s universal microscopic derivation.

Bloch states have periodic cell factors. Crystal wavevectors differing by a reciprocal lattice vector represent related translation phases, motivating a Brillouin-zone description. Physical observables need band character and occupations, not merely a folded curve.

A filled band and a gap can be insulating in the elementary model, while partial filling can allow metal-like response. Mott physics, disorder localization and symmetry/topology can alter that inference. Effective quasiparticle energies also require interpretation when comparing with actual addition/removal spectra.

Tight-binding and nearly-free approximations suit different limits. Basis convergence, exchange/correlation treatment and geometry affect calculated bands. A density-functional Kohn–Sham gap need not equal an exact optical or quasiparticle gap; excitons and measurement processes add physics.


### Derivation and transfer

A one-dimensional nearest-neighbor tight-binding band E=−2tcos(ka) has group velocity (1/ℏ)dE/dk=2ta sin(ka)/ℏ. A band edge has zero group velocity even though the state can have nonzero crystal momentum.

The display isolates this derivative in a=ℏ=1 units. Metallic conduction also needs filling, scattering and interactions. A plotted dispersive band does not by itself establish the transport of a real material.

</LessonSection>
