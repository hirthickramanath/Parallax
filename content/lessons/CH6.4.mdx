<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Substitution and elimination are competing mechanistic families rather than labels fixed solely by the molecular formula. Substrate geometry, reagent, medium and temperature affect the pathway and products.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A reacting pair can either replace a leaving group or remove a neighbouring proton while forming a π bond. The approach geometry and available barriers decide which route is accessible.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

SN2 is a concerted bimolecular substitution model; SN1 involves rate-limiting ionization in its idealized regime. E2 is concerted elimination, while E1 proceeds through an ionized intermediate under its model assumptions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For an ideal elementary SN2 step, rate is $k[\mathrm{substrate}][\mathrm{nucleophile}]$. Backside approach to the reacting tetrahedral centre gives inversion of local geometry. Calling that automatically an R-to-S switch is unsafe because substituent priorities can change. Steric crowding raises the approach barrier, and nucleophile/solvent interactions influence effective reactivity.

For the ideal SN1 ionization-limited model, rate is $k[\mathrm{substrate}]$. A carbocation-like intermediate can permit rearrangement and attack from more than one side. Perfect 50:50 racemization is not guaranteed because ion pairs and solvent cages can retain directional memory. A stable-looking carbocation drawing is a hypothesis that needs evidence.

E2 removes a β proton while the leaving group departs and a double bond forms. An antiperiplanar arrangement is a common favourable orbital geometry; cyclic conformations can restrict which β bonds achieve it. Product distribution depends on structure and conditions, so one slogan about the most substituted alkene is not a universal law.

E1 and SN1 can share ionization intermediates, while E2 and SN2 often compete for bimolecular encounters. Strong basicity does not equal high nucleophilicity in every solvent: steric demand, solvation and polarizability affect them differently. Kinetic isotope effects and stereochemical outcomes can help distinguish hypotheses, but each inference has model limits.

For a first-order ideal rate r=k[S], doubling nucleophile concentration does not change r when that mechanism remains rate controlling. For a bimolecular r=k[S][Nu], doubling each concentration multiplies r by four. Observing such orders supports a kinetic model under the measured regime, not an unconditional mechanism proof for every related substrate.


### Derive, test and transfer

A simple SN2 rate law is k[substrate][nucleophile], while an ionization-limited SN1 regime can have a rate proportional only to substrate. These are regime-dependent effective laws, not labels to attach solely from a product drawing. Solvent, leaving group and competition with elimination matter.

Backside SN2 attack inverts the local tetrahedral geometry, but an R label need not always become S if substituent priorities also change. The rate comparison keeps substrate and the chosen coefficient fixed and uses nonzero nucleophile concentrations; it does not model every competing channel or ion-pair lifetime.

</LessonSection>
