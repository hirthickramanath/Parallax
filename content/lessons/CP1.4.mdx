<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

An ODE solver advances a dynamical model in finite steps. Long-time mechanics demands attention to structure and invariants, not just an attractive animation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Local slopes approximate a curve. A method can reduce each local error yet accumulate a systematic change in energy or phase over many cycles.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

An initial-value ODE problem specifies dy/dt=f(t,y) and y(t₀). A numerical integrator approximates its trajectory under stability and regularity constraints.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Forward Euler uses $y_{n+1}=y_n+hf(t_n,y_n)$ with first-order global error in a suitable smooth nonstiff problem. Classical RK4 evaluates four weighted slopes and has fourth-order global error under appropriate conditions. Local error order differs by one power because errors accumulate over many steps.

For the test problem $y'=\lambda y$, Euler multiplies each step by $1+h\lambda$. A decaying true solution with negative λ can numerically grow if |1+hλ|>1. Accuracy and stability are distinct. Stiff problems can demand an implicit method even when the visible solution changes slowly.

For a conservative mechanical system with acceleration a(q), velocity Verlet is $q_{n+1}=q_n+hv_n+h^2a(q_n)/2$, followed by $v_{n+1}=v_n+h[a(q_n)+a(q_{n+1})]/2$. With suitable separable Hamiltonian assumptions and fixed step it is a second-order symplectic method. Symplectic does not mean exactly conserving the original energy at every step; it often bounds long-time energy error better than a nonstructural method.

```python
# One velocity-Verlet step for q'' = -q, unit mass
h = 0.01
q, v = 1.0, 0.0
q_new = q + h*v - 0.5*h*h*q
v_new = v - 0.5*h*(q + q_new)
```

Validate against an analytic oscillator, halve the time step and compare phase, amplitude and energy error over many periods. Near-collision gravity, discontinuous forces and event detection require additional treatment. An orbit that conserves energy approximately may still have unacceptable phase error. Adaptive step control needs a declared tolerance, scale and solver strategy; arbitrary variable steps can break a simple symplectic construction.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

For $y'=\lambda y$, explicit Euler has amplification $1+h\lambda$; decay is numerically stable only when $|1+h\lambda|<1$. With real negative $\lambda$, this requires $0<h<2/|\lambda|$. A stable multiplier can still produce an inaccurate trajectory, so compare refined steps or an exact test problem. Symplectic methods preserve a phase-space geometric structure for Hamiltonian problems and can exhibit bounded oscillatory energy error under suitable assumptions. They do not guarantee exact conservation of the original Hamiltonian or universal stability at arbitrary step size.


### Derivation and transfer

For y′=−λy, explicit Euler advances by factor 1−λh. Absolute stability requires |1−λh|≤1 on this real negative test axis, while RK4 has factor 1−z+z²/2−z³/6+z⁴/24 with z=λh. Accuracy and stability are different requirements.

The display evaluates RK4's one-step amplification polynomial, not an adaptive solver or long-time symplectic orbit. A symplectic method is designed around Hamiltonian structure; it is not interchangeable with a high local order for arbitrary dissipative equations. Tolerances require a convergence check.
