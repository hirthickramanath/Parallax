<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Chemical formulas tell us composition but not geometry or electron distribution. Electron-pair structures organized many patterns before more detailed quantum models became available. Their strength is economical reasoning; their limitations become clear in electron-deficient, odd-electron and delocalized systems.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

First conserve the available valence-electron count. Then ask which arrangement plausibly distributes bonding and lone-pair domains. A drawing that minimizes formal charges can be useful without implying electrons are frozen into little lines.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

A covalent bond represents an energetically favorable interaction involving electron density and nuclei. A Lewis model assigns pairs to bonds/lone pairs. VSEPR estimates shape from electron domains; a multiple bond counts as one directional domain in this model. The octet rule is a limited pattern, not an inviolable law.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a Lewis structure, count all valence electrons, adding one per negative charge and subtracting one per positive charge. Each single bond uses two electrons. Assign remaining electrons as lone pairs while checking whether the intended species is representable by this model.

The formal charge on an atom is

$$FC=V-N-\frac B2,$$

where V is its neutral valence-electron count, N its assigned nonbonding electrons, and B the bonding electrons shared with neighbors. Summing formal charges over all atoms gives the net molecular/ionic charge because each bonding pair is split once between its endpoints.

For water, oxygen contributes 6 and two hydrogens contribute 2 electrons: 8 total. Two O−H bonds use 4; the remaining 4 give two lone pairs on oxygen. Oxygen’s formal charge is 6−4−4/2=0. Four electron domains suggest a tetrahedral electron-domain arrangement, but the molecular shape is bent because only two domains end in nuclei.

For CO₂, 16 valence electrons admit O=C=O with zero formal charges. The central carbon has two directional domains, giving a linear arrangement in the simple model. Geometry follows domains, not counting each bond line as an independent direction.

Equivalent resonance drawings do not imply a molecule switches between discrete structures. They indicate that no single localized Lewis drawing represents the electronic state well. Formal charge differs from partial charge, which depends on how the actual density is partitioned. A quantum molecular-orbital description is needed to treat delocalization, spin and quantitative energies beyond this bookkeeping model.


### Derivation and transfer checkpoint

A Lewis formal charge is $V-N-B/2$, where $V$ is the free atom's valence count, $N$ its nonbonding electrons and $B$ the electrons in bonds attached to it. This bookkeeping is not a direct measurement of partial charge. In VSEPR, a multiple bond counts as one directional electron domain; molecular geometry describes atom positions, while electron-domain geometry also includes lone pairs.

Four equivalent ideal directions can be represented by $(1,1,1)$, $(1,-1,-1)$, $(-1,1,-1)$ and $(-1,-1,1)$ divided by $\sqrt3$. Any pair has dot product $-1/3$, giving the ideal tetrahedral angle. Treating one or two domains as lone pairs changes the visible atom geometry, but the ideal-vector construction does not compute actual angle relaxation. Real structures require chemical evidence or a more detailed energy model.

</LessonSection>
