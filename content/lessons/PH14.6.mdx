<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Magnetization is magnetic moment per volume in a declared macroscopic convention; susceptibility is a response derivative with specified field/units.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A simple weak-field independent-moment high-temperature model gives a Curie susceptibility proportional to 1/T. Exchange interactions and anisotropy change collective ordering and response. A classical dipole-alignment picture alone does not explain every electronic magnetic ground state.

A mean-field ferromagnet can break rotational or discrete symmetry and develop spontaneous order, with domains reducing some macroscopic stray-field costs. Zero net sample magnetization need not mean absence of local order: domains or antiferromagnetic sublattices can cancel.

For an Ising pair, $H=-Js_1s_2$ favours aligned spins when J>0 and opposite spins when J&lt;0 under this convention. Quantum Heisenberg models and itinerant-electron magnetism have different state spaces and dynamics. Spin waves describe small deviations in suitable ordered phases.

Hysteresis depends on metastability, defects and dynamics; an equilibrium susceptibility curve does not predict every loop. Dimensionality, interaction range and anisotropy limit ordering theorems. Experimental moments can include orbital as well as spin contributions.


### Derivation and transfer

Curie's ideal paramagnetic law is χ=C/T in a suitable high-temperature weak-field regime. Interactions can change it to a Curie–Weiss form and generate ordering; a susceptibility divergence in a simple fit is not a complete microscopic phase transition calculation.

The display keeps positive T and a prescribed Curie constant. Saturation, anisotropy and hysteresis are omitted. Magnetization should not be assumed linear at arbitrarily strong fields.

</LessonSection>
