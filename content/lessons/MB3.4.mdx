<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

When does the biggest derivative fail to identify the biggest value? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Optimizing a design and accumulating a measured quantity both demand a model before any calculus. A familiar formula can still give the wrong answer if it optimizes the wrong variable, omits an endpoint, or integrates a density with the wrong units.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A zero derivative means a locally flat tangent, not necessarily a turning point. A hilltop, a valley, and a flat inflection can all have zero slope. To find a global best value on a bounded interval, compare all viable candidates.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

For an interior differentiable local extremum, the derivative is zero (Fermat’s theorem). The converse is false. Nondifferentiable interior points can also be extrema. The extreme value theorem requires continuity and a compact domain for guaranteed attainment.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Suppose f has a local maximum at interior c and is differentiable there. For small positive h, $[f(c+h)-f(c)]/h\le0$. For small negative h the numerator is still nonpositive but the denominator is negative, so the quotient is nonnegative. If the two-sided derivative exists, it must be both nonpositive and nonnegative. Thus $f'(c)=0$. Minima follow by applying the same reasoning to −f.

The converse fails for $f(x)=x^3$: f′(0)=0 but the function increases through zero. The second derivative test follows from a local Taylor approximation: if f′(c)=0 and f″(c) is positive, a sufficiently small nonzero displacement raises f, yielding a local minimum; f″(c) is negative yields a maximum. If f″(c)=0, no conclusion follows from that test alone.

On a closed interval, evaluate f at interior critical/nondifferentiable points and at both endpoints. For $f(x)=x^3-3x$ on [−2,2], f′=3x²−3, so interior candidates are ±1. Values at −2,−1,1,2 are −2,2,−2,2; the global extrema are attained more than once.

For a related-rate problem, write the geometric constraint before differentiating. A sphere has $V=4\pi r^3/3$, so $\dot V=4\pi r^2\dot r$. This requires a sphere changing through spherical shapes.

For accumulation, a nonuniform rod with linear density λ(x) has $M=\int_a^b\lambda(x)dx$. Its moment about the origin is $\int_a^b x\lambda(x)dx$, giving $x_{cm}=M^{-1}\int_a^b x\lambda(x)dx$ for M>0. Units distinguish mass from first moment. These formulas come from taking limits of sums of small segment masses.


### Derivation and transfer checkpoint

For a differentiable function on a closed bounded interval, an interior extremum must be stationary, but a global search must also include endpoints and any nondifferentiable points. For $(x-2)^2+1$ on $[0,3]$, the stationary value is the minimum and the larger endpoint value is the maximum. A stationary inflection such as $x^3$ demonstrates why a zero first derivative alone is insufficient.

A center of mass is a ratio of a first moment to a total mass: $x_{cm}=\int x\lambda(x)dx/\int\lambda(x)dx$. The numerator has units kg m, so it cannot itself be a position. Related rates likewise require the time-dependent chain rule: $dV/dt=4\pi r^2dr/dt$ for a sphere, evaluated at the specified instantaneous radius and rate.

</LessonSection>
