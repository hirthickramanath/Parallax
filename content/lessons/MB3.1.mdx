<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Rates and approximation create questions that ordinary substitution cannot settle. A difference quotient may be undefined at zero displacement while its nearby values approach a unique answer. Limits give that statement a precise meaning; continuity asks whether the point value agrees with it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

Imagine measuring values closer and closer to a marked point while the exact point is hidden. A removable hole changes the point value but not the nearby pattern. A jump is different: approaching from opposite sides produces incompatible targets.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

For a function defined on a punctured neighborhood of a, lim f(x) = L means every requested positive output error can be met by restricting the nonzero input distance. Continuity additionally requires f(a) to exist and equal L. A plotted pattern suggests a limit but does not prove it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The finite-limit definition is: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $0<|x-a|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$.

For $f(x)=3x+2$, propose $L=3a+2$. The output error is $|3x+2-(3a+2)|=3|x-a|$. Choose $\delta=\varepsilon/3$; then the required implication follows. This proves the limit for every a, not just tested values.

For a square, use factorization: $|x^2-a^2|=|x-a||x+a|$. First require $|x-a|<1$, so $|x+a|\le |x-a|+2|a|<1+2|a|$. Choose

$$\delta=\min\left(1,\frac\varepsilon{1+2|a|}\right).$$

Then the product is less than ε, proving $\lim_{x\to a}x^2=a^2$. The extra bound prevents the second factor from being uncontrolled.

For $g(x)=(x^2-1)/(x-1)$ and $x\ne1$, factorization gives $g(x)=x+1$. Therefore its limit at 1 is 2. The original formula remains undefined at 1; defining $g(1)=2$ creates a continuous extension.

Contrast $h(x)=|x|/x$ for nonzero x. On the right it is 1; on the left it is −1. If a two-sided limit L existed, both sides would have to converge to L, contradicting these unequal values. Thus no two-sided limit exists.

Finally, limits are unique. If distinct L and M were limits, choose ε = |L−M|/3 and x close enough for both error bounds. The triangle inequality gives $|L-M|\le|L-f(x)|+|f(x)-M|<2|L-M|/3$, a contradiction.


### Derivation and transfer checkpoint

For the quadratic at $a=1$, $|x^2-1|=|x-1||x+1|$. Restrict $|x-1|<1$ to obtain $0<x<2$ and $|x+1|<3$. The explicit choice $\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/3)$ then gives a strict $\varepsilon$ bound for every admitted $x$, not just a plotted sample. A graph can suggest a bound but cannot supply the universal quantifier.

A removable hole and a jump are different failures. For $x\ne2$, $(x^2-4)/(x-2)=x+2$; the limiting value is 4 regardless of an assigned value at the excluded point. Setting $g(2)=4$ restores continuity. If left and right limits differ, no single point assignment can make the common two-sided limit exist.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For f(x) = 3x + 2 near a = 4, the proposed limit is 14. To ensure output error below 0.03, take δ = 0.01. If 0 < |x−4| < 0.01, then |f(x)−14| = 3|x−4| < 0.03. The strict inequality matters.



### Worked investigation: A removable discontinuity versus a missing limit

For x≠2, (x²-4)/(x-2)=x+2, so the limit at x=2 is 4. The original expression is undefined at 2; cancellation proves the nearby limit, not a value there. Defining f(2)=4 creates a continuous extension, while defining f(2)=7 leaves the same limit but a discontinuity.

By contrast |x|/x approaches -1 from the left and +1 from the right at zero, so no two-sided limit exists. To justify the first limit quantitatively, |(x+2)-4|=|x-2|; choose delta=epsilon with 0&lt;|x-2|&lt;delta. A graph or a few decimal samples can suggest this argument but cannot replace its quantifiers.
