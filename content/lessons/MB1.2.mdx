<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Brightness ratios and radioactive timescales span enormous ranges. Logarithms compress multiplicative scales, while complex numbers encode amplitude and phase without treating an imaginary coordinate as a second physical displacement.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

An exponential counts repeated scale changes. A logarithm asks how many changes were needed. A complex number is a point and a rotation-scaling operation on a plane.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

For positive b other than 1, log base b is the inverse of b raised to a real power. A complex number is a+ib with i²=−1.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For positive $x,y$, $\log_b(xy)=\log_b x+\log_b y$: write $x=b^u$, $y=b^v$, then $xy=b^{u+v}$. Likewise $\log_b(x^p)=p\log_b x$ for positive x. There is no corresponding rule replacing the logarithm of a sum by a sum of logarithms. Real logarithms require positive arguments, and an exponential with positive base has positive real output.

The natural exponential is normalized so its derivative equals itself. A constant fractional growth rate gives $dy/dt=ky$ and $y(t)=y_0e^{kt}$. The product $kt$ must be dimensionless. For decay, the half-life satisfies $e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}}=1/2$, giving $t_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda$.

For $z=a+ib$, define its conjugate $\bar z=a-ib$ and modulus $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. Since $z\bar z=|z|^2$, division by a nonzero complex number can be performed by multiplying numerator and denominator by its conjugate.

Polar form is $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}$. Multiplication adds arguments and multiplies moduli; angles are defined modulo $2\pi$. De Moivre's formula follows by repeated multiplication. The n roots of a nonzero z have modulus $r^{1/n}$ and arguments $(\theta+2\pi k)/n$, k=0,...,n−1. Zero has only the root zero. Complex logarithms require a branch choice; real-log rules must not be extended blindly.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

For $z=a+ib$, multiply by its conjugate to obtain $z\bar z=a^2+b^2$; modulus is the positive square root, not the squared modulus itself. For positive real arguments, $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$. Principal complex arguments require branch control: $\operatorname{Log}(-1)=i\pi$ under the argument convention $(-\pi,\pi]$, while $\operatorname{Log}(1)=0$. The two sides of the product rule can therefore differ by $2\pi i$. Real exponential equations additionally use injectivity: equality of powers with a fixed positive base other than 1 implies equality of exponents.


### Derivation and transfer

A real logarithm requires a positive argument. Product and power rules must respect domains; over complex numbers a chosen branch adds further restrictions. The exponential is monotone on the real line, so it can preserve order when solving real logarithmic equations.

The display compares ln x under a positive scaling. It does not implement every complex branch, and a formal algebraic transformation cannot admit an original zero or negative real argument.

</LessonSection>
