<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The local Hubble law v≈H₀d is a low-redshift expansion for the cosmological flow; measured redshift also contains peculiar-motion and observational contributions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For sufficiently small cosmological redshift, $cz\approx H_0d$ after the relevant velocity/frame corrections. At higher redshift a relativistic cosmological distance relation replaces this linear approximation. Redshift is measured from wavelength ratios, not generally a unique Newtonian recession speed.

A distance ladder links geometric calibrators to standardizable objects and more distant samples. Each step has population, extinction, crowding, selection and calibration uncertainties, including correlations shared across many sources. Averaging many objects does not remove a common zero-point error.

Luminosity distance is defined by F=L/(4πD_L²), while angular-diameter distance relates physical transverse size and angle. They are not interchangeable at cosmological redshift. In a transparent metric cosmology with the usual photon-propagation assumptions, D_L=(1+z)²D_A.

Peculiar velocities strongly affect nearby redshifts, whereas higher-redshift fits depend more on expansion-model parameters. Compare likelihoods, covariance, calibrator choices and selection before interpreting a discrepancy as a new physical phenomenon. This lesson uses hypothetical values rather than reporting a newly measured H₀.


### Derive, test and transfer

For nearby sources dominated by expansion, v≈H₀d is the first-order distance–redshift relation. A peculiar velocity adds to the measured low-redshift motion, so fractional distance inference is especially noisy nearby. A slope fit must propagate errors and account for calibration; fitting v/d for each noisy point is not always equivalent to an appropriate regression.

Distance-ladder steps transfer calibration through geometric anchors, standardizable objects and more distant samples. Shared zero-point uncertainties correlate the steps rather than averaging away independently. At high redshift, a cosmological distance integral replaces the nearby linear approximation. The investigation changes a chosen H₀ and distance; it does not claim a new measurement.



### Worked inference: Keep a local expansion estimate separate from peculiar motion

1. Take the local linear model vcos=H0 d with H0=70 km/s/Mpc. A galaxy at 40 Mpc has model expansion velocity 2800 km/s.

2. If it also has a +300 km/s line-of-sight peculiar velocity in the same frame, the observed estimate is 3100 km/s. Ignoring the peculiar part gives d≈44.29 Mpc rather than 40 Mpc.

3. This illustrates an inference bias, not a universal correction. High-redshift observations require a cosmological distance relation, and nearby bound systems need not follow a simple Hubble flow.

</LessonSection>
