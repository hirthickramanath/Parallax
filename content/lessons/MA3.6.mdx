<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Hermitian structure connects complex vector spaces with real-valued quadratic forms and orthogonal mode expansions. It is foundational for vibration models and finite-dimensional quantum observables, but different symmetry conditions must not be conflated.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

An operator equal to its adjoint is symmetric relative to the inner product. Its eigen-directions can be chosen orthonormal, so the operator separates into independent real-weighted directions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

In a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space, A is Hermitian if A=A*. It is normal if AA*=A*A. Every Hermitian matrix is normal, but normal matrices can have nonreal eigenvalues.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If Av=λv with v≠0 and A=A*, then $v^*Av=\lambda v^*v$ is real because its conjugate equals itself. Since v*v>0, λ is real. If Au=λu and Av=μv with distinct real eigenvalues, the adjoint identity gives $(\lambda-\mu)u^*v=0$, so the eigenvectors are orthogonal.

The finite-dimensional spectral theorem states that Hermitian A has an orthonormal eigenbasis and $A=U\Lambda U^*$ with U unitary and Λ real diagonal. A proof chooses one complex eigenvector, uses reality of its eigenvalue, observes that its orthogonal complement is invariant, and continues by induction on dimension. Existence of an eigenvalue over the complexes follows from the characteristic polynomial; orthogonality and induction complete the basis claim.

More generally a complex normal matrix is unitarily diagonalizable, with possibly complex diagonal entries. A real symmetric matrix can be orthogonally diagonalized with real eigenvectors. A defective matrix is not covered by these conclusions. In infinite-dimensional operator theory, domains and continuous spectra require additional hypotheses; not every operator has a finite-style eigenvector basis.

For Hermitian A, the Rayleigh quotient $R(x)=x^*Ax/(x^*x)$ is a weighted average of real eigenvalues in an orthonormal eigenbasis. Thus its minimum and maximum are the smallest and largest eigenvalues. Positive definiteness is equivalent to every eigenvalue being positive, and allows a unique positive-definite Hermitian square root.

In a finite quantum model a normalized state has expectation x*Ax for a Hermitian observable, while outcome probabilities are squared projections onto its eigenspaces. An expectation can lie between eigenvalues and need not be one outcome. This mathematical structure alone is not a full derivation of quantum measurement physics.


### Derive, test and transfer

A finite-dimensional Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis. This follows by conjugate symmetry for reality and by restricting successively to invariant orthogonal complements. Degenerate eigenspaces admit an orthonormal basis, but arbitrary vectors chosen inside one degenerate eigenspace are not automatically mutually orthogonal.

For real symmetric A, a rotation can diagonalize a two-dimensional quadratic form. Its eigenvalues bound the Rayleigh quotient v transpose A v divided by v transpose v. An arbitrary real matrix lacks this theorem's hypotheses; merely having real entries does not make it symmetric. Infinite-dimensional self-adjoint operators further require domains and can have continuous spectrum, so the finite matrix statement is not a complete operator theorem.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For A=[[2,1],[1,2]], the normalized sum and difference vectors have eigenvalues 3 and 1. The unit state (1,0) has expectation 2, halfway between them. It is not itself an eigenstate, so expectation 2 is not an extra eigenvalue.
