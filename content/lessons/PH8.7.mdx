<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A pure spin-half state can be represented by a unit Bloch vector r; measurement along unit axis n has probabilities (1±r·n)/2.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The density matrix is $\rho=(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$, with |r|≤1; pure states have |r|=1. The projector for spin up along n is (I+n·σ)/2, giving p_up=(1+r·n)/2. The minus outcome has the complementary probability. A maximally mixed state has r=0 and gives one half along every direction.

A Stern–Gerlach field gradient couples a magnetic moment to spatial motion, separating measurement outcomes under the apparatus approximations. It is not simply a uniform field rotating an already separated pair of classical orientations. A selected output prepares a conditional state; discarding the outcome produces a different ensemble description.

Prepare z-up, measure x and select x-up, then measure z. The first x probability is one half and the final z-up probability is one half conditional on the x selection. Without conditioning, probabilities must sum over both x branches with their weights. The intervening measurement destroys the original z certainty in the ideal projective model.

A uniform magnetic field generates unitary precession rather than spatial filtering. For H=ℏωσ_z/2, the Bloch vector rotates around z with the appropriate sign convention; measurement and rotation are different operations. Experimental imperfections, finite gradients, decoherence and detector efficiency require extensions before interpreting real beam counts.


### Derivation and transfer

A spin-up state measured along an axis tilted by θ has spin-up probability cos²(θ/2). An ideal Stern–Gerlach apparatus correlates spin projection with spatial separation; it does not reveal a hidden classical orientation already sharp along every axis.

The display predicts an ensemble probability. Consecutive noncommuting measurements require state update, and detector inefficiency changes observed counts. It is not a deterministic trajectory calculation for each atom.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For an initial z-up state and a measurement axis at 60° to z, p_up=(1+cos60°)/2=.75. The down probability is .25. For a perpendicular axis both are .5; no single hidden classical vector projection replaces these discrete outcomes.
