<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Fourier optics separates field propagation, coherence and instrument response; intensity-only reasoning can lose phase and correlation information.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

First-order mutual coherence is a correlation of complex fields; a normalized degree of coherence includes intensity normalization and an averaging convention.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For stationary fields, $\Gamma_{12}(\tau)=\langle E_1^*(t)E_2(t+\tau)\rangle$ and γ₁₂=Γ₁₂/√(I₁I₂). Equal-intensity two-beam visibility is |γ₁₂| in the stated scalar measurement. Temporal stationarity and the averaging process must be specified before using this relation.

Under appropriate stationarity/integrability conventions, the spectrum and temporal autocorrelation form a Fourier-transform pair. Narrow bandwidth often gives a long coherence time, but spectral shape controls the precise envelope and constants. Spatial coherence depends on source extent and propagation, not only temporal bandwidth.

An ideal thin lens maps a suitable field to a scaled Fourier transform at its focal plane under paraxial coherent assumptions. A finite pupil multiplies spatial-frequency content and determines the coherent point-spread amplitude. In incoherent imaging the intensity point-spread function convolves object intensity; coherent imaging instead acts on amplitudes before squaring.

The modulation transfer function describes contrast response for the stated linear intensity model. A pixel samples an already filtered field/intensity, so aliasing and optical blur are different processes. Phase retrieval and deconvolution need constraints and noise handling; dividing by tiny transfer values can amplify error rather than restore absent information.


### Derive, test and transfer

For two beams with intensities I1 and I2, the interference term is 2sqrt(I1I2) times the real part of their degree of coherence with its phase. Equal beams with real coherence magnitude γ give I=2I0(1+γcosφ). The resulting visibility is γ in this restricted equal-intensity case.

Unequal beam intensities reduce visibility even with full coherence, so low contrast alone does not uniquely diagnose decoherence. Fourier optics also depends on aperture, propagation convention and spatial-frequency filtering. A finite sampling grid can alias high frequencies; plotting a continuous formula does not validate a discrete reconstruction. The investigation holds equal beam intensities fixed to isolate the coherence parameter.

</LessonSection>
