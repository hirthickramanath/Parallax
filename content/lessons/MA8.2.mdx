Why can a sample average look Gaussian when individual observations do not? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Limit theorems explain averaging under explicit assumptions. They are not a license to fit every distribution with a Gaussian or to ignore rare tails and correlated samples.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A normalized sum balances many independent contributions. As the number grows, its characteristic shape can simplify even though the original observations retain their own distribution.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For independent identically distributed variables with finite mean μ and finite nonzero variance σ², the classical central limit theorem gives a standard-normal limit for √n times the centered sample mean divided by σ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For iid finite-variance observations, $E[\bar X]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$. Chebyshev's inequality gives $P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$, proving convergence in probability of the average. This law-of-large-numbers statement concerns concentration, not the Gaussian shape of standardized errors.

The classical CLT states $\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)/\sigma\Rightarrow N(0,1)$. A characteristic-function proof expands the centered single-observation characteristic function near zero to 1−σ²t²/2 plus a smaller remainder, then raises its t/√n version to the nth power. This is a proof outline; the regularity and convergence steps rely on the finite-second-moment theorem.

A Gaussian observation model is not the same as a CLT approximation for an average. At small n, skewness and rare events can make tail probabilities inaccurate. With a finite third absolute moment, a Berry–Esseen bound controls the distribution-function approximation at order n⁻¹/² with a distribution-dependent coefficient. There is no universal magic sample size that fixes every population.

Bernoulli sums are binomial under identical independent trials. Poisson limits arise for many rare trials with controlled total mean. Their Gaussian approximations need appropriate count sizes and can be poor at low counts or boundaries. A Cauchy distribution has no finite mean or variance; averaging iid Cauchy variables does not produce the familiar Gaussian concentration.

Correlated time-series samples require covariance terms, not simply σ²/n. Infinite variance, changing populations or strong dependence require other limit theorems or direct models. Report whether the uncertainty formula describes observations, a sample mean or an inferred parameter; these are not interchangeable objects.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For n=100 independent observations with standard deviation 2, the sample-mean standard deviation is 2/√100=0.2. Chebyshev bounds probability of a deviation at least 1 by 4/(100×1)=0.04, regardless of the distribution shape under the finite-variance assumptions.

</LessonSection>
