<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Markov models describe transitions between states, while Poisson processes describe continuous-time arrival counts. Similar-looking distributions can hide very different dependence and convergence properties.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Stationarity means an entire distribution is unchanged by one transition. Convergence asks whether an arbitrary starting distribution actually approaches it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A discrete-time Markov chain has next-state probabilities determined by its current state under the model. A homogeneous transition matrix P has nonnegative rows summing to one.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a row probability vector π, one step gives πP. A stationary distribution satisfies $\pi P=\pi$. A finite irreducible chain has a unique stationary distribution, but convergence from every initial state additionally requires aperiodicity in the standard finite-chain theorem. The two-state deterministic alternation P=[[0,1],[1,0]] has stationary distribution (1/2,1/2), yet a point start alternates forever.

Detailed balance πᵢPᵢⱼ=πⱼPⱼᵢ implies stationarity by summing over i. It is a sufficient reversible construction, not a necessary condition for every stationary chain. Absorbing or reducible chains can have multiple stationary distributions and start-dependent long-time behaviour.

A homogeneous Poisson process with rate λ has independent stationary increments and $N(t+s)-N(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda s)$. Thus E[N(t)]=Var[N(t)]=λt. Specifying those one-time marginals alone is insufficient to establish independent increments; dependence across time is part of the process definition.

The waiting time to the first event satisfies P(T>t)=P(N(t)=0)=e⁻λt, so it is exponential with mean 1/λ and a memoryless survival property. Successive waiting times are iid exponentials in the homogeneous Poisson model. A time-varying rate leads to an integrated intensity and generally nonstationary increments.

Real photon or event streams can include dead time, bunching, changing source intensity or instrumental triggers. These effects can violate a simple Poisson process even if a limited histogram looks plausible. State whether transitions, waiting times or aggregate counts were observed and test the corresponding dependence assumptions.

</LessonSection>

For rate λ=3 events/s over 2 s, the expected count and variance are both 6. The mean waiting time is 1/3 s, not 1/6 s. Separately, a deterministic alternating chain illustrates why having the stationary vector (1/2,1/2) does not imply point-start convergence.
