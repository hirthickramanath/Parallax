<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Force laws, decay measurements and population changes often specify a rate rather than a finished curve. The mathematical task is to find a function whose derivative satisfies that relation and whose initial state matches the experiment.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A rate law tells the slope at each allowed state. An initial condition chooses where the trajectory starts. Different starting points can follow the same rule without tracing the same curve.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

An initial-value problem combines y′ = F(t,y) with y(t₀)=y₀. For the basic local existence/uniqueness theorem, continuity of F supports existence and a local Lipschitz condition in y supports uniqueness. These hypotheses must be checked; there are equations with nonunique solutions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Consider y′ = ky for constant k. For y≠0, separate variables: dy/y = k dt. Integration gives ln|y| = kt + C, hence $y = A e^{kt}$. The equilibrium y=0 must be restored because division by y excluded it. The single formula with A=0 includes it, and y(0)=y₀ gives $y=y_0e^{kt}$.

For a linear equation y′+p(t)y=q(t), define $\mu(t)=e^{\int p(t)dt}$, so μ′=pμ. Multiplying gives

$$(\mu y)'=\mu q,\qquad y=\frac{C+\int\mu q\,dt}{\mu}.$$

The integrating factor is nonzero, so this division is allowed. Continuous p and q on an interval make the construction valid there.

For y′+ay=b with a>0, the result is $y=b/a+(y_0-b/a)e^{-at}$. Substitution checks both the equation and initial condition; the solution tends to the equilibrium b/a.

Uniqueness cannot be taken for granted. For y′=√|y| with y(0)=0, y(t)=0 is one forward solution. Another is y(t)=t²/4 for t≥0. More generally the solution may remain zero until any c≥0 and then follow (t−c)²/4. At the join the derivative is zero from both sides. The square-root function is not locally Lipschitz at zero, so the usual uniqueness theorem does not apply.

An approximate numerical method such as Euler uses $y_{n+1}=y_n+\Delta t\, F(t_n,y_n)$. This discretization is not the differential equation itself. Its accuracy and stability depend on step size and the problem.


### Derivation and transfer checkpoint

Separating $y'=ky$ on a nonzero branch yields $\ln|y|=kt+C$, hence $y=Ae^{kt}$. The equilibrium $y=0$ must be checked separately because division by $y$ excluded it. For $y'+ay=b$ with constant coefficients and $a\ne0$, the equilibrium is $b/a$ and deviations evolve as $Ce^{-at}$. Initial data determine $C$.

Existence and uniqueness are different claims. Continuity of the right-hand side can support local existence, while a suitable local Lipschitz condition in $y$ gives uniqueness. Numerical Euler steps replace the exact flow by a tangent-line step and introduce both accuracy and stability restrictions. For negative $k$, the multiplier $1+kh$ must be checked before trusting apparent decay.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If a cooling model has T′ = −0.1(T−20) in °C per minute and T(0)=80°C, then $T(t)=20+60e^{-0.1t}$. At t=10 min, T≈42.1°C. The model assumes a fixed ambient temperature and a rate proportional to the temperature difference.



### Worked investigation: Relaxation and a stability check

For y'=-k(y-y_eq) with k>0 and constant equilibrium, write z=y-y_eq. Separation gives z(t)=z(0)exp(-kt), so perturbations decay without a finite-time crossing. If y_eq=3, y(0)=11 and k=ln(2) per second, then y(1)=7 and y(2)=5. The excess halves each second; the whole value y does not halve.

The limiting value is 3, consistent with setting y'=0. Units require k to be inverse time. If k were negative the same formula would amplify perturbations, and the equilibrium would be unstable. A real cooling or population model may lose this constant-coefficient form when its environment or response coefficient changes.
