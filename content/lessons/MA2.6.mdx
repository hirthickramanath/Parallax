<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Flux measures how much of a vector field crosses an oriented surface. A field tangent to the surface contributes nothing even if its magnitude is large.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A patch has both an area and a chosen normal. The dot product with that area vector selects the crossing component.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For an oriented smooth parameterized surface r(u,v), vector flux is ∫∫F(r)·(rᵤ×rᵥ)du dv. Scalar surface area integrals use the cross-product magnitude.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The parameter tangents rᵤ and rᵥ span an infinitesimal parallelogram. Its area vector is $(r_u\times r_v)du\,dv$, assuming a regular parameterization where the cross product is nonzero. Swapping parameter order reverses orientation; it does not change scalar surface area.

For a graph z=g(x,y) with upward orientation, the area vector is $(-g_x,-g_y,1)dx\,dy$. The scalar area element is $\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\,dx\,dy$. Replacing one with the other confuses signed flux and area. A unit normal times scalar area gives the same vector area as the cross-product expression.

A closed surface is conventionally oriented outward for flux theorems. Piecewise smooth surfaces can be handled patch by patch with consistent orientation, including boundaries and exceptional parameter points. Some abstract surfaces, such as a Möbius strip, do not admit a continuous global normal, so a standard globally oriented flux integral needs care.

For a sphere of radius R centered at the origin, the outward unit normal is r/R. For F=r, F·n=R, so total flux is 4πR³. This scales as a volume, consistent with divergence 3. For a constant field, the total signed flux through the closed sphere is zero, although each hemisphere can have a nonzero contribution.

A flux value has units field times area; physical interpretations differ. A velocity field gives volume flow only with the appropriate geometry, while mass flow uses density times velocity. An electric field gives electric flux, not a count of particles physically crossing the surface. State the field and its meaning before calling every flux a transport rate.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a parameterized surface r(u,v), oriented vector area is $(r_u\times r_v)du\,dv$. Flux is the integral of F dot that vector; interchanging the parameter order reverses the sign. Scalar area instead uses the magnitude of the cross product.

For a sphere and radial field F=(x,y,z), the outward normal is r/R, so F dot n=R and flux is 4πR³. This can also be checked using divergence three times enclosed volume. A surface element is not usually its flat coordinate projection: a graph z=f(x,y) has scalar factor $\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}$. Keeping oriented flux distinct from unsigned area avoids a common sign and Jacobian mistake.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For constant F=(0,0,3) through a horizontal unit square, upward flux is 3 and downward flux −3. Through a vertical unit square with normal along x, flux is zero. All three surfaces have the same area but different normal projections.
