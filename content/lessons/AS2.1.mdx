<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Brightness at a detector is not intrinsic energy output. Astronomical distance estimates and stellar comparisons require separating luminosity, geometric dilution, extinction and the measured wavelength band.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

The same emitted power spreads over a larger sphere farther away. A detector samples only part of that sphere and usually only part of the spectrum.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Luminosity is emitted power. Flux is received power per collecting area; spectral flux density is additionally per frequency or wavelength interval.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For an isotropic steady source in a transparent local Euclidean setting, conservation over a sphere gives $F=L/(4\pi d^2)$. This is not a universal law for anisotropic beams or absorbed light. Cosmological luminosity distance is defined so the same bolometric form holds, but it is not simply a static geometric separation and includes propagation effects.

For a shared band and zero-point system, apparent magnitudes satisfy $m_2-m_1=-2.5\log_{10}(F_2/F_1)$. A larger magnitude is fainter. Five magnitude units correspond to a factor 100 in flux. Absolute magnitude M is the apparent magnitude a source would have at 10 pc in the stated band under the defined extinction convention.

For a local inverse-square model with band extinction A, $m-M=5\log_{10}(d/10\,\mathrm{pc})+A$. The logarithm takes the dimensionless distance ratio. Setting A=0 without evidence can make an extinction-affected object appear farther away. A bolometric correction relates a specific band magnitude to a bolometric convention; it is not a universal constant for all temperatures.

Spectral densities transform with their interval: $F_\lambda\,d\lambda=F_\nu\,|d\nu|$ and $F_\lambda=(c/\lambda^2)F_\nu$ at corresponding wavelength and frequency. Flux values per nanometre and per metre differ numerically by a unit conversion. Integrating the appropriate density over a passband, including response, gives a band measurement rather than simply reading one central wavelength.

A parallax p in arcseconds gives d in parsecs as 1/p for a positive geometric parallax in the ideal relation. Inverting a noisy or negative measured parallax requires an inference model and uncertainty treatment, not discarding awkward data. Luminosity uncertainty inherits distance uncertainty quadratically in the simple flux model.


### Derive, test and transfer

Integrate the outgoing energy crossing a sphere: $L=\int F\,dA=4\pi d^2F$ for isotropic radiation in Euclidean space. Therefore doubling distance quarters flux, while doubling luminosity doubles it. Apparent brightness alone cannot distinguish a luminous distant source from a dim nearby one. With absorption, add a transmission factor rather than redefining intrinsic luminosity.

The magnitude difference is $m_2-m_1=-2.5\log_{10}(F_2/F_1)$ for the same photometric convention. Combining inverse-square dilution with the definition of absolute magnitude yields $m-M=5\log_{10}(d/10\,\mathrm{pc})$ in the extinction-free nearby limit. A band-limited magnitude requires a specified filter and zero point; bolometric luminosity cannot be substituted silently. For cosmological distances, luminosity distance packages redshift effects and differs from angular-diameter distance.



### Worked inference: Infer luminosity from both flux and distance

1. Two transparent, isotropic sources have measured fluxes FA=8 and FB=2 in the same bolometric units, with distances dA=10 pc and dB=20 pc.

2. Since L=4πd²F, LB/LA=(2/8)(20/10)²=1. The fainter source can have the same luminosity.

3. The ratio calculation cancels the common distance units and 4π. Extinction, anisotropy and unmatched bandpasses would require corrections before making this inference.
