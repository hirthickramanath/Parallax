<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Photometry estimates brightness in a defined band and aperture; spectroscopy estimates a wavelength-dependent signal through a calibrated instrument response. Both require propagating uncertainty through calibration and subtraction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Background subtraction removes an estimated mean contribution. The random arrivals that produced the source-region background and the uncertainty of its estimate remain in the result.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A calibrated measurement specifies raw counts, bias/dark treatment, response correction, extraction geometry, wavelength or band calibration, and an uncertainty model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For aperture photometry, let C be source-aperture counts over n pixels and b a background estimate per pixel from a separate region. Net counts are S=C−nb. If C and b are independent, $\operatorname{Var}(S)=\operatorname{Var}(C)+n^2\operatorname{Var}(b)$. If b is the average of m independent equal-noise background pixels, its variance is that single-pixel variance divided by m. Shared calibration or resampling can introduce covariance that this formula omits.

For small fractional flux uncertainty, differentiating m=−2.5log10F+constant gives $\sigma_m\approx(2.5/\ln10)\sigma_F/F\approx1.086\sigma_F/F$. At low or negative net flux a symmetric magnitude error is not appropriate; model flux likelihoods and nondetections rather than taking the logarithm of a nonpositive result.

Spectral resolving power is R=λ/Δλ under a declared width convention. It is distinct from wavelength accuracy and from the statistical precision of a centroid. A high-signal line centroid can be localized more precisely than one resolution element under a valid model, while blending or calibration biases can dominate.

A spectroscopic extraction needs wavelength calibration, sky subtraction, detector corrections and instrumental line-spread-function treatment. Flux calibration and atmospheric corrections add their own uncertainty. Equivalent width $W_\lambda=\int(1-F_\lambda/F_c)d\lambda$ is positive for absorption under this convention and depends on continuum placement.

Compare observations through a forward model of the actual response rather than assigning every pixel a perfect monochromatic flux. Resampling can correlate errors. A reproducible result retains raw data or provenance, masks, calibration versions, extraction settings and units; a smooth-looking corrected spectrum is not evidence that these uncertainties vanished.


### Derive, test and transfer

For independent photoelectrons, source shot-noise variance equals the expected source count. If independent sky, detector and background-estimation contributions sum to V, a useful model is $\mathrm{SNR}=N_s/\sqrt{N_s+V}$. In a source-dominated exposure N_s scales with time t and SNR scales as √t, so four times the exposure is required to double SNR.

Background subtraction removes a mean, not the associated counting variance. Shared calibration errors and correlated pixels do not generally average down like independent Poisson counts. Spectroscopy further spreads photons across wavelength bins, trading per-bin counts against resolution. A measured line strength needs a continuum model and uncertainty propagation; a formal count significance is not itself proof of an astrophysical identification.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If an aperture has C=1000 counts over n=10 pixels and background estimate b=20 counts per pixel, net signal is 800. If Var(C)=1000 and Var(b)=4 with independent quantities, net variance is 1000+100×4=1400, not merely 800.
