<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A shock is a thin transition across which mass, momentum and energy fluxes obey conservation with a specified EOS and loss model; entropy increases in the physical dissipative solution.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a steady planar hydrodynamic shock, mass flux ρv is continuous and momentum flux p+ρv² is continuous absent additional stresses. The energy jump includes enthalpy and kinetic energy for an adiabatic ideal-gas model. A discontinuity solution must satisfy the physical entropy condition, not only algebraic conservation.

For a strong nonrelativistic ideal-gas shock with γ=5/3 and negligible upstream pressure, compression ratio is $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)=4$. Magnetic pressure, cosmic-ray feedback, radiation losses and different EOS change the result. The elementary downstream temperature scales as shock speed squared with composition factors.

An ideal energy-conserving spherical blast in a uniform ambient medium has $R\propto(Et^2/\rho)^{1/5}$ in the Sedov–Taylor similarity regime. Free expansion and later radiative stages have different scalings. A shell radius and age cannot be interpreted with one similarity law regardless of its evolutionary stage.

Observed remnant emission weights electrons, ions, magnetic fields and accelerated particles differently. Electron-ion temperatures need not immediately equilibrate, and non-equilibrium ionization affects spectra. A fitted X-ray temperature is therefore not automatically the unique shock speed or explosion energy without a plasma and geometry model.


### Derive, test and transfer

Mass, momentum and energy conservation across a planar steady ideal-gas shock give $\rho_2/\rho_1=(\gamma+1)M_1^2/[(\gamma-1)M_1^2+2]$. At M=1 the compression is one; for a strong shock it approaches $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which is four for γ=5/3. The finite limit follows from retaining the energy equation, not merely balancing momentum.

A radiative shock can cool downstream and compress further; magnetic pressure can resist compression, and cosmic-ray energy transfer changes the budget. Supernova-remnant dynamics also evolve with swept-up mass and ambient structure. The displayed discontinuity relation does not supply a whole remnant trajectory or particle-acceleration spectrum.

</LessonSection>
