<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Physical measurements support models over tested regimes. A mathematical theorem has a different obligation: every case allowed by its hypotheses must follow logically. Induction provides one way to cross an infinite family without measuring every member.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Dominoes fall only if a first domino falls and each falling domino reaches the next. A perfect connection rule without a starting point proves nothing.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

Induction proves P(n) for all integers n≥n₀ by establishing P(n₀) and P(k) implies P(k+1) for an arbitrary integer k≥n₀.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

To prove $1+2+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2$, first check n=1. For the induction step assume the statement at an arbitrary k. Adding k+1 gives $k(k+1)/2+(k+1)=(k+1)(k+2)/2$, exactly the statement at k+1. The assumption was used conditionally, not declared true without proof. The base case initiates repeated implication.

Strong induction assumes all statements from the base through k to prove the next one. For integer factorization, an integer m>1 is prime or a product of smaller integers greater than 1. The latter factors can be decomposed using the earlier hypotheses. This establishes existence of a prime factorization, while uniqueness requires a further argument.

A universal statement is refuted by one counterexample. To disprove that every increasing function has positive derivative everywhere, use $f(x)=x^3$: it is strictly increasing but its derivative is zero at 0. A converse is a new claim: P implies Q does not itself establish Q implies P.

For a proof by contradiction assume the negation of the conclusion together with the hypotheses and derive an impossibility. The classic parity proof for the irrationality of $\sqrt2$ begins with a lowest-terms fraction p/q. From $p^2=2q^2$, p is even, then q is even, contradicting lowest terms. The lowest-terms requirement is essential.

A clear proof states its domain, assumptions, intermediate reason and conclusion. Numerical examples are useful for finding a conjecture or an error; they do not replace the logical link for an arbitrary case.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

For the sum identity, the base case is $1=1(2)/2$. Assuming $\sum_{j=1}^n j=n(n+1)/2$, adding $n+1$ gives $(n+1)(n+2)/2$. The hypothesis is an assumption within an implication, not permission to assert the desired conclusion outright. If a recurrence advances by two indices, a single base case covers only one parity class. For $a_{n+1}=a_n+3$, induction proves $a_n=2+3(n-1)$; testing the claimed formula at $n=1$ prevents an off-by-one error.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

The claim n²≥n holds for integers n≥1. Base: 1≥1. If k²≥k, then (k+1)²=k²+2k+1≥3k+1≥k+1 since k≥1. Thus the implication and base prove all permitted integers. A shorter direct proof is n(n−1)≥0.

</LessonSection>
