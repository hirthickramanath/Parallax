<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Protoplanetary discs contain gas and solids whose coupled dynamics can form planetesimals, cores and planets under model-dependent conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Small grains experience drag and can drift relative to pressure-supported gas. Sticking, fragmentation and radial drift compete; a smooth extrapolation from dust to planet is not guaranteed. Concentration mechanisms and collective effects can enable larger bound bodies in appropriate regimes.

A planet’s approximate Hill radius on a circular orbit is $r_H=a[M_p/(3M_*)]^{1/3}$ for small mass ratio. It compares local planetary gravity with stellar tidal effects, not an exact spherical boundary for every eccentric/inclined system. Gas and solid capture also depend on dissipation and flow.

Core accretion couples solid growth with gaseous-envelope cooling and supply, while gravitational disc instability requires conditions involving self-gravity and cooling. These labels do not remove the need for transport and thermodynamics. Migration torques can change semimajor axes while planets grow.

Snow lines describe condensation transitions with composition/pressure and disc-temperature assumptions; they move as discs evolve. Final architectures reflect resonances, scattering and disc dispersal as well as local formation radius. An observed orbital radius is not automatically the exact birthplace of its planet.


### Derive, test and transfer

Growth can proceed before radial loss only if its characteristic time is short enough compared with drift. Comparing $t_{growth}/t_{drift}$ makes that necessary competition explicit, but both times depend on particle size, gas pressure gradients, turbulence and collisions. A ratio below one is not a proof that every particle becomes a planet.

Discs carry angular momentum and evolve while solids settle, collide, fragment and concentrate. A radial pressure gradient can make gas rotate slightly below the local Kepler speed, producing aerodynamic drift for solids. Streaming instabilities and gravitational collapse introduce collective dynamics beyond a two-clock estimate. The display treats the clocks as controlled inputs; deriving them is part of a physical disc model, not something inferred from the ratio alone.

</LessonSection>
