<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Valence-bond models build electronic states from localized atomic-orbital combinations and spin coupling. Hybrid orbitals are linear combinations within a selected atomic orbital space.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Constructing hybrids is a basis transformation, not the creation of extra electrons or an observable collision between isolated atomic orbitals. Four tetrahedrally directed combinations of one s and three p functions form a useful sp³ basis in an idealized model. Different bases can span the same orbital space while describing the same total state.

A localized σ bond has approximately cylindrical overlap along the bond axis, while π bonding uses side-on orbital character with a nodal plane containing that axis in the simple diatomic description. Multiple bonding and conjugation can require a delocalized treatment beyond independent two-centre pairs.

For ideal tetrahedral directions, $\cos\theta=-1/3$, giving about 109.47°. Actual equilibrium geometry minimizes the full electronic and nuclear energy under the model, not an independent hybridization command. Lone pairs, substituents, symmetry and electronic degeneracies can distort ideal directions.

Valence-bond and molecular-orbital descriptions are alternative expansions of electronic structure, not necessarily mutually exclusive physical realities. Correlation, resonance and basis completeness control their quantitative quality. Simple d-orbital hybrid labels for hypervalent main-group molecules should not be treated as proof of substantial d participation without electronic evidence.


### Derive, test and transfer

For equivalent ideal spn hybrids made from one s and n p contributions, orthogonality yields cosθ=−1/n in the simple directed-hybrid construction. This gives linear sp, trigonal sp² and tetrahedral sp³ reference angles. These are model geometries, not exact bond-angle laws for every molecule.

Lone pairs, unequal ligands, multiple bonding and the actual electronic wavefunction can shift a geometry away from an ideal hybrid picture. Hybrid orbitals also depend on how occupied states are represented; they are not unique directly observable objects. Use the molecular-geometry explorer to compare ideal electron-domain geometry with actual molecular shape rather than treating a hybrid label as the complete explanation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

An sp³ basis combines one s and three p functions into four hybrids, preserving the dimension four. If four ideal bond directions are tetrahedral, each pair has direction cosine −1/3. The word hybrid does not add a fifth orbital or prescribe an actual excitation history.
