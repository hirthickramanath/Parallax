<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A Landau free-energy expansion describes a coarse order parameter near an appropriate transition, constrained by symmetry and stabilized by higher-order terms.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a scalar order parameter m with m→−m symmetry at zero field, use $f(m)=f_0+a m^2/2+b m^4/4-hm$. If b>0 and h=0, stationarity gives m(a+bm²)=0. For a>0 the minimum is m=0; for a&lt;0 the minima are m=±√(−a/b). Second derivatives distinguish stable minima from the stationary maximum. A symmetric free energy can therefore have two symmetry-related equilibrium minima.

If a=a₀(T−T_c), mean field predicts |m|∝(T_c−T)¹ᐟ² below the transition. The susceptibility above it is (∂m/∂h)=1/a in this simple normalization. These are mean-field exponents, not universal exact values for every spatial dimension. The expansion assumes that the chosen order parameter and retained analytic terms describe the relevant long-scale physics.

For b&lt;0 a positive sixth-order term can stabilize the polynomial and allow a first-order transition. Equality of free energies, not mere disappearance of a local minimum, determines coexistence. Metastable minima, barriers and spinodals are distinct; nucleation kinetics can delay an observed transition. For a fluid, density rather than a magnetic moment can serve as the order parameter, with the correct symmetry and field correspondence treated carefully.

An interface costs gradient energy, often modeled by κ|∇m|²/2 with κ>0. The resulting functional includes spatial variation absent from a uniform polynomial. Near criticality, fluctuations and correlations can invalidate mean field; dimensionality, interaction range and conserved variables matter. A finite polynomial fit cannot by itself prove the thermodynamic order of a real material transition.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a=−4, b=1 and h=0, the minima are m=±2. At m=2 the curvature a+3bm² is −4+12=8, confirming local stability. At m=0 the curvature is −4, so the central stationary point is unstable.

</LessonSection>
