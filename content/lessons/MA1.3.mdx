<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Sequences formalize repeated approximation. A simulated trajectory at shrinking step sizes and a sequence of measurements both require distinguishing a proposed limit from finite evidence about it.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A limit controls the whole tail, not just occasional terms. Once a threshold index is crossed, every later term must stay inside any chosen tolerance.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A real sequence aₙ converges to L if for every ε>0 there exists N such that n≥N implies |aₙ−L|&lt;ε. N may depend on ε, but not on the later n.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For $a_n=1/n$, choose an integer N>1/ε. Then n≥N gives 0&lt;1/n≤1/N&lt;ε. The bound must cover every later term; finding one close term does not prove convergence.

Limits are unique. If L and M were distinct limits, choose ε=|L−M|/3 and a tail where both bounds hold. The triangle inequality gives $|L-M|\le|L-a_n|+|a_n-M|<2|L-M|/3$, a contradiction. A convergent sequence is bounded: its tail lies within distance 1 of L, and its finitely many earlier terms have a finite maximum magnitude.

If aₙ→a and bₙ→b, sums and products converge to the corresponding values. For products write $a_nb_n-ab=a_n(b_n-b)+b(a_n-a)$ and use boundedness of aₙ to control the first factor. Quotients require b≠0; then the denominator stays away from zero eventually. A limit law without this condition can fail.

A subsequence selects strictly increasing indices. Every subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit. Conversely, different subsequential limits prove nonconvergence. Bolzano–Weierstrass says every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence, not necessarily that the full sequence converges. A bisection proof repeatedly selects a closed half-interval containing infinitely many terms, then uses completeness and increasing indices.

A sequence is Cauchy if its tail terms become uniformly close to one another. Convergence implies Cauchy by the triangle inequality. In the real numbers the converse follows from completeness; it need not hold in the rationals. “Infinite terms close together” is insufficient unless the bound covers all sufficiently late pairs.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

For $a_n=1/n$, choose an integer $N>1/\varepsilon$. Then every $n\geq N$ obeys $|a_n|\leq1/N<\varepsilon$. Testing many values cannot replace the word every in this tail condition. Quotient limit laws additionally require a nonzero denominator limit; division by a vanishing limiting denominator needs a separate argument.

A sequence is Cauchy when for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is one $N$ controlling $|a_m-a_n|$ for all $m,n\geq N$. Completeness connects this internal tail condition to convergence in $\mathbb R$. Boundedness alone yields a convergent subsequence, not convergence of the original sequence: $(-1)^n$ has two incompatible subsequential limits.


A useful strengthening is that a bounded real sequence with exactly one possible subsequential limit must converge to it. Otherwise some fixed $\varepsilon>0$ permits a subsequence remaining at least $\varepsilon$ away. This subsequence is bounded, so Bolzano–Weierstrass provides a convergent subsubsequence whose limit still remains at least $\varepsilon$ away, contradicting uniqueness.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For aₙ=(−1)ⁿ, even-index terms equal 1 and odd-index terms equal −1. These subsequences have different limits, so the full bounded sequence does not converge. For aₙ=(−1)ⁿ/n, the bound |aₙ|≤1/n proves convergence to zero despite alternating signs.
