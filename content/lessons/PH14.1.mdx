<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A Bravais lattice specifies translations; a crystal basis attaches constituents. Reciprocal vectors encode periodic phase matching.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Constructive elastic scattering from lattice translations requires momentum transfer equal to a reciprocal lattice vector under the ideal periodic model. A plane-spacing form is $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$, with θ the half-scattering-angle convention. Different conventions for the instrument’s displayed 2θ must not be mixed.

A structure factor sums basis amplitudes with relative phases. Some reciprocal-lattice peaks vanish by basis interference even though translation periodicity permits them. Atomic form factors, thermal motion and instrument geometry affect intensities.

Finite size, disorder and strain broaden peaks; powder averaging loses orientation information. Multiple structures can share limited peak positions, so identification needs intensity and chemical/context constraints. A unit cell choice is not a unique physical atom arrangement by itself.

Real-space defects and nonperiodic materials require correlation-based descriptions beyond a perfect infinite lattice. Reciprocal-space patterns are transformed, response-weighted observations rather than literal photographs of point atoms.


### Derivation and transfer

Bragg diffraction requires 2d sinθ=nλ, with θ the angle to lattice planes in the stated convention. A detector scattering angle is often 2θ, so confusing the two produces an incorrect lattice spacing. Structure factors determine which geometrically possible peaks have intensity.

The display follows the first-order wavelength satisfying the condition. It is not a complete crystal diffraction pattern or a proof that every reciprocal-lattice point is allowed.

</LessonSection>
