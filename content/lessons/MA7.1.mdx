<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Gamma and beta integrals appear in normalized distributions, dimensional integrals and radiation calculations. Their domains and normalization determine which formal manipulations are valid.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A weighted area under an exponential curve interpolates factorial values. A change of variables separates a two-variable positive integral into an overall scale and a fractional share.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For Re z>0 define Γ(z)=∫₀∞tᶻ⁻¹e⁻ᵗdt using the real logarithm of positive t. For Re a,Re b>0 define B(a,b)=∫₀¹tᵃ⁻¹(1−t)ᵇ⁻¹dt.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Near zero the gamma integrand behaves as tᶻ⁻¹, so Re z>0 controls convergence there; the exponential controls the positive-real tail. Integration by parts, with vanishing endpoint terms in this half-plane, yields $\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)$. Since Γ(1)=1, Γ(n+1)=n! for nonnegative integers n. Thus Γ(n)=(n−1)!, not n!.

The Gaussian integral follows by squaring ∫e⁻ˣ²dx on the line and using polar coordinates in the plane. It gives $\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi$ after t=x² on the positive half-line. Recurrence then gives Γ(3/2)=√π/2 and Γ(5/2)=3√π/4. Justify changes of order for these nonnegative or absolutely integrable expressions; not every oscillatory double integral permits the same interchange.

For Re a,Re b>0, write Γ(a)Γ(b) as a double integral on positive variables x,y. Set r=x+y and t=x/(x+y); the Jacobian is r. The integrand factors into rᵃ⁺ᵇ⁻¹e⁻ʳ and tᵃ⁻¹(1−t)ᵇ⁻¹. Therefore $B(a,b)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$. For a,b positive real, this also supplies the normalization of a beta density. Outside those original integral domains, a continued formula need not equal a convergent defining integral.

Recurrence extends Γ meromorphically with simple poles at zero and the negative integers; it does not make the original positive-axis integral converge there. The reflection identity $\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)$ holds away from integer singularities with the usual meromorphic interpretation. Near an integer where individual factors are singular, evaluate a justified limit rather than substituting an undefined infinity into algebra.

For large positive x, Stirling’s leading form is $\Gamma(x+1)\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}(x/e)^x$. The ratio tends to one, not equality at finite x. Logarithmic evaluation avoids overflow for large factorials; log Γ is often the appropriate numerical object. Asymptotic corrections require a stated regime and remainder control. A symbolic extension of a normalization constant does not guarantee a positive, integrable probability density outside its parameter domain.


### Derivation and transfer

Integration by parts gives Γ(x+1)=xΓ(x) for positive x, with Γ(1)=1. Consequently Γ(n)=(n−1)! for positive integers, not n!. The beta integral gives B(p,q)=Γ(p)Γ(q)/Γ(p+q) when both real arguments are positive.

Endpoint convergence is a hypothesis, not a consequence of manipulating a factorial symbol. The display integrates the beta density shape only as an unnormalized function on a closed interior interval. It does not perform analytic continuation or evaluate singular endpoint values.

</LessonSection>
