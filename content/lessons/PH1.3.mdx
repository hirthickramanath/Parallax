<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Central-force dynamics connects conservation laws to conic orbits and stability. It also shows which Keplerian conclusions fail when the force differs from inverse square.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A central force exerts no torque about its centre. The position and velocity stay in a plane perpendicular to the fixed angular-momentum vector when that vector is nonzero.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For two-body relative motion in a central potential, use reduced mass μ and conserved angular momentum L. The radial coordinate moves in an effective potential combining physical attraction and an angular-momentum barrier.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a central force $\mathbf F=F(r)\hat r$, torque r×F is zero, so L=r×μv is constant. For L≠0, motion lies in the plane perpendicular to L and $L=\mu r^2\dot\theta$. For L=0, the planar-angle reduction is singular; radial motion must be treated separately. Equal areas in equal times follow from constant r²θ̇ and require a central force, not specifically an inverse-square force.

For a time-independent central potential U(r), energy is

$$E=\frac{\mu\dot r^2}{2}+U_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad U_{\rm eff}=U(r)+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.$$

A circular orbit occurs at a stationary effective potential. A local minimum is radially stable for fixed L; a maximum is unstable. The angular barrier is not a new physical repulsive interaction—it results from eliminating angular motion using its conserved momentum.

For inverse-square gravitational relative motion, let $k=G(M+m)$ and specific angular momentum $\ell=r^2\dot\theta$. With u=1/r, the radial equation gives $u''+u=k/\ell^2$, where primes denote θ derivatives. Thus $r=p/[1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_0)]$ with p=ℓ²/k. The orbit is a conic on its allowed radial domain.

Specific energy ε=v²/2−k/r satisfies $e^2=1+2\varepsilon\ell^2/k^2$. Negative energy gives a bound ellipse for a noncollisional nonzero-angular-momentum orbit; zero energy gives the parabolic escape boundary; positive energy a hyperbola. For an ellipse ε=−k/(2a) and $T^2=4\pi^2a^3/k$. The circular case has e=0 and is a special ellipse.

Perturbing the force law can preserve planarity and area rate while changing closedness of the orbit. Extra bodies, dissipation or time dependence can break the simplified conserved quantities. Do not assign a two-body eccentricity a constant physical meaning in a strongly perturbed trajectory without defining an osculating approximation.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

For nonradial inverse-square motion with $r>0$ and nonzero $h$, specific angular momentum $h=r^2\dot\theta$ is constant. Set $u=1/r$ and use $d/dt=(h/r^2)d/d\theta$ in the radial equation to obtain $u''+u=\mu/h^2$. Its solution is $u=(\mu/h^2)[1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_0)]$. The conic geometry therefore follows from the force law, not from the area law alone.

Combining the orbit with $\varepsilon=\dot r^2/2+h^2/(2r^2)-\mu/r$ gives $e^2=1+2\varepsilon h^2/\mu^2$, $a=-\mu/(2\varepsilon)$ for bound orbits, and $h^2=\mu a(1-e^2)$. At an apsis $\dot r=0$; radial acceleration distinguishes a minimum from a maximum radius. At fixed radius, a tangential speed between circular and escape gives positive radial acceleration and a new elliptical periapsis. Perturbations, extended mass distributions and relativistic corrections require revisiting these identities.

</LessonSection>
