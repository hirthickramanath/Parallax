Can a set of functions be a vector space even though its elements are not arrows? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Linear algebra organizes objects by their operations, not by their appearance. Functions, polynomial coefficients, quantum states and numerical signals can share the same structural rules.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A vector space is a setting where weighted addition is consistent. A basis supplies independent building blocks, and coordinates describe an object relative to those chosen blocks.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A vector space over a specified field has vector addition and scalar multiplication obeying the vector-space axioms. A subspace inherits these operations and is closed under them.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A subset W of a vector space V is a subspace exactly when it is nonempty and closed under every linear combination αu+βv of its members. This ensures zero and additive inverses are present. The plane x+y+z=0 in R³ is a subspace; x+y+z=1 is an affine plane, not a subspace, because it excludes zero.

The span of a collection is the set of its finite linear combinations and is the smallest subspace containing it. A finite collection is independent if a zero linear combination forces every coefficient to be zero. A basis is independent and spans. Independence gives uniqueness of coordinates; spanning gives their existence.

The exchange principle says an independent list can replace members of a spanning list without losing spanning. Repeated replacement shows every independent list in a space spanned by n vectors has length at most n. Consequently finite bases have the same size, called dimension. This is a theorem, not a property inferred by looking at arrows in R³.

Polynomials of degree at most n form a space of dimension n+1, with basis 1,x,...,xⁿ. Polynomials of degree exactly n do not form a subspace: leading coefficients can cancel. Continuous real functions on [0,1] form an infinite-dimensional vector space since arbitrarily long lists of monomials are independent.

A sum U+W is a subspace, while a union U∪W need not be: vectors drawn from different pieces may add outside the union. For finite-dimensional subspaces, $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$. A direct sum has trivial intersection and unique decomposition. Always specify the scalar field: a complex vector space can have a different dimension when regarded over the reals.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Test closure for homogeneous planes, shifted planes, fixed-degree polynomials and two intersecting lines. For each accepted space, give a basis and prove both independence and spanning. A few randomly sampled successful sums do not prove closure for all scalars.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

The vectors (1,−1,0) and (0,1,−1) are independent and span x+y+z=0. For (a,b,c) with a+b+c=0, choosing coefficients a and −c gives (a,−a−c,c)=(a,b,c). Thus the subspace has dimension 2.

</LessonSection>
