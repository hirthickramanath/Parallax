<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Existence results in optimization depend on both the function and its domain. Compactness is the mechanism that prevents maximizing inputs from escaping a limit point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A continuous function carries nearby inputs to nearby outputs. A compact domain keeps a convergent subsequence inside the allowed set, preventing a boundary value from being lost.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Continuity at x means every output tolerance has an input tolerance valid for all admitted nearby inputs. In the real line, compact sets are exactly closed bounded sets.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The ε–δ definition states: for every ε>0 there exists δ>0 such that admitted x with $|x-a|<\delta$ satisfy $|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon$. Equivalently, for every sequence xₙ in the domain converging to a, f(xₙ)→f(a). One direction applies δ to the sequence tail; the other constructs a violating sequence with |xₙ−a|&lt;1/n if continuity fails.

A continuous function on a nonempty compact real set is bounded and attains its supremum and infimum. For boundedness, an unbounded sequence of output magnitudes would correspond to inputs having a convergent subsequence inside the set; continuity then forces finite output convergence, a contradiction. For attainment, choose inputs with values approaching the supremum, take a convergent subsequence and apply continuity. The nonempty hypothesis matters.

A continuous function on a closed interval [a,b] has every intermediate value between its endpoint values. A proof uses the supremum of points remaining on one side of the proposed value and continuity to exclude a jump. A continuous function on a disconnected compact set need not take intermediate values; compactness alone is not connectedness.

Continuity on a compact set is uniform: one δ can serve all points for a chosen ε. Otherwise find xₙ,yₙ in the set with |xₙ−yₙ|→0 yet output differences at least a fixed ε. A convergent subsequence xₙₖ→a forces yₙₖ→a too; continuity contradicts that fixed separation.

On an open or unbounded set these conclusions may fail. The function x on (0,1) attains neither boundary extreme. The function 1/x on (0,1) is continuous but unbounded. Uniform continuity also needs care: x² is continuous on the real line but not uniformly continuous there because arbitrarily small changes at large x can have a fixed-sized output effect.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

For a continuous function on a compact nonempty real set, boundedness and sequential compactness produce an attained extremum. Take a sequence whose values approach the infimum; a subsequence converges to a point in the domain, and continuity transfers the limiting value to that point. An open interval can exclude precisely the needed endpoint, as $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ demonstrates.

To prove uniform continuity by contradiction, suppose one fixed $\varepsilon_0>0$ persists while pairs $x_n,y_n$ approach one another with $|x_n-y_n|<1/n$. Compactness gives a convergent subsequence of $x_n$; the corresponding $y_n$ converge to the same domain point. Continuity forces both image sequences to the same limit, contradicting their separation by $\varepsilon_0$. The single uniform tail choice is the key extra content beyond pointwise continuity.


There is also an inverse-continuity consequence. If $f$ is continuous and injective on compact $K$, a closed subset $F$ of $K$ is compact, and $f(F)$ is compact and therefore closed in $\mathbb R$. As a preimage under the inverse, $f(F)$ is closed in $f(K)$. This closed-set criterion proves that the inverse on the image is continuous; injectivity alone would not supply this argument.

</LessonSection>
