<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Magnetostatics connects steady currents with magnetic circulation. Biot–Savart and Ampère methods share a regime of validity but offer different computational advantages.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A magnetic field forms circulating patterns around currents. Net closed-surface magnetic flux remains zero; circulation and outward flux are different integrals.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For stationary currents in vacuum, ∇×B=μ₀J and ∇·B=0. Biot–Savart reconstructs B from a steady localized current distribution under appropriate boundary conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a steady thin-wire current, $dB=\mu_0I\,dl'\times(r-r')/[4\pi|r-r'|^3]$. Integrating the actual wire geometry gives its field away from the idealized source. For an extended current density, replace I dl′ by J(r′)dV′. A steady conserved current must satisfy ∇·J=0, including return paths or a suitable ideal infinite geometry.

Ampère's integral law is $\oint B\cdot dl=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}$ for the stationary vacuum case. Symmetry around an ideal infinite straight wire makes B tangential with constant magnitude on a centred circle, giving B=μ₀I/(2πs). Merely drawing a circular loop around arbitrary currents does not impose that symmetry.

For a circular loop of radius R, contributions perpendicular to its axis cancel and Biot–Savart gives the axial field $B_z=\mu_0IR^2/[2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$. At the centre this is μ₀I/(2R); far along the axis it has dipole scaling. The current sense sets the sign through the right-hand convention.

A long ideal solenoid gives approximately B=μ₀nI inside and a much smaller exterior field away from ends, with n turns per length. Finite ends and winding geometry matter; the exterior is not exactly zero for every real coil. Fields in magnetic materials require an additional constitutive and bound-current description.

Time-varying fields require Maxwell's displacement-current term and cannot generally be reduced to the stationary Ampère law. A charging capacitor is a classic warning: current through a wire and changing electric flux through a different spanning surface must be treated consistently. Use magnetostatic formulas only when the timescale and geometry support the approximation.


### Derive, test and transfer

For an infinite straight z-directed current in vacuum, cylindrical symmetry and Ampere's law give Bφ=μ₀I/(2πr). The right-hand rule fixes the circulation sign; reversing I reverses the entire vector field. The distance dependence differs from the inverse-square electric field of an isolated point charge.

A field probe samples the magnetic field, not the magnetic force without a specified test velocity. That force is q v cross B and vanishes for a stationary test charge in this magnetic-only model. A finite or bent wire has end/geometry effects, and a time-dependent situation requires the full Maxwell–Ampere law. Symmetry is what makes dividing a loop integral by circumference legitimate.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In units with μ₀/(2π)=1, an infinite wire carrying I=3 at distance s=2 has field magnitude 1.5. Doubling s halves that field. This differs from the inverse-square magnitude of a point electric charge and the far-field inverse-cube dipole scale.
