<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

An open problem is a precisely stated unresolved question; proposed explanations must be evaluated against established constraints and controlled predictions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Examples span dark matter, quantum gravity, strong correlations and nonequilibrium complexity, but each has different evidence and scale requirements. A dimensional estimate such as $t\sim L/v$ supplies a conditional scale, not proof of a detailed mechanism.

Separate robust observations, model-dependent inferences, established mathematical results and conjectures. An apparent anomaly can arise from calibration, selection or an inadequate approximation; it can also motivate a new model. Neither automatic dismissal nor automatic discovery is justified.

Evaluate a proposal by internal consistency, recovery of tested limits, explicit predictions and comparison with alternatives. A flexible model fitting one dataset may add parameters without resolving independent constraints. Falsifiability depends on specified observable regimes and uncertainty, not a rhetorical promise.

Maintain exact dated references and do not invent current experimental status. The course’s draft introductions and automated rendering checks do not make a student an independently certified researcher. A responsible next step is a bounded reproducible project with a real review process and clearly unresolved source/science checks.


### Derivation and transfer

An open problem is not resolved by listing an attractive dimensional relation or fitting an unvalidated toy. A proposed explanation needs a well-defined model, predictions that differ from alternatives and evidence assessed with appropriate uncertainties.

The display shows a standardized discrepancy for a declared model-data difference. It is not evidence for a particular new theory and does not solve dark matter, quantum gravity or other frontiers. Multiple testing, selection, model flexibility and correlated errors can change the interpretation.

</LessonSection>
