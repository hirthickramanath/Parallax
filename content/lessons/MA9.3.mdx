<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Noether’s theorem connects continuous variational symmetries with conservation laws. It depends on the action, boundary term and equations of motion, not merely on a visually symmetric diagram.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A continuous transformation shifts a path into a neighbouring one. If the action change is only a boundary contribution, its first variation can be expressed as a conserved current along a stationary path.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For first-order L(t,q,q′), consider an infinitesimal point transformation δt=ετ and δq=εη. Allow the action density to change by a total derivative ε dB/dt.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Define canonical momentum p=Lq′ and H=p·q′−L. The induced variation at fixed coordinate time is $\xi=\eta-q'\tau$. Integration by parts of the action variation separates the Euler–Lagrange residual and a boundary expression. If the transformation is a variational symmetry up to total derivative B, then on a solution

$$J=p\cdot\eta-H\tau-B$$

satisfies dJ/dt=0. The formula requires the stated transformation convention; changing active/passive signs changes the displayed charge by a corresponding sign. A conserved quantity multiplied by −1 is the same conservation statement.

For a translation of coordinate qj with τ=0, ηj=1 and B=0, invariance gives conservation of pj. For time translation with τ=1, η=0 and no explicit time dependence, J=−H, giving energy conservation in that variational description. The absence of explicit time in L should be checked in the chosen coordinates; a time-dependent transformation can alter the apparent Hamiltonian.

For a rotationally invariant particle Lagrangian, η=a×q with constant infinitesimal axis a yields J=p·(a×q)=a·(q×p). Since the axis is arbitrary, angular momentum is conserved. Translational invariance likewise concerns the full interacting system and its boundaries, not an open subsystem exchanging momentum with omitted degrees of freedom.

A symmetry of the differential equations need not be a symmetry of the action, and the simple Noether charge does not follow automatically in that case. Gauge symmetries and field theories introduce further identities and local-current structure beyond this finite-coordinate statement. Discrete symmetries do not generate the same infinitesimal conservation law through this theorem.

The converse question—whether a proposed conserved quantity arises from a variational symmetry—requires the appropriate mathematical setting. In applications, verify the change of Ldt and its boundary term explicitly, then differentiate the proposed charge using the equations of motion as an independent check.


### Derivation and transfer

For a continuous symmetry of an action, a suitable boundary-term variation gives a conserved Noether charge on solutions. Time translation for an autonomous regular Lagrangian yields energy; coordinate translation yields its conjugate momentum when the Lagrangian is invariant.

A visually symmetric trajectory is not enough: the action and allowed transformations matter. The display checks energy along a harmonic solution q=Acos(ωt), q′=−Aωsin(ωt). Its constant value illustrates a charge after solving the motion, rather than proving every system with an attractive-looking orbit conserves that same quantity.

</LessonSection>
