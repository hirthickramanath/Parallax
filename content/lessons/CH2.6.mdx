<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

NMR addresses nuclear-spin transitions; EPR addresses suitable unpaired-electron magnetic states. Resonance and shifts require a reference and angular/cyclic frequency convention.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a simple spin in a field, $\omega_L=|\gamma|B$ in an angular-frequency convention. Electronic shielding changes the local nuclear response; a chemical shift is usually a referenced dimensionless frequency difference reported in ppm. Different sign/reference conventions must be checked.

Spin–spin couplings split levels and correlate resonances. Chemical equivalence, exchange and strong-coupling regimes can change simple line-counting rules. EPR g factors and hyperfine couplings encode electronic environment and anisotropy, not one universal isotropic number for every radical.

Relaxation times T₁ and T₂ describe different population/coherence processes in qualified models; inhomogeneous broadening adds T₂* behaviour. Pulse sequence, temperature and motion determine what spectrum/echo is measured. A width cannot be converted into a unique chemical lifetime without a line-shape model.

Compare assignments through calibration, field dependence and multiple observables. A predicted chemical shift is a model output with method/environment error, not an exact count of local electrons. This lesson is theoretical and supplies no new measured resonance dataset.


### Derive, test and transfer

Magnetic-resonance angular frequency is ω=|γ|B for a simple isolated spin in a uniform field, while ordinary frequency is ω/(2π). Quoting a gyromagnetic ratio requires stating whether it is angular or cyclic-frequency units; omitting that convention creates a factor-of-2π error.

Chemical shifts compare frequencies relative to a reference, typically in parts per million. The reference frequency and sign convention must be supplied. Shielding, spin coupling, relaxation and anisotropy add structure beyond a single Larmor line; NMR and EPR involve different magnetic moments and experimental scales. The display is a clock relation, not a complete multiplet or relaxation simulation.

</LessonSection>
