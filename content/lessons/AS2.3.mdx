Why can an opaque cloud appear bright instead of simply blocking the background? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Radiation traveling through matter is both removed from and added to a ray. Optical depth tracks attenuation, while the source function determines what the medium replaces it with.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A slab partly erases the incoming image and contributes its own brightness. At large optical depth a constant-source slab hides the background and approaches its own source function.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For nonnegative extinction coefficient αν and emissivity jν under a stated ray convention, transfer is dIν/ds=−ανIν+jν. Optical depth increases along the propagation direction as dτν=ανds.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For αν>0, define source function Sν=jν/αν. The transfer equation becomes $dI_\nu/d\tau_\nu=-I_\nu+S_\nu$. An integrating factor gives the formal solution

$$I(\tau)=I(0)e^{-\tau}+\int_0^\tau S(t)e^{-(\tau-t)}dt.$$

For a constant source function, $I_{\rm out}=I_{\rm in}e^{-\tau}+S(1-e^{-\tau})$. For pure absorption S=0 this reduces to exponential attenuation. The formula assumes the stated one-dimensional ray treatment and constant S for the simplified slab.

If τ≪1, the first-order result is Iout≈Iin+(S−Iin)τ. A thin layer may brighten or dim the ray depending on its source function relative to the incident intensity. If τ≫1, the constant-source solution approaches S and loses memory of the background. “Optically thick” therefore does not mean black in every observing band.

With mass extinction opacity κν, $\tau_\nu=\int\kappa_\nu\rho\,ds$, dimensionless. Opacity usually depends on frequency, composition and state, so one cloud can be thin in one band and thick in another. Extinction includes removal by scattering as well as absorption, while emissivity can include scattering into the ray.

For true thermal absorption/emission in local thermodynamic equilibrium, the corresponding thermal source function is Bν(T). With scattering, the total source generally also depends on the angular radiation field; LTE matter alone does not make every total scattering source equal to Planck intensity. Population inversions can produce effective negative absorption and amplification, outside this nonnegative-extinction slab bench.

A brightness measurement at one frequency can be degenerate between source function and optical depth. Multiple bands, line ratios, geometry or independent temperature information help break it. The slab formula does not by itself solve an atmosphere whose temperature, opacity and angular field vary with depth.


### Derive, test and transfer

Write absorption and emission along one ray as $dI/ds=-\alpha I+j$. For optical depth increasing along the path, $d\tau=\alpha ds$ and $dI/d\tau=-I+S$, with $S=j/\alpha$. Multiplication by the integrating factor eτ yields $I(\tau)=I_0e^{-\tau}+\int_0^\tau S(t)e^{-(\tau-t)}dt$. A constant source function reduces the integral to $S(1-e^{-\tau})$.

An optically thick slab approaches its source function rather than becoming arbitrarily bright. An optically thin expansion gives $I\simeq I_0+(S-I_0)\tau$, making the sign of an emission or absorption contrast explicit. LTE can identify the thermal source function with a Planck function for the relevant process; scattering and non-LTE populations can invalidate that identification. A single-ray scalar model does not solve a full angular scattering field.

</LessonSection>
