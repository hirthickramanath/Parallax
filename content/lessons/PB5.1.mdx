<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Photoelectric thresholds challenged a purely classical energy-delivery picture. Atomic line spectra independently showed that electron energies could not vary continuously in a bound atom.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

Increasing intensity sends more photons in the simple model; increasing frequency raises each photon’s energy. These are different experimental controls.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

A photon has energy hf. In the elementary photoelectric model, the maximum emitted electron kinetic energy is hf−φ when hf≥φ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For work function φ, energy conservation gives $K_{\max}=hf-\phi$. The threshold frequency is φ/h; below threshold the elementary single-photon model predicts no emission regardless of intensity. At fixed above-threshold frequency, intensity mainly changes the number of emitted electrons. Strong-field multiphoton effects lie outside this model.

A stopping potential obeys $eV_s=K_{\max}$. Plotting Vs against frequency yields slope h/e and intercept −φ/e if contact potentials, surface preparation and instrumental offsets are handled. The relation involves maximum electron energy, not the mean energy of all emitted electrons.

Bohr's hydrogen model combines Coulomb attraction with the quantization $mvr=n\hbar$ for a fixed-nucleus nonrelativistic approximation. It gives $r_n=a_0n^2/Z$ and $E_n=-13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ eV approximately. Reduced mass corrects the electron-mass approximation. Emission between bound states satisfies $hf=E_{\rm high}-E_{\rm low}$.

The Bohr orbit is a historically useful calculation, not a literal electron trajectory in modern quantum mechanics. A wavefunction gives state amplitudes; measured probabilities follow its squared magnitude. The model fails for many-electron interactions and does not explain all intensities or fine structure. A hydrogenic formula applies to one-electron ions with further relativistic corrections at large Z.

The de Broglie relation λ=h/p links matter wavelength to momentum. It does not imply that a macroscopic object has a visibly resolvable wave pattern; preparation, coherence and environmental interaction matter. Threshold and spectral experiments motivate a theory richer than either tiny classical balls or classical waves alone.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

Increasing intensity at fixed frequency increases the photon arrival rate, not $hf$. The stopping potential measures the maximum electron energy rather than the average emitted energy. In the Bohr spectrum, bound levels are negative relative to ionization; excitation energies are differences between levels, not their absolute magnitudes.

### Derivation and transfer

The photoelectric stopping potential follows eVs=hf−Φ for photons above the threshold. Increasing intensity at fixed frequency chiefly changes the number of emitted electrons in the simple single-photon regime, not their maximum kinetic energy.

The display uses h=e=1 and a nonnegative energy excess. Below threshold its zero output means no emission in this model, not an emitted electron with a measured zero kinetic energy. Multiphoton and material-surface effects require additional assumptions.

</LessonSection>
