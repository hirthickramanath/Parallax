Formal electron counting assigns an oxidation state and a model d count, while actual electronic populations depend on covalency and state.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a common neutral-valence bookkeeping scheme, the nominal d count is group number minus oxidation state, $n_d=g-q$. This rule needs the relevant valence/configuration convention and does not measure local electron density directly.

Ligand geometry splits d-like levels, while spin pairing, exchange and covalent mixing affect occupations. The same nominal d count can produce different spin, spectra and reactivity. Octahedral and square-planar models cannot use identical level diagrams.

Metal-to-ligand backbonding and ligand-to-metal donation involve symmetry and energy matching. Redox can be ligand-centred rather than purely metal-centred in noninnocent systems. A formal oxidation-state change does not uniquely localize where electron density moved.

Compare spectroscopic, magnetic and structural evidence under stated solvent/temperature conditions. A simple electron-count heuristic can organize a hypothesis but not replace kinetic or thermodynamic analysis of a reaction.


### Derive, test and transfer

Formal oxidation state follows an agreed electron-allocation convention; for a complex with n singly negative ligands and other neutral ligands, metal oxidation number equals total charge plus n. This bookkeeping is useful without making oxidation state identical to an experimentally partitioned atomic charge.

Coordination number, geometry and ligand identity influence orbital splitting and reactivity. A formal d count then uses group and oxidation conventions, but strong covalency and redox-active ligands can complicate a simple ionic assignment. The display checks charge balance only and does not certify that every chosen formal composition is a stable real compound.

</LessonSection>
