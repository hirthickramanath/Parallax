<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Galaxy morphology describes light distribution and dynamical components; empirical scaling relations connect conditional population properties with intrinsic scatter.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Discs, bars, bulges and spheroids are structural components, while spiral arms can be density/kinematic patterns rather than material features that rotate rigidly with every star. Viewing inclination, bandpass and dust alter appearance. A red disc and a blue compact galaxy illustrate why colour alone is not morphology.

Tully–Fisher-like relations connect rotation and luminosity or baryonic mass under a defined sample/calibration. Faber–Jackson and fundamental-plane relations concern pressure-supported systems with size, brightness and dispersion. A schematic $L\propto\sigma^4$ is a fit regime, not an exact gravitational law for every spheroid.

The virial scaling M∼Rσ²/G motivates some relations but stellar populations, dark matter, nonhomology and measurement definitions create tilt and scatter. Fitting must handle errors, selection and which variable is regressed on which. Intrinsic scatter and measurement scatter are not identical.

Morphological transformations can involve mergers, secular processes and environmental effects. A cross-sectional sequence is not automatically a movie of one object through time. Use resolved kinematics, populations and context rather than assigning one unique formation history from a single image.


### Derive, test and transfer

A log-linear relation $\log L=\alpha\log v+C$ is equivalent to $L\propto v^\alpha$ within its calibrated regime. Doubling v changes predicted L by $2^\alpha$, so both the slope and its uncertainty matter. Scatter can be intrinsic or measurement-driven and is not eliminated by drawing a best-fit line.

Galaxy morphology, gas content, stellar population and wavelength band influence the chosen observable. A stellar-luminosity relation and a baryonic-mass relation are not interchangeable. Selection thresholds and correlated distance errors can bias fitted slopes. The investigation is a controlled power-law illustration and does not present a new measured scaling relation or a universal physical law.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In a supplied L∝σ⁴ fit regime, doubling σ multiplies predicted L by 16. If a stellar population’s mass-to-light ratio doubles at unchanged stellar mass, luminosity halves instead. Dynamics and population evolution therefore cannot be conflated.
