<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why does a transition-state estimate need a correction for trajectories that cross back? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Transition-state theory converts a dividing-surface population into a rate estimate. Its assumptions expose why a free-energy barrier is useful without making every barrier-crossing event a completed reaction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Imagine counting crossings of a surface separating reactants and products. Counting every forward crossing as success overestimates net conversion when trajectories recross.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Transition-state theory uses a chosen dividing surface, an equilibrium-like population estimate near it, and a flux calculation. A transmission coefficient accounts for dynamical corrections within the adopted formulation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a suitably defined unimolecular standard-state free-energy barrier, the Eyring form is $k=\kappa(k_BT/h)\exp[-\Delta G^\ddagger/(RT)]$. The prefactor has inverse-time units. Bimolecular rates require the appropriate concentration or standard-state factor; copying this expression without its molecularity convention gives incorrect units.

Writing ΔG‡=ΔH‡−TΔS‡ makes $\ln(k/T)=\ln(\kappa k_B/h)+\Delta S^\ddagger/R-\Delta H^\ddagger/(RT)$ when these terms are effectively constant over the fitting interval. Temperature-dependent heat capacities, tunneling or a changing transmission coefficient can spoil a simple straight-line interpretation.

Classical no-recrossing transition-state theory provides a reference flux estimate. Recrossing reduces the fraction that commits to product. Quantum tunneling can enhance a rate relative to a purely classical barrier model, and a combined empirical κ can encode more than one effect; do not assign it a universal range without defining which reference theory it corrects.

A reaction coordinate is a model of progress, not necessarily one bond distance. Solvent and internal motions can influence whether it separates reactant and product well. The committor probability offers a dynamical perspective: configurations near a good transition region have intermediate probabilities of reaching either basin first.

Rate predictions need both the free-energy landscape and dynamics. Raising pressure or changing solvent can modify friction, populations and pathways. A computed barrier in vacuum is not automatically the activation free energy for a solvated reaction. Validate against temperature trends, isotope effects and other discriminating observables.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Compare synthetic rates for two barriers differing by a known multiple of RT. Draw an ideal dividing surface and trajectories that commit or recross, recording what the flux count assumes. Keep a barrier estimate separate from a full dynamical validation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If all other Eyring factors stay fixed and a barrier rises by RT ln10, the rate falls by a factor of ten. If a recrossing-only correction changes from 1 to 0.5 under the same reference model, the predicted rate halves. Neither example measures an actual molecular barrier.

</LessonSection>
