<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Conductors rearrange mobile charge until electrostatic equilibrium is reached. Capacitance describes how geometry and materials connect potential differences with stored charge, not an intrinsic amount of charge a component must contain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A conductor is an equipotential volume at equilibrium. Surface charges adjust to enforce that condition, and empty cavities can acquire their own boundary-value solution.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

An ideal conductor at electrostatic equilibrium has E=0 in its bulk and constant potential on each connected piece. Capacitance relates a specified charge arrangement to its potential difference.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If a static internal electric field remained in a perfect conductor, mobile charge would continue to move. Equilibrium therefore requires zero bulk field. A small pillbox crossing its vacuum surface gives the outward normal field just outside as σ/ε₀, while the tangential electrostatic field is zero. This is an equilibrium idealization, not the resistive electric field within a current-carrying wire.

An empty enclosed cavity has constant boundary potential. By Laplace uniqueness its interior potential is the same constant and its field is zero, assuming no internal charges and a complete ideal conducting boundary. Charges inserted into a cavity induce an inner-surface charge and change that conclusion. Holes, time-dependent fields and finite conductivity need further treatment.

For an isolated conducting sphere of radius R in vacuum with reference V∞=0, V=kQ/R and $C=Q/V=4\pi\epsilon_0R$. For concentric spherical conductors with radii a&lt;b carrying ±Q, integrating the gap field gives $C=4\pi\epsilon_0ab/(b-a)$. A single conductor’s capacitance relative to infinity differs from the two-conductor capacitance of a device.

For a linear fixed capacitor, assembly energy is $U=\int_0^Qq/C\,dq=Q^2/(2C)=CV^2/2$. Field-energy integration ε₀∫E²dV/2 gives the same isolated-system result. With an attached voltage source, work and charge exchanged with that source matter when geometry changes; minimizing capacitor field energy alone can predict a wrong mechanical force.

Capacitors in series share equal-magnitude intermediate-node charges under the standard initially uncharged isolated-node setup, while parallel capacitors share voltage. Precharged floating nodes and stray capacitance can require additional charge-balance equations. A diagrammatic rule is not a substitute for the actual conductor and source constraints.


### Derive, test and transfer

For ideal parallel plates, uniform field E=V/d gives capacitance C=εA/d from Q=εEA. Stored field energy is Q²/(2C) or CV²/2, but these expressions must be used with the actual controlled variable. Doubling separation at fixed charge raises stored energy; at fixed voltage it lowers the capacitor's stored energy while the voltage source exchanges energy.

Electrostatic equilibrium makes a connected ideal conductor an equipotential, not a region with zero surface charge in every circumstance. Fringing and finite geometry alter the parallel-plate approximation. A mechanical-force or energy argument must include the battery when voltage is controlled; comparing only isolated field energies can give the wrong sign for work.

</LessonSection>
