<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A bounded linear operator satisfies ||Ax||≤C||x|| on its whole normed domain with finite C. An unbounded operator requires an explicitly specified domain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The operator norm is $\|A\|=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|$ when finite. In finite-dimensional normed spaces every linear operator is bounded, but this does not extend to all differential operators on infinite-dimensional function spaces.

On periodic smooth waves eⁱⁿˣ normalized in L², the derivative has norm growing as |n|, so it is unbounded in the L² norm. It can still be a meaningful densely defined operator on a Sobolev-type domain. Convergence of vectors alone need not imply convergence of their images.

A symmetric operator agrees with its adjoint pairing on its stated domain; self-adjointness additionally requires equality of domains and the adjoint operator. Boundary conditions enter integration by parts and can select different self-adjoint realizations of the same formal differential expression. A formal matrix of coefficients is insufficient in this setting.

Closedness and closability control limits of graph pairs (x,Ax). Quantum unitary-evolution theorems require self-adjoint generators under their hypotheses, not just an informally real energy expression. Commutators and uncertainty inequalities also need a common domain where the required products exist.


### Derivation and transfer

An operator is bounded when one constant C controls ||Tx||≤C||x|| on its whole domain. Finite matrix examples are bounded, but differentiation on an L²-based domain is not bounded in the L² norm: higher-frequency modes amplify its norm.

For exp(inx), differentiation multiplies by in, so the norm ratio is |n|. Truncating to finite n hides the unbounded limit. The display makes that sequence explicit; it does not assert differentiation is unbounded under every possible graph or Sobolev norm.

</LessonSection>
