<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Newtonian models become subtle when forces depend on velocity or a chosen system exchanges mass. A valid equation begins by defining the system boundary and reference frame, rather than applying a memorized derivative to an open subsystem.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A drag force responds to motion through a medium. A rocket changes its momentum partly by pushing exhaust away; the expelled material carries momentum across the chosen rocket boundary.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a fixed collection of particles in an inertial frame, the external force equals the time derivative of total momentum. An open system additionally requires momentum flux across its boundary.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For constant mass m falling downward through a stationary medium with linear drag coefficient c>0, choose downward v positive. The equation is $m\dot v=mg-cv$. With τ=m/c and terminal speed $v_t=mg/c$, the solution is $v(t)=v_t+(v_0-v_t)e^{-t/\tau}$. Integrating gives $y(t)-y_0=v_tt+(v_0-v_t)\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})$. Initial conditions and the c→0 limit recover the appropriate vacuum motion. The formula assumes constant g, mass and linear drag; a quadratic law needs a different solution.

For an ideal one-dimensional rocket, let m decrease and let expelled material have velocity u relative to the rocket. Comparing momentum of the rocket plus the just-expelled material over dt gives $m\dot v=F_{\rm ext}+u\dot m$ to first order. Since backward exhaust has u&lt;0 and mass loss has ṁ&lt;0, the thrust term is positive. Differentiating only the rocket's mv without accounting for outgoing material would assign the wrong flux.

With negligible external force and constant exhaust-speed magnitude ve=−u, integrate $dv=-v_e\,dm/m$ to obtain $v-v_0=v_e\ln(m_0/m)$. This logarithm compares two positive masses and is dimensionless. The equation assumes a classical nonrelativistic exhaust model, instantaneous relative exhaust speed and no pressure-thrust correction. Gravity and aerodynamic losses add trajectory-dependent terms.

Energy accounting must include the exhaust and the propulsion energy source. Conservation of momentum does not imply the rocket's kinetic energy is constant or that chemical energy converts entirely into vehicle kinetic energy. An open subsystem is not closed merely because its external aerodynamic force is zero.

For numerical work, test initial position and speed, terminal behaviour and the vacuum limit of the drag solution. Very small c can cause cancellation in 1−exp(−t/τ); a stable exponential-difference function or a series expansion can be needed even when the analytic expression is correct.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

With downward speed positive, the linear-drag equation is $m\dot v=mg-bv$. Subtract $v_\infty=mg/b$ to get $d(v-v_\infty)/dt=-(b/m)(v-v_\infty)$, hence $v=v_\infty+(v_0-v_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=m/b$. Terminal motion has balanced nonzero forces; it does not mean gravity disappeared. Quadratic drag has a different transient and must not be inserted into this exponential solution unchanged.

A rocket is an open subsystem. For backward exhaust speed $u>0$ relative to the rocket, conservation of momentum across an infinitesimal ejection gives $m\,dv=-u\,dm$ in the ideal force-free limit. Integrating gives $\Delta v=u\ln(m_0/m_1)$. The result uses a relative exhaust velocity and dimensionless mass ratio. Applying the closed-system formula to a changing rocket mass without a momentum-flux term changes the physical system and yields an incorrect equation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For m=2 kg, c=1 kg/s and g=10 m/s², τ=2 s and terminal speed is 20 m/s. Starting from rest gives v(2)=20(1−e⁻¹)≈12.64 m/s. For an ideal rocket with ve=1000 m/s and mass ratio 2, the vacuum velocity gain is 1000 ln2≈693.15 m/s.
