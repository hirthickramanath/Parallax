<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Physical parameters are often linked by conservation laws. Optimization must search only allowed changes; a direction that increases the objective may be forbidden by a constraint.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

At a regular constrained optimum the objective cannot rise along any tangent direction. Its gradient must therefore point normal to the constraint surface.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a smooth equality constraint g(x)=c with nonzero gradient, a differentiable local constrained extremum satisfies ∇f=λ∇g. The condition is necessary, not sufficient.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

At an unconstrained interior extremum of a differentiable scalar function, all directional derivatives vanish, hence ∇f=0. With a twice differentiable function, a positive-definite Hessian gives a strict local minimum and a negative-definite one a strict local maximum. An indefinite Hessian gives a saddle. Semidefinite cases need higher-order or other analysis: x⁴ and −x⁴ both have zero second derivative at zero but opposite extremum types.

For a regular constraint g=c, local tangent vectors v satisfy ∇g·v=0. Stationarity requires ∇f·v=0 for each such v, so $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ for some scalar λ. With several constraints whose gradients are independent, ∇f is a linear combination of their gradients. Independence is a constraint qualification; at a singular point the ordinary multiplier conclusion may fail.

Solve the stationarity equations together with the original constraints, then classify or compare all admissible candidates. A compact feasible set with a continuous objective guarantees existence of a global extreme, but the multiplier equations alone do not prove the found candidate is global. Boundary pieces need their own checks when the constraints include inequalities or an interval domain.

For f(x,y)=x+y on the unit circle, equations (1,1)=λ(2x,2y) give x=y and x²+y²=1. The two candidates are ±(1/√2,1/√2), giving ±√2. Compactness establishes attainment and comparison identifies the global extremes.

At the singular equality g(x)=x²=0, the only feasible point is x=0. The objective f(x)=x has an extremum on that singleton, yet f′=1 cannot equal λg′=0. This is a direct warning against using multiplier equations without regularity. Inequality constraints lead to sign and complementarity conditions in the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker framework; those require additional hypotheses beyond this equality lesson.


### Derive, test and transfer

On the regular constraint $x^2+y^2=1$, optimizing f=x+y gives $(1,1)=\lambda(2x,2y)$ and hence x=y. The two candidates yield ±√2; compactness of the circle ensures extrema exist and comparison identifies the global maximum/minimum. The multiplier equations alone produce candidates, not their classification.

Regularity matters: if the constraint gradient vanishes, its normal-space description can fail. The equation x²+y²=0 describes only the origin but has zero gradient there; for f=x the usual proportional-gradient equation has no solution even though the feasible singleton is an extremum. Inequality constraints additionally require boundary/active-set conditions rather than applying one unrestricted multiplier rule everywhere.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Maximize xy on x+y=10 with x,y≥0. Substitute y=10−x to get 10x−x² on [0,10]. Its derivative vanishes at x=5; the value is 25 and both boundary endpoints give zero. The negative second derivative and endpoint checks establish the global maximum.
