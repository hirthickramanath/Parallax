Why must a constrained variation preserve the constraint to first order? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Functional constraints include fixed total integrals and pointwise geometric restrictions. Their multipliers have different mathematical roles, and unconstrained variations can otherwise derive the wrong equation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A global integral condition restricts one total number. A pointwise condition restricts every location on the path, requiring a multiplier function rather than generally one constant.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a regular isoperimetric constraint ∫G(t,q,q′)dt=C, a stationary constrained path can satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation for L+λG with a constant multiplier λ under the standard qualification.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Let δS and δC be the first-variation linear functionals at a candidate. When δC is nonzero as a functional and the constrained stationarity condition holds, a multiplier combines them into δS+λδC=0 for every admitted endpoint-zero variation. This gives the Euler–Lagrange equation for L+λG together with the original integral constraint. The regularity qualification rules out singular cases where the constraint gradient vanishes.

For a pointwise holonomic restriction g(t,q)=0, use an augmented integrand $\widetilde L=L+\lambda(t)g(t,q)$ under the ordinary variational formulation. Varying λ gives the original constraint at every time; varying q gives the corresponding multiplier-force term. The multiplier is not a free parameter to choose after solving the motion; it is determined jointly with the trajectory and constraints.

For a length-constrained curve, using arc length as a functional may require handling its differentiability and endpoint data carefully. A constrained extremum still needs classification after its stationarity equation is found. Equality constraints differ from inequality obstacles, where active sets and complementarity can enter.

Velocity-dependent nonholonomic constraints are especially delicate. Imposing them directly inside a variational principle can produce vakonomic equations that differ from physical d'Alembert nonholonomic dynamics. One must specify the physical principle and the allowed virtual displacements, rather than assuming every constraint belongs in the same multiplier recipe.

A change to generalized coordinates can sometimes solve regular holonomic constraints explicitly, reducing the variational dimension. Coordinate singularities or dependent constraints can invalidate a local elimination. Check both the original constraint and admissible variation class after any reduction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For stationary ∫₀¹q′²/2 dt with q(0)=q(1)=0 and ∫₀¹q dt=1, use L=q′²/2+λq. Euler–Lagrange gives q″=λ. Endpoint conditions yield q=λ(t²−t)/2. Its integral is −λ/12, so λ=−12 and q=6t(1−t). The integral is indeed 1.

</LessonSection>
