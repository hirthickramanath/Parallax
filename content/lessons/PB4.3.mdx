<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Magnetic deflection is central to charged-particle instruments, plasmas and dynamos. Induction explains how a changing field configuration can transfer energy through an electric field.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

The magnetic part of the Lorentz force turns velocity sideways. A changing magnetic flux is associated with an electric circulation that can do work.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

The Lorentz force is q(E+v×B). Magnetic flux is the oriented integral of B through a surface; Faraday’s law relates its change to electric circulation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For motion perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field, $|F_B|=|q|vB$. Equating to mv²/r gives $r=mv/(|q|B)$ nonrelativistically. The sign of q determines curvature direction. Since $\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=0$, a static magnetic force alone does no work; a parallel velocity component produces a helix. Relativistic momentum replaces mv at high speed.

A straight current element experiences $d\mathbf F=I\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B$. For a long straight wire in vacuum, Ampère symmetry yields $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$. Finite geometry and material magnetization alter the simple result. Magnetic Gauss law gives zero net flux through a closed surface; field lines do not begin or end at isolated classical magnetic charges.

For a stationary loop, $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi_B/dt$. The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced response opposes the flux change, consistent with energy conservation. For a moving circuit the magnetic force on moving charges also contributes to the emf. Flux depends on area, orientation and field, not just field strength.

A coil with self-inductance L has induced emf −L dI/dt in the linear fixed-geometry model and stored energy LI²/2. In an ideal RL step with resistance R, current approaches its final value on timescale L/R. Interrupting current abruptly can require a large voltage; a laboratory demonstration needs a safe protected circuit.

Electrostatic potential alone cannot represent the induced electric field around a changing flux: the closed-loop electric circulation is nonzero. This distinction is needed before combining induction with the earlier conservative-field picture.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

Integrating the electric field around a stationary loop gives $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-d\Phi_B/dt$. In a changing magnetic field this nonzero circulation prevents a single electrostatic scalar potential from describing the entire induced field. For $N$ identical turns, flux linkage is $N\Phi_B$; confusing linkage with per-turn flux loses a factor of $N$. In an ideal series RL step, $L\,di/dt+Ri=V_0$ has solution $i=(V_0/R)(1-e^{-tR/L})$ for zero initial current. The time constant has units H/Ω = s.


### Derivation and transfer

For a long straight wire, magnetic field magnitude is μ0I/(2πr) with tangential direction from the right-hand rule. Faraday induction instead relates a loop emf to minus the rate of magnetic flux change, including orientation.

The display uses normalized μ0/(2π)=1 and a signed current. It is a wire-field slice, not a complete induction experiment. Changing current can require an electric field, but a static inverse-radius plot does not calculate the full transient.

</LessonSection>
