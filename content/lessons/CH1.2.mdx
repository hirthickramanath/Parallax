<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Phase equilibrium combines component potentials with composition and pressure. Mixture vapour and liquid can have different compositions, so a pure-substance shortcut can fail.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A volatile component partitions between phases until its thermodynamic price matches on both sides. Each phase can reach that balance at a different composition.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Coexisting phases in equilibrium share temperature, pressure in the appropriate mechanical model, and chemical potentials of components that can exchange.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For an ideal liquid solution and ideal gas vapour at low pressure, Raoult's law is $p_i=x_i p_i^*(T)$, where pi* is the pure-component saturation pressure at T. Total pressure is ∑pi and vapour mole fraction is yi=pi/ptotal. Vapour and liquid compositions are generally different. Nonideal mixtures require activities, fugacities or other validated models.

Henry's law describes a dilute solute with a convention-dependent proportionality such as pi=Hi xi. Its constant and units depend on the chosen concentration and standard-state convention. It should not be equated numerically with Raoult's pure-component pressure without checking the limit and definition.

For two phases of a pure substance, differentiating chemical-potential equality gives the Clapeyron equation $dp/dT=\Delta S/\Delta V=\Delta H/(T\Delta V)$. If vapour is ideal, liquid volume negligible and vaporization enthalpy approximately constant, integration gives ln(p₂/p₁)=−ΔH/R(1/T₂−1/T₁). Those approximations fail near a critical point or over a large range with varying enthalpy.

For a simple nonreacting equilibrium system, Gibbs phase rule F=C−P+2 counts independent intensive variables under its assumptions. Fixing pressure removes one degree of freedom. Chemical reactions, extra external fields or additional constraints change the count and must be included explicitly.

A phase diagram records stability boundaries, not a full transformation rate. Superheating, supercooling and nucleation barriers can delay a phase change. A measured transient temperature need not be an equilibrium boundary, and an azeotrope is a composition-dependent mixture feature rather than proof that all mixtures behave like pure substances.


### Derive, test and transfer

An ideal liquid solution with ideal vapor obeys a component partial pressure pi=xi pi*, so total pressure is the mole-fraction-weighted sum. A pressure–composition line therefore encodes both ideality and the chosen pure-component vapor pressures; it is not a general nonideal phase diagram.

For ideal mixing, entropy change is −RΣni ln xi, with xi ln xi interpreted by its zero limit for an absent component. Equality of chemical potential between phases supplies the actual equilibrium condition. Nonideal activities, azeotropes and composition-dependent excess Gibbs energy can invalidate a straight Raoult line. A curve at fixed temperature cannot silently predict all temperature-dependent phase boundaries.

</LessonSection>

For an ideal binary liquid with xA=0.25, pA*=80 and pB*=20 in shared pressure units, pA=20 and pB=15, so total pressure is 35. The vapour fraction yA=20/35=4/7, larger than the liquid fraction.
