<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Define B=∇×A and E=−∇φ−∂ₜA. Gauge transformations A→A+∇χ, φ→φ−∂ₜχ leave these fields unchanged for sufficiently regular χ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The potential representation satisfies ∇·B=0 and Faraday’s law identically in an appropriate local or topologically admissible domain. Global vector potentials can require patches when topology is nontrivial; writing one expression locally is not a proof of a single global chart.

In Lorenz gauge, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0$, Maxwell equations reduce to wave equations for φ and A with charge/current sources. Charge continuity is required for consistency. Gauge freedom persists in residual functions solving the homogeneous wave equation; fixing a gauge is not itself a measurement of a new physical field.

For localized sources in vacuum and retarded boundary conditions, $\phi(\mathbf r,t)=(4\pi\epsilon_0)^{-1}\int \rho(\mathbf r',t-R/c)/R\,d^3r'$ with R=|r−r′|, and A has μ₀/(4π) times the analogous current integral. Derivatives act on both the geometry and retarded time when obtaining fields. A homogeneous free-field solution must be included when imposed by initial conditions.

Coulomb gauge can feature an instantaneous-looking scalar potential, while the complete electric field remains causal after the vector-potential contribution is included. Potentials individually depend on gauge, but suitable phase/flux combinations can have observable significance in quantum theory. Do not infer a faster-than-light force from an isolated gauge-dependent term.


### Derive, test and transfer

For time-dependent potentials, E=−gradφ−∂A/∂t and B=curl A. The gauge transformation A→A+gradχ must accompany φ→φ−∂χ/∂t so both fields remain invariant. Transforming only A while freezing φ generally changes the electric field and is not the full gauge transformation.

Retarded solutions depend on source values at t−R/c rather than instantaneous distant source values. The differential equations also admit homogeneous radiation determined by initial/boundary data; a particular retarded source integral alone does not encode arbitrary incoming radiation. Causality and gauge convention must be tracked together when interpreting potentials.

</LessonSection>
