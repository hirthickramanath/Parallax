<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Pointing telescopes and reconstructing three-dimensional motion require more than a flat drawing. Coordinates describe a geometric situation relative to chosen axes; they are not the situation itself.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

A line has a starting point and one direction. A plane has a point and two independent directions, or equivalently a normal direction.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

In Euclidean 3D space a position vector locates a point relative to an origin; a displacement vector connects two points independently of that origin.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The distance between points A and B follows from three-dimensional Pythagoras: $|\mathbf B-\mathbf A|=\sqrt{(B_x-A_x)^2+(B_y-A_y)^2+(B_z-A_z)^2}$. A line through A with nonzero direction d is $\mathbf r=\mathbf A+t\mathbf d$ for real t. Rescaling d changes the parameter but not the line.

A plane with point A and nonzero normal n satisfies $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf A)=0$. The dot product vanishes for every displacement lying in the plane. The perpendicular distance of point P is $|\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf P-\mathbf A)|/|\mathbf n|$; omitting normalization makes a false dependence on the chosen normal magnitude.

For two lines, equate their vector expressions and solve three scalar equations in two parameters. Parallel directions need a separate coincidence test. Nonparallel lines may intersect, or may be skew: they lie in no common plane. A sketch on paper can hide this distinction.

In spherical coordinates with polar angle θ measured from positive z and azimuth φ from positive x, $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=r\cos\theta$. Geographic latitude is π/2−θ. State conventions before comparing coordinates. At the poles the azimuth is indeterminate even though the spatial point is well defined.

An orthonormal basis makes the Euclidean dot product a simple component sum. A change of orthonormal axes preserves lengths and angles, although numerical components change. In nonorthogonal coordinates a metric is needed; the component sum cannot be assumed.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

The plane z=2 has normal (0,0,1). Point (3,4,7) is distance |7−2|=5 from it. Choosing the normal (0,0,10) gives numerator 50 and denominator 10, preserving the same distance.
