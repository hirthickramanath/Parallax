<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Nuclear binding energy is the mass difference between a specified set of constituents and the bound nucleus, with atomic/electron conventions matched.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Using consistent masses, $B=[Zm_p+Nm_n-M_{nuc}]c^2$. A positive B means the bound mass is smaller than the free constituents. Atomic masses include electrons, so nuclear and atomic conventions cannot be mixed without correction.

A semiempirical mass model includes volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry and pairing contributions. It summarizes broad trends but does not resolve all shell closures, deformations or excited levels. Coefficients are fitted, not exact universal constants derived by counting nucleons alone.

Shell models use nucleons in effective single-particle potentials plus residual interactions, with spin-orbit structure important for magic numbers. Collective models describe rotations/vibrations in suitable nuclei. They are complementary approximations rather than one literal classical arrangement.

Nuclear radius scales roughly as r₀A¹ᐟ³ over a regime, reflecting approximate density saturation. Halo, light and strongly deformed nuclei can depart. Spectra, scattering, moments and decay data constrain structure beyond one mass measurement.

</LessonSection>
