<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

The equivalence principle relates free fall and local inertial physics, while curvature distinguishes gravity across a finite region.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For timelike free fall in an appropriate test-body regime, $u^\nu\nabla_\nu u^\mu=0$. Coordinate acceleration can be nonzero in a chosen chart even though an ideal accelerometer measures no nongravitational proper acceleration.

A metric defines spacetime intervals and light cones. Signature and coordinates must be stated; a coordinate speed of light need not equal the locally measured c in arbitrary coordinates. The local equivalence statement is not a global transformation removing every gravitational field.

Tidal separation depends on curvature through geodesic deviation. A sufficiently extended apparatus can detect relative acceleration between nearby freely falling bodies. The principle does not say all finite gravitational effects are indistinguishable from uniform acceleration in flat spacetime.

Test universality and coupling assumptions need experimental context. Charged bodies, pressure forces and self-gravity can depart from ideal test-geodesic motion. Spacetime geometry plus matter dynamics and initial/boundary conditions are needed for predictions, not the equivalence principle alone.

### Separate the connection from curvature operationally

At an event, choose a freely falling orthonormal frame. The metric can be Minkowski there and its first derivatives can vanish, while second derivatives need not. The connection is chart dependent; the relative motion of nearby test bodies supplies measurable tidal information. A nonrotating supported observer in flat spacetime can have nonzero proper acceleration even though curvature vanishes. Conversely, a freely falling observer in curved spacetime can have zero proper acceleration while measuring tides.

In the Newtonian weak-field, slow-motion limit, relative acceleration for small separation is

$$\delta\ddot x^i=-\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,\xi^j.$$

The Hessian has units of inverse time squared and multiplies a length to give acceleration. This is a useful tidal diagnostic in that limit; it is not a coordinate-independent definition of the relativistic curvature tensor in arbitrary coordinates.


### Derivation and transfer

In a weak static field, a clock frequency ratio differs by approximately ΔΦ/c² between nearby potentials. This is a leading small-potential statement, not an exact law valid arbitrarily near every horizon. Locally a freely falling observer can remove uniform gravitational acceleration at a point, but tidal curvature remains.

The display uses a prescribed small dimensionless potential difference. It does not calculate a metric or equate a coordinate acceleration with an invariant curvature measurement.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

If a supplied local tidal coefficient maps separation ξ=2 to relative acceleration −6, its coefficient is −3 in consistent units. Doubling ξ doubles that leading small-separation response, not every finite separation effect.

For $\Phi=-GM/r$, compare nearby freely falling particles with radial separation $\xi_r$ much smaller than $r$. Differentiating the radial gravitational acceleration gives $\delta\ddot r\simeq 2GM\xi_r/r^3$: radial stretching. In local transverse directions the corresponding coefficients are $-GM/r^3$: compression. The three eigenvalues sum to zero outside the source, consistent with $\nabla^2\Phi=0$ there. Inside matter that vacuum condition need not hold.

This sign pattern also illustrates why the earlier supplied negative tidal coefficient cannot be assumed for every direction in a gravitational field.

</LessonSection>
