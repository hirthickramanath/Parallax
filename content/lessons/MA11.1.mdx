<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A tensor is a multilinear geometric object with a specified transformation law; upper and lower component indices encode tangent and dual transformations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Under a smooth invertible coordinate change, $v'^i=(\partial x'^i/\partial x^j)v^j$, while a covector transforms with the inverse Jacobian. A gradient of a scalar is naturally a covector. Treating both component types as identical Euclidean arrays in arbitrary coordinates loses the distinction.

A repeated upper/lower index denotes contraction in the Einstein convention; free indices must match across an equation. Dummy indices can be renamed consistently but cannot collide with an existing free index. A scalar contraction such as α_i v^i is invariant because Jacobian and inverse factors cancel.

A nondegenerate metric identifies vectors and covectors through v_i=g_ijv^j, but lowering an index is not generally a cosmetic notation change. In curvilinear coordinates or spacetime, metric components and signature affect the result. Tensor products, contractions and addition of matching types preserve tensor character.

An arbitrary table of numbers or a partial derivative of tensor components does not automatically transform as a tensor. The connection supplies correction terms for differentiation. Checking one coordinate system cannot establish a proposed tensor law; derive the transformation or give a coordinate-free definition.


### Derivation and transfer

A vector and a covector transform oppositely so their contraction is invariant. In coordinates with metric g, lowering an index multiplies by g; it is not merely changing a symbol position. For a diagonal metric diag(1,a²), a vector with components (0,v) has squared norm a²v².

The display changes this coordinate metric scale. A coordinate component and a physical measured magnitude are distinct. Tensor transformation laws must hold for all allowed changes of basis, not just for one convenient diagonal example.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>
