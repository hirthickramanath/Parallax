<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a continuous variational symmetry of an action, Noether’s theorem gives a conserved current on equations of motion under its hypotheses.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A continuous field variation with action invariant up to a boundary term yields a current satisfying $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$ on shell. Its integral charge is conserved only when boundary flux and time-dependent domain effects are handled. Local conservation is not the claim that no charge can enter a chosen box.

Time translations, spatial translations and rotations correspond to energy, momentum and angular momentum in the appropriate symmetric background. A time-dependent external driving field can break a subsystem’s time-translation invariance while the enlarged system has a different conservation balance.

Discrete symmetries such as parity do not supply Noether currents by the same continuous-parameter argument. Selection rules depend on Hamiltonian and interaction operator symmetry. Spontaneous breaking differs from explicit breaking: the equations/action may retain symmetry while a state does not.

A classical symmetry can fail as a quantum symmetry through an anomaly; gauge consistency imposes stringent anomaly conditions. Approximate symmetries give qualified near-conservation, not exact eternal constants. Specify level of description and boundary conditions before applying a conservation claim.


### Derivation and transfer

A continuous symmetry of the action gives an associated conserved current under the relevant conditions. Conservation of one quantity does not imply every imaginable reaction occurs; all charges and kinematics must be checked together.

The display uses a normalized two-channel branching model with partial rates Γ1 and Γ2. Branch fractions sum to one when these are the only channels. This arithmetic does not derive a symmetry, calculate a matrix element or show that arbitrary rates obey a physical selection rule.

</LessonSection>
