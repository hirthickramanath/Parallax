<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

How can the distribution of scattering angles reveal a force too small to see directly? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Rutherford scattering links measured trajectories to an inverse-square interaction. The inference depends on an incident-beam distribution, collision geometry and approximations about the target, not only one bent path.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

The impact parameter labels how far an incoming straight asymptote misses the centre. More central approaches experience stronger deflection; a ring of incident paths maps into a ring of outgoing directions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For repulsive potential κ/r with κ>0, classical elastic two-body scattering can be reduced to relative motion with reduced mass and centre-of-mass energy E. Scattering angle θ refers to that relative problem.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Let the relative incident speed be v∞ and reduced mass μ, so E=μv∞²/2 and angular momentum L=μv∞b. The repulsive hyperbolic orbit gives

$$b=\frac{\kappa}{2E}\cot(\theta/2).$$

The formula uses centre-of-mass relative scattering. For a very heavy target this approximates the laboratory projectile angle; a finite target mass requires an angle transformation. Head-on b=0 gives θ=π. At large b the deflection becomes small.

For an azimuthally symmetric beam, trajectories in the incident annulus b to b+db contribute area $d\sigma=2\pi b|db|$. The outgoing solid-angle annulus is $d\Omega=2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$. Dividing yields $d\sigma/d\Omega=(b/\sin\theta)|db/d\theta|$. Substituting b(θ) produces

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\left(\frac{\kappa}{4E}\right)^2\csc^4(\theta/2).$$

Dimensions are area per solid angle, with solid angle dimensionless in SI. The result is an angular probability-density scale after beam flux and target number are supplied, not the probability at an exact mathematical angle.

For a head-on repulsive encounter, all relative kinetic energy converts temporarily into potential energy at closest approach, giving rmin=κ/E. At nonzero impact parameter the angular kinetic term also contributes at the radial turning point; using κ/E there underestimates that distance.

The ideal unscreened Coulomb cross section diverges in the forward limit. Atomic screening, finite experimental acceptance and multiple scattering limit direct application there. Finite nuclear size, nuclear interactions and relativistic or quantum effects can alter the classical point-charge prediction. An approximately heavy stationary target and a thin foil suppress some complications, but these assumptions must be checked rather than inferred from the name of the experiment.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

For an azimuthally symmetric beam, trajectories in the annulus $[b,b+db]$ occupy cross-sectional area $2\pi b|db|$. Their scattering directions occupy solid angle $2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$, giving $d\sigma/d\Omega=(b/\sin\theta)|db/d\theta|$. Substitution of $b=[\kappa/(2E)]\cot(\theta/2)$ yields $d\sigma/d\Omega=[\kappa/(4E)]^2\csc^4(\theta/2)$ in the appropriate two-body relative-coordinate description.

A detector accepts a finite solid-angle bin, so predicted counts require integrating over that acceptance and including beam exposure and detector efficiency. The ideal forward singularity is not automatically integrable: near zero the angular measure leaves an integral proportional to $\int\theta^{-3}d\theta$. Screening or a finite angular cutoff changes the physical prediction. Converting relative-frame scattering to a laboratory angle additionally requires the target/projectile mass kinematics.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For κ=2 and relative E=1 in consistent units, κ/(2E)=1. A 90° scattering angle has cot45°=1, so b=1. A head-on encounter at this energy reaches rmin=2. Doubling E at fixed angle halves b and reduces the differential cross section by a factor of four.

</LessonSection>
