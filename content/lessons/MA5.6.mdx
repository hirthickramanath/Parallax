<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Gravitational and electrostatic potentials depend on source and boundary information. Separation of variables is a construction method whose geometry and uniqueness assumptions must be explicit.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A source bends a potential locally, while the boundary controls which homogeneous solution can be added. Harmonic corrections can change an entire interior field.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Laplace’s equation is Δu=0; here use the Poisson sign convention −Δu=f. A boundary-value problem must specify boundary data and the domain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a rectangular two-dimensional domain, an ansatz u=X(x)Y(y) in Δu=0 gives X″/X=−Y″/Y, a separation constant. If u vanishes at x=0,L, the nontrivial x modes are sin(nπx/L), while the y factors are hyperbolic functions. The basis follows the boundary conditions; it cannot be chosen solely because a sine expansion looks convenient.

For the strip 0&lt;x&lt;L, 0&lt;y&lt;H with zero sides and bottom and top value h(x), a separated solution is $u(x,y)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\frac{\sinh(n\pi y/L)}{\sinh(n\pi H/L)}$, with bₙ the sine coefficients of h. Smooth compatibility at corners is an additional condition for smooth boundary behaviour; a convergent interior representation can coexist with nonsmooth corner data.

To solve $-\Delta u=f$, one may find a particular solution and add a harmonic correction matching the boundary. The particular solution alone rarely satisfies the prescribed boundary data. In a smooth connected bounded domain, a sufficiently regular zero-source difference of two equal-Dirichlet solutions has $\int|\nabla w|^2=0$ by integration by parts, so w is constant and its zero boundary fixes w=0. This proves uniqueness, not an explicit construction for every domain.

For Neumann data ∂ₙu=g and the convention −Δu=f, integrating gives $\int_\Omega f\,dV=-\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,dS$. This is a compatibility condition. If it holds under suitable solvability hypotheses, adding a constant preserves the problem, so a reference value or zero mean removes the ambiguity. Mixed conditions need their own analysis; pure Neumann uniqueness is not the same as Dirichlet uniqueness.

In a ball or cylinder, angular/radial separation produces geometry-adapted modes and regularity requirements at the origin or axis. Growing/decaying branches must be selected by domain and boundary behaviour. A harmonic function obeys a mean-value property and a maximum principle under the usual hypotheses; a sourced Poisson solution does not inherit every zero-source statement unchanged. Numeric residuals and boundary error both matter when checking an approximate field.


### Derive, test and transfer

A harmonic function solves ∇²u=0. On a bounded regular connected region with continuous boundary data, the maximum principle constrains its extremes and supports uniqueness of the Dirichlet solution. Uniqueness does not mean a chosen simple separated expression automatically matches the requested boundary values.

For a rectangular separated example sin(kx)sinh(ky), the second x derivative is −k² times the function and the second y derivative is +k² times it, so they cancel. Poisson's equation adds a source and requires a particular solution plus boundary correction. A point-source singularity falls outside an ordinary smooth interior harmonic claim and must be handled explicitly.

</LessonSection>
