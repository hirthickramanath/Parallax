<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Renormalization separates intermediate regulator choices from matched observables and organizes scale dependence and effective-theory uncertainty.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Regularization defines singular expressions; renormalization fixes parameters/operators through conditions so predictions at a stated order can be compared across scales.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A cutoff or dimensional regulator changes intermediate integrals. Counterterms consistent with symmetry and power counting absorb dependence into defined parameters; simply deleting every large term is not a justified procedure. The renormalization condition specifies what measured or matched quantity fixes a parameter.

A running coupling obeys a beta function $\beta(g)=dg/d\ln\mu$ under this energy-scale convention. Coefficients and coupling definitions can be scheme dependent, while consistently matched observables agree to the admitted accuracy. A coupling is not a universal scale-independent number in every process.

For an EFT, higher-dimension operators are suppressed by a high scale under a stated power counting. Nonrenormalizable in the old finite-counterterm sense does not imply useless: it can be predictive to fixed order with finitely many needed coefficients. Extrapolation past the cutoff is not justified.

Perturbative series can be asymptotic, and large logarithms may require resummation. A regulator-independent answer still has truncation, input and model uncertainties. Naturalness arguments and speculative ultraviolet completions are not direct experimental measurements.

### Carry out a matching subtraction in a regulated toy model

Let $q>0$ and define the dimensionless integral $I_\Lambda(q)=\int_0^\Lambda dk/(k+q)=\ln[(\Lambda+q)/q]$. This is an illustrative integral, not a claimed loop amplitude for a specified QFT. Write $A_\Lambda(q)=c_\Lambda+I_\Lambda(q)$ and fix a common matching condition $A_\Lambda(q_0)=A_0$ at positive $q_0$. Eliminating the bare coefficient gives

$$A_\Lambda(q)=A_0+\ln\frac{(\Lambda+q)q_0}{q(\Lambda+q_0)}\longrightarrow A_0+\ln(q_0/q).$$

The divergent constant cancels because the same physical matching condition fixes it. At finite large cutoff, the leading residual is $(q-q_0)/\Lambda$. The result does not authorize dropping an arbitrary momentum-dependent divergence with a single constant counterterm.

### Integrate a specified beta function

For the toy law $dg/d\ln\mu=bg^2$, separation of variables gives $g(\mu)=g_0/[1-bg_0\ln(\mu/\mu_0)]$. Differentiating verifies the differential equation. For $b>0$ this approximation grows with energy. A zero denominator is a failure of this perturbative extrapolation; it is not by itself experimental proof of a physical singularity or a complete ultraviolet theory.


### Derivation and transfer

For a declared one-loop toy beta function dα/dlnμ=bα², integration gives α(μ)=α0/[1−bα0ln(μ/μ0)]. The coefficient and sign depend on the theory. A denominator zero warns of the perturbative expression's breakdown rather than providing a trustworthy infinite physical coupling.

The display keeps well away from that pole with b=1. It does not fit a real gauge theory or show that every interaction runs in the same direction. Scheme and matching thresholds belong to a quantitative calculation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a toy beta law dg/dlnμ=.1 at effectively constant beta over a step Δlnμ=2, Δg≈.2. This linear approximation is not a universal QFT running law. A term suppressed as (E/Λ)² is .01 at E/Λ=.1.

Take $q_0=1$, $q=2$ and $A_0=0$. The matched finite-cutoff prediction is $\ln[(\Lambda+2)/(2(\Lambda+1))]$. At cutoffs 10 and 100 it is approximately −0.6061 and −0.6833, approaching $-\ln2\simeq-0.6931$. Matching removes the logarithmic divergence while finite-cutoff errors remain.

For $b=0.1$, $g_0=0.5$ and $\ln(\mu/\mu_0)=2$, the integrated toy running gives $g=5/9$, rather than replacing the beta function by a constant over the whole interval.

</LessonSection>
