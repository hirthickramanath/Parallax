<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

How can a fictitious image charge give the correct physical field? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Boundary-value methods replace difficult surface-charge distributions with mathematical constructions. Image charges are useful because uniqueness certifies a field in the physical domain, not because hidden charges actually exist inside a conductor.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A mirrored charge can enforce one simple boundary condition. Only the region outside the replaced conductor is the intended solution domain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The method of images constructs a potential meeting the source equation and specified boundary data in a physical region. Fictitious sources lie outside that region.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Place a real charge q at z=a>0 above an infinite grounded conducting plane z=0. In z>0, combine its potential with an image −q at z=−a. At every plane point the distances to the two charges are equal, so V=0. The image has no singularity in the physical half-space. With appropriate decay at infinity, Dirichlet uniqueness proves this is the exterior solution.

The force on the real charge is the field of the induced charge, represented by the image, excluding the real charge’s self-field. Its vertical component is $F_z=-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a^2)=-kq^2/(4a^2)$. The image is 2a away and moves as a changes in the construction.

The physical interaction energy is $U=-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a)=-kq^2/(4a)$ with zero at infinity. It includes a factor 1/2 relative to q times the image potential at the charge. This follows either by integrating the force during assembly or by the induced-charge energy expression. Treating the image as an independent fixed physical charge gives a wrong assembly energy.

For a grounded sphere of radius R and an external charge q at distance a>R from its centre, an image q′=−qR/a at radius R²/a along the same radial direction enforces zero sphere potential. A neutral isolated sphere requires a different total-charge condition, often supplied by an additional central image. Grounded and isolated boundaries are not interchangeable.

Other boundary methods include separation of variables and numerical finite-difference or finite-element solutions. A numerical residual alone does not prove convergence to the intended continuum boundary problem; refine the mesh and check the actual boundary conditions and known limits. Images are special constructions, not a universal recipe for every conductor geometry.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Draw the plane and real/image positions, verify zero potential at several plane points, then prove the equality from the distance relation. Compute force and energy separately and check Fz=−dU/da. Mark the image region as mathematical rather than physically charged.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Use k=1, q=2 and a=1 for the grounded plane. Force is −1 in the vertical direction and energy is −1 in compatible units. Doubling a reduces the force magnitude to 1/4 and changes the energy to −1/2.

</LessonSection>
