<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Fourier modes connect vibrating apparatus, heat flow and astronomical spectra. A series must be interpreted in a specified convergence sense before a plotted approximation becomes a physical claim.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Think of orthogonal coordinates for functions: each sine and cosine measures a different direction. Truncation keeps a finite set of directions; it does not erase an unresolved jump.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

On an interval of length 2π, Fourier coefficients are projections onto the trigonometric basis. Pointwise, uniform and mean-square convergence are different statements.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a real square-integrable function on [−π,π], define $a_n=\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx$ and $b_n=\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx$, with constant term a₀/2. Orthogonality makes a finite Fourier partial sum the least-squares projection onto its retained modes. Completeness of the trigonometric system gives convergence in L² for an L² function; this alone is not a theorem of pointwise convergence everywhere.

For a periodic, piecewise smooth function with one-sided limits, the usual Dirichlet convergence theorem gives the midpoint of the two limits at a jump and the function value at a continuity point. Values assigned at isolated points do not affect coefficients or the L² equivalence class. A continuous periodic function need not have every stronger convergence property without additional hypotheses.

For the odd square wave f(x)=1 on (0,π) and −1 on (−π,0), parity removes cosine terms and integration gives bₙ=4/(πn) for odd n and zero for even n. Thus $f(x)\sim\frac4\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2j+1)x)}{2j+1}$. At x=0 the series converges to zero. The periodic extension also jumps at the interval endpoints.

Near a jump, ordinary partial sums exhibit Gibbs oscillations. Their spatial region shrinks with more modes while the limiting relative overshoot does not disappear. This does not contradict L² convergence. Fejér averaging changes the summation method and has better positivity/convergence properties; it is not the same finite reconstruction as an ordinary sharp spectral truncation.

With this normalization, Parseval reads $\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f|^2dx=a_0^2/2+\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2+b_n^2)$ for real f. For complex functions use modulus-squared coefficients in the complex convention. Termwise differentiation requires additional regularity/convergence justification: differentiating an L² series formally is not automatically differentiating its limit. A half-range sine or cosine expansion chooses an odd or even extension, so boundary behaviour belongs to the chosen extension.


### Derive, test and transfer

On [−π,π], sine and cosine orthogonality isolate Fourier coefficients by integration. For f(x)=sin x+2sin3x, only the first and third sine coefficients are nonzero. Orthogonality is an integral statement on a stated interval and measure, not a claim that two mode values are perpendicular at every point.

At a jump, an appropriately regular Fourier series converges to the midpoint of the two one-sided limits. Gibbs overshoot persists near the discontinuity as more modes narrow its spatial extent; pointwise convergence away from the jump does not imply uniform convergence to a discontinuous target. A partial-sum plot can illustrate the effect but cannot replace the convergence hypotheses.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For f(x)=3+2cos x−4sin2x, orthogonality gives a₀=6, a₁=2, b₂=−4 and all other positive-mode coefficients zero. The mean square (1/(2π))∫f²dx is 9+(4+16)/2=19. Parseval gives the same value; confusing a₀ with the constant term would double the mean.
