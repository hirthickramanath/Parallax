<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Mechanistic drawings are conservation bookkeeping with physical hypotheses. Correct arrows do not prove a real pathway, but incorrect electron and charge bookkeeping can rule one out immediately.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Follow an electron pair from a lone pair or bond into its new bonding location. A charge label is not an electron reservoir by itself.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A nucleophile donates an electron pair to an electrophilic centre. A full curved arrow tracks a pair; a single-headed fishhook represents a one-electron process.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In a two-electron elementary-step drawing, arrows begin at a lone pair or bond and end at the new pair location. Breaking a bond heterolytically sends the pair to one fragment. Homolytic cleavage sends one electron to each, requiring one-electron arrows. The drawn process must conserve nuclei, total electrons and net charge for the closed modeled step.

Formal charge is $\mathrm{FC}=V-N-B/2$, where V is valence electrons of the neutral atom, N nonbonding electrons and B bonding electrons in that Lewis description. It is a bookkeeping assignment, not a direct measurement of a continuously distributed electronic charge. Resonance can change formal-charge allocation without changing total charge or nuclear positions.

An intermediate corresponds to a local minimum on the relevant free-energy surface; a transition state corresponds to a saddle along a reaction path. Drawing a species between arrows does not establish that it is a long-lived intermediate. Activation free energy and reaction free-energy change answer different questions: rate versus thermodynamic tendency.

Acid-base transfer, substitution, addition and elimination are patterns of bond change. A proposed elementary mechanism must match kinetic order, stereochemistry, isotope effects and environmental constraints. Solvent participation and ion pairing can make a bare gas-phase arrow scheme a poor solution model, or vice versa.

For a bimolecular step involving independent reactants in a stated dilute regime, a rate law may be k[A][B]. The balanced overall reaction does not automatically determine that law, and the units of k depend on the order. Radical chains and photochemical excited states require additional state and electron bookkeeping; a ground-state pair-arrow model is not universal.


### Derive, test and transfer

Curved-arrow bookkeeping moves electron pairs from an occupied source to a receiving atom or bond, while total charge and atom count are conserved for the full stated step. A resonance arrow reorganizes a representation without changing atom connectivity; a reaction arrow describes a chemical transformation.

Energy surfaces can illustrate why plausible arrows do not uniquely establish a mechanism. The embedded two-state crossing toy uses coupled parabolic surfaces; it is not a computed reaction profile. A real elementary-step claim needs chemically valid intermediates, solvent/conditions, stereochemistry and kinetic evidence rather than arrow appearance alone.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a methyl-halide substitution drawn as HO⁻+CH₃Br→CH₃OH+Br⁻, one pair arrow runs from oxygen’s donor pair to carbon and another from the C–Br bond to bromine. Total net charge is −1 on both sides, and each atom is retained. This schematic is a bookkeeping example, not a complete condition-dependent kinetic claim.
