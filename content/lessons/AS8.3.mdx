<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a spherical Newtonian mass distribution, a circular orbit satisfies v_c²=GM(&lt;r)/r; applying this inversion to a flattened disc without qualification is approximate.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If v_c stays constant in the spherical model, $M(\lt r)=v_c^2r/G$ grows linearly with radius. A finite isolated point mass instead predicts v_c∝r⁻¹ᐟ². This comparison identifies an extended gravitational contribution within that model; it is not a direct image of invisible particles.

Observed velocities need inclination, gas pressure support, beam smearing and noncircular-motion corrections. Stellar/gas mass estimates require distance and mass-to-light assumptions. A disc’s gravitational field is not determined only by the mass inside a sphere, so realistic mass decomposition uses its actual geometry.

Dark haloes are constrained by rotation, dispersions, lensing and cosmological structure with different systematics. A named halo profile is a parametric model, not an observed exact universal density law. Central cores/cusps, feedback and baryonic degeneracies require careful comparison.

Alternative-gravity proposals must be tested against the same range of data, not dismissed or accepted from one curve alone. Distinguish evidence for missing gravitational effects from identification of a particle species. Calibration, selection and non-equilibrium systems affect the strength of a particular inference.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a spherical circular orbit, centripetal balance yields $v_c^2/r=GM(<r)/r^2$, hence $M(<r)=v_c^2r/G$. A flat v_c therefore implies enclosed mass increasing linearly with radius in that spherical approximation. A point mass instead gives a declining Kepler speed proportional to r^−1/2.

Flattened discs do not obey the spherical enclosed-mass shortcut exactly. Gas pressure, inclination errors and noncircular motions affect observed rotation speeds. Dark-matter inference combines the measured kinematics with baryonic mass models and gravity assumptions; a flat curve is not itself a particle identification. The model displays the spherical inference while retaining those limits.

</LessonSection>

For a spherical circular-speed model with v=4,r=3,G=2, enclosed mass is 24. If r doubles while v stays flat, the inferred enclosed mass doubles. This is an explicitly spherical inference, not a literal thin-disc inversion.



### Worked investigation: A flat rotation curve implies a mass profile

For a circular test-particle orbit in a spherical Newtonian mass distribution, v_c²=GM(&lt;r)/r. If v_c stays at 200 km/s between radii 10 and 20 kpc, inferred enclosed mass doubles. A point-mass Keplerian decline would instead give v proportional to r^(-1/2). A locally flat curve corresponds to M(&lt;r) proportional to r and, within the spherical approximation, rho proportional to r^(-2).

The inference requires a geometry and a dynamical model. Inclination errors, pressure support and noncircular streaming can bias the measured circular speed. A flat curve motivates additional gravitating mass within a specified theory; it does not by itself identify a particle species or prove the same profile extends to infinite radius.
