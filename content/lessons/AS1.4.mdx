<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

The two-body solution is exceptional: adding a third gravitating body generally removes a simple closed orbital solution. Lagrange points are equilibrium configurations in a rotating description, not holes in gravity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

In the co-rotating frame, the attraction of both primaries and the centrifugal term can balance. Nearby motion still needs a stability analysis; force balance at one point does not guarantee nearby objects stay there.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

The circular restricted three-body problem treats two massive bodies in circular mutual motion and a third body of negligible mass that does not alter them.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Choose total primary mass 1, separation 1 and rotation rate 1 in normalized units. Let $\mu=M_2/(M_1+M_2)$, with primaries at x=−μ and x=1−μ. For a planar test particle define $\Omega=(x^2+y^2)/2+(1-\mu)/r_1+\mu/r_2$. The rotating equations are $\ddot x-2\dot y=\partial\Omega/\partial x$ and $\ddot y+2\dot x=\partial\Omega/\partial y$. The velocity terms are Coriolis terms; the x²+y² term supplies centrifugal acceleration.

An equilibrium in this frame has zero velocity and acceleration, so ∇Ω=0. There are three collinear points L1, L2 and L3, and two triangular points L4 and L5 forming equilateral triangles with the primaries. In an inertial frame the configuration rotates; these are not stationary spatial positions.

For small μ, the distance from the smaller primary to L1 or L2 is approximately $a(\mu/3)^{1/3}$. This leading-order expansion is not the exact position and does not describe the triangular points. All three collinear points are linearly unstable in the ideal circular restricted model. Triangular points are linearly stable for $\mu(1-\mu)<1/27$ within that model, with marginal cases and nonlinear behaviour requiring care. Real orbital eccentricity and additional forces complicate station keeping.

A useful conserved quantity in the circular restricted model is $C=2\Omega-(\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2)$ with the three-dimensional Ω. Zero-velocity surfaces delimit energetically allowed regions for a chosen C; they do not alone predict the trajectory. A general N-body computation instead integrates all accelerations $\ddot{\mathbf r}_i=G\sum_{j\ne i}m_j(\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i)/|\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i|^3$ and checks numerical energy and angular-momentum errors. Chaotic sensitivity is not a license to ignore those checks.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

With μ≈3×10⁻⁶ and separation a=1 AU, the small-μ L1/L2 distance estimate is a×(10⁻⁶)^(1/3)=0.01 AU, about 1.5 million km. This is a leading-order scale estimate; a precise mission orbit requires additional dynamics and corrections.

</LessonSection>
