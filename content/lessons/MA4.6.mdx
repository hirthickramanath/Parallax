<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why do different vibration modes become orthogonal even when they overlap in space? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Sturm–Liouville theory places many separated wave and radial equations into a common self-adjoint framework. Boundary conditions are part of the operator and determine its spectrum.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Integration by parts moves derivatives between two trial modes. If the boundary contributions cancel, different eigenvalues force the weighted overlap to vanish.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A regular Sturm–Liouville problem on a finite interval has −(py′)′+qy=λwy with p>0 and weight w>0, suitably regular real coefficients, and self-adjoint boundary conditions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Use the weighted inner product $\langle u,v\rangle_w=\int_a^b\overline u v\,w\,dx$. Integration by parts gives a boundary form involving p times u and v derivatives. Separated real Dirichlet, Neumann or suitable Robin conditions make this form vanish for two admitted functions. Merely writing a symmetric differential expression without imposing a compatible domain is not enough for self-adjoint spectral conclusions.

For two eigenfunctions with eigenvalues λ and μ, subtract the integrated equations to obtain $(\lambda-\mu)\int_a^b\overline u v\,w\,dx=0$ after boundary cancellation. A similar conjugate argument makes eigenvalues real. Distinct eigenvalues therefore give orthogonal modes in the weighted, not necessarily unweighted, inner product.

For a nonzero admitted real test function, the Rayleigh quotient is the integrated energy form divided by ∫wy², including any boundary contribution appropriate to the stated conditions. With Dirichlet endpoints it is $R[y]=\int[p(y')^2+qy^2]dx/\int wy^2dx$. Positivity of p alone does not force all eigenvalues positive when q can be negative.

For −y″=λy on (0,L) with y(0)=y(L)=0, negative and zero λ yield no nonzero solution satisfying both endpoints. Positive λ gives $y_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $\lambda_n=(n\pi/L)^2$, n=1,2,... . Their weighted orthogonality here uses w=1. Expansion completeness is a theorem under the regular assumptions; convergence mode must still be specified for a particular expansion.

Singular endpoints, infinite intervals, vanishing coefficients or non-self-adjoint conditions require additional theory. For example a radial problem can need integrability and boundary selection at the origin. A finite-interval sine basis cannot be transplanted to every open-domain physical problem without checking its operator domain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="interactive" title="Investigate the claim" layer="interactive">

Compute the first few modes for Dirichlet and Neumann endpoints and compare the zero-mode possibility. Verify weighted overlaps numerically and by trigonometric identities. Record the boundary form before claiming two eigenfunctions must be orthogonal.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For L=π, the first two Dirichlet eigenvalues are 1 and 4, with eigenfunctions sin x and sin2x. Their overlap integrates to zero on [0,π]. Their squared norms are π/2, so unit-normalized modes multiply by √(2/π).

</LessonSection>
