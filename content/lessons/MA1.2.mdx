<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Calculus needs limits that actually belong to its number system. Rational arithmetic is dense but incomplete: it can approach a missing value indefinitely. Completeness is an axiom about the real line that closes this gap.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A supremum is the tightest ceiling above a set. It need not be a point in that set, but any lower proposed ceiling must fail.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A nonempty real set bounded above has a least upper bound in the real numbers. This least-upper-bound property is the completeness axiom used here.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A number u is an upper bound of S if s≤u for every s in S. Its supremum α is an upper bound with the property that for every ε>0 there exists s in S with $\alpha-\varepsilon<s\le\alpha$. If this failed, α−ε would be a smaller upper bound. A maximum additionally belongs to S; a supremum need not. The interval (0,1) has supremum 1 and no maximum.

Completeness implies the Archimedean property. If positive integers were bounded above, let α be their supremum. There would be an integer n>α−1, hence n+1>α, contradicting the bound. Consequently for every ε>0 there exists an integer n with 1/n&lt;ε. The property is about arbitrarily large finite integers, not an integer called infinity.

To see why rationals are not complete, consider positive rationals q with q²&lt;2. They are nonempty and bounded above, but have no rational least upper bound. A rational number squaring to 2 would be impossible by the lowest-terms parity proof. More constructively, if a proposed rational boundary squared below 2, a sufficiently small positive rational increment would remain below 2; if above 2, a sufficiently small rational decrement would remain above it. Neither is the least upper bound in the rational system.

A key consequence is monotone convergence: if a real sequence increases and is bounded above, take α as the supremum of its range. Given ε>0, choose some term aN>α−ε. For n≥N, monotonicity gives $\alpha-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le\alpha$, proving convergence to α. Without boundedness it can diverge; without completeness the boundary may be missing.

Nested nonempty closed bounded intervals with lengths tending to zero have one common real point. The endpoints form monotone bounded sequences, whose limits coincide. Closedness keeps the limits inside each interval. Open intervals, or closed unbounded sets, do not support the same conclusion without further hypotheses.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

Completeness is a property of the real number system, not just an inequality technique. A nonempty set bounded above has a least real upper bound. The set $\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb N, n\geq1\}$ has supremum 1, although 1 is not attained. If $u<1$, choosing $n>1/(1-u)$ gives a term larger than $u$.

The analogous property fails in $\mathbb Q$: consider nonnegative rational $q$ with $q^2<2$. A proposed rational supremum with square below 2 can be increased by a sufficiently small rational increment; one with square above 2 can be decreased while remaining an upper bound. Equality would require a rational square root of 2, ruled out by the usual even/odd contradiction in lowest terms. Nested closed-interval proofs must use closedness and shrinking lengths explicitly; nested open intervals can lose every candidate point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For $S=\{1-1/n:n\in\mathbb N,\ n\ge1\}$, every term is below 1. Given ε>0 choose n>1/ε, making 1−1/n>1−ε. Therefore sup S=1, but 1 is not attained. The same estimate proves convergence of the increasing sequence.
