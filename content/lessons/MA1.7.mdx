<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Integration is not just reverse differentiation. Defining it from upper and lower sums shows which irregularities are tolerated and which require a broader integral concept.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Partition an interval into bins and bound the area in each bin by its lowest and highest function values. Integrability means the total uncertainty can be made arbitrarily small.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a bounded real function on [a,b], lower and upper Darboux sums use the infimum and supremum on each partition interval. Riemann integrability means their limiting lower and upper integrals agree.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For partition $a=x_0<\cdots<x_n=b$, set $L(f,P)=\sum m_i\Delta x_i$ and $U(f,P)=\sum M_i\Delta x_i$. Refinement raises lower sums and lowers upper sums. A bounded function is Riemann integrable exactly when for each ε>0 some partition has $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. This criterion controls all choices of sample values within that partition's cells.

A continuous function on a closed bounded interval is integrable: uniform continuity makes the oscillation within sufficiently small cells uniformly tiny, so the upper-minus-lower sum is at most that oscillation times interval length. Bounded functions with a finite number of discontinuities are also integrable; cover those points by intervals of arbitrarily small total length and control the remaining continuous pieces.

The function equal to 1 on rational points and 0 on irrational points of [0,1] is not Riemann integrable. Every nontrivial cell has infimum 0 and supremum 1, so every partition leaves lower sum 0 and upper sum 1. A finite plot cannot display the density-based obstruction accurately. A broader Lebesgue integral assigns it a value, but that is a different definition.

For integrable f define $F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$. At a point where f is continuous, the average integral over a shrinking interval approaches f(x), proving F′(x)=f(x). If a differentiable antiderivative G has derivative f on the interval and f is integrable, the fundamental theorem yields $\int_a^b f=G(b)-G(a)$. The hypotheses should accompany the shorthand “integration reverses differentiation.”

Improper integrals at an infinite endpoint or singularity are limits of proper integrals. Split at an interior singularity and require each one-sided limit separately; cancellation across divergences is a principal-value construction, not ordinary improper convergence. For example, symmetric cancellation does not make ∫₋₁¹1/x an ordinary improper integral.


### Quantitative proof and failure case

For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, monotonicity gives lower sum $L_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i^2/n^3$ and upper sum $U_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^2/n^3$. Their difference telescopes to $1/n$. The square-sum formula gives $U_n=1/3+1/(2n)+1/(6n^2)$; squeezing proves the integral is $1/3$ and supplies an explicit numerical error bound.

The Darboux criterion requires partitions with arbitrarily small upper-minus-lower gap for a bounded function. For the rational indicator, density of rationals and irrationals makes each cell's supremum 1 and infimum 0. The gap remains 1 regardless of mesh size, so the function is not Riemann integrable. A finite floating-point sampling grid cannot establish which values occur throughout every interval.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For f(x)=x on [0,1] with n equal cells, lower sum is (n−1)/(2n) and upper sum (n+1)/(2n). Their difference 1/n tends to zero, so the integral is 1/2. This gives an error bracket without assuming an antiderivative formula.

</LessonSection>
