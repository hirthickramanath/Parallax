<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Photon counts, measurement noise and population models require probability distributions rather than one guessed value. Expected values summarize a distribution; they need not be possible individual outcomes.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

The expectation is a probability-weighted balance point. Variance measures the weighted squared spread about it, not a typical signed deviation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

A random variable maps outcomes to numbers. Its distribution specifies their probabilities or a density; its expectation is a weighted sum or integral when it exists.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a discrete X, $E[X]=\sum_x xp(x)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-E[X])^2]=E[X^2]-E[X]^2$. Standard deviation is the square root of variance and has the same units as X. Expectation is linear even for dependent variables, but variances add without a covariance term only for uncorrelated variables.

For independent Bernoulli trials with common success probability p, the count X has binomial probability $P(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. Its mean is np and variance np(1−p), obtained by writing X as a sum of indicators. Trials with different success probabilities or dependence need another model.

A Poisson count with mean λ has $P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$ and variance λ. It approximates many rare independent opportunities and models a constant-rate process with independent increments. Counts from variable rates or clustered arrivals can be overdispersed. For a Poisson count N, the rough large-count noise scale is √N; this is not an exact symmetric confidence interval at low counts.

For independent identically distributed measurements with finite variance σ², the sample mean has expectation μ and variance σ²/n. Thus random standard error falls as $1/\sqrt n$. A fixed calibration offset persists under averaging. Correlated measurements reduce the effective gain.

The central limit theorem concerns the suitably standardized sum under stated assumptions; it does not claim every underlying quantity is Gaussian. Infinite-variance distributions and strongly dependent data can violate its familiar form. A confidence statement also requires an inferential method, not just a histogram resembling a bell.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Four independent unbiased measurements each have standard deviation 2 units. Their mean has standard deviation 2/√4=1 unit. If every measurement also has a +3 unit calibration bias, that bias in the mean remains +3.

</LessonSection>
