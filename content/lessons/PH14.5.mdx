Semiconductor carrier densities depend on bands, temperature, chemical potential and dopants; device currents also require generation, recombination and transport.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In a suitable nondegenerate equilibrium model, intrinsic carrier scaling includes $n_i\propto e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$ with temperature-dependent density-of-states factors. This exponential is not valid unchanged in a degenerate or heavily doped regime. Dopant ionization and compensation affect the actual free carriers.

Conductivity is σ=e(nμ_n+pμ_p) in a basic low-field model. A hole is an effective missing-electron carrier, not a new elementary positively charged particle. Mobility depends on scattering and field/temperature.

A pn junction forms a space-charge/depletion region in equilibrium with one electrochemical potential. A built-in potential is not a freely extractable perpetual battery voltage around a complete equilibrium circuit. Forward/reverse driven currents depend on boundary injection, recombination and regime.

Ideal diode equations and depletion approximations have limited ranges. Tunnelling, breakdown, series resistance, heating and traps require extensions. This lesson explains low-energy device physics; it does not prescribe hazardous apparatus operation or claim a fabricated device measurement.


### Derivation and transfer

In a simple nondegenerate intrinsic semiconductor model, carrier concentration includes an activation factor exp(−Eg/(2kT)). Both electrons and holes contribute, while density-of-states prefactors and their temperature dependence complete the expression.

The display holds that prefactor fixed. It is not a doped junction solution or a universal conductivity prediction: mobility, defects, degeneracy and contacts also matter. Doubling a gap cannot be interpreted without the temperature and units.

</LessonSection>
