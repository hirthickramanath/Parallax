Why can a system with one repeated eigenvalue produce a polynomial times an exponential? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Defective linear dynamics cannot always be decoupled into independent exponentials. Generalized eigenvectors organize the remaining coupling, and the matrix exponential still solves the system without a diagonalization assumption.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A Jordan block is an eigenvalue plus a chain that shifts information between coordinates. Repeated shifts eventually vanish, producing a finite polynomial factor in the exponential.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Over the complex numbers, every finite square matrix is similar to a Jordan form. A block is λI+N with nilpotent N. The matrix exponential is the convergent power series ∑Aᵏtᵏ/k!.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A generalized eigenvector of rank k is annihilated by (A−λI)ᵏ. Jordan chains satisfy $(A-\lambda I)v_1=0$ and $(A-\lambda I)v_{j+1}=v_j$. A complete set of such chains yields a basis in which the operator has Jordan blocks. Ordinary eigenvectors supply only the first member of each chain.

For one block J=λI+N of size s, Nˢ=0 and λI commutes with N. Hence

$$e^{tJ}=e^{\lambda t}\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\frac{t^kN^k}{k!}.$$

For A=SJS⁻¹, $e^{tA}=S e^{tJ}S^{-1}$. The power-series definition is valid even if Jordan form is not computed, and differentiating the series gives $d(e^{tA})/dt=Ae^{tA}$. The solution of x′=Ax with x(0)=x₀ is $e^{tA}x_0$.

For J=[[λ,1],[0,λ]], the exponential is $e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. If λ=0, some states grow linearly even though all eigenvalues have zero real part. If every eigenvalue has strictly negative real part, finite polynomial factors are eventually dominated by exponential decay; boundary cases with zero real part depend on block structure.

Time-dependent matrices require caution. Writing exp(∫A(t)dt) as a fundamental solution generally needs commutation conditions. Matrix products do not commute by default, so the constant-coefficient derivation cannot be transferred unchanged to a changing operator.

Jordan form is useful for exact classification but can be numerically unstable near defective matrices: arbitrarily small perturbations can split eigenvalues and alter the block structure. Practical matrix-exponential algorithms use stable factorizations and scaling strategies, such as Schur-based or Padé scaling-and-squaring methods, rather than inferring an exact Jordan structure from noisy decimal data.


### Derive, test and transfer

For a Jordan block J=λI+N with N²=0, the commuting exponential factorization gives exp(tJ)=exp(λt)(I+tN). The polynomial factor arises from the nilpotent part and is lost if one replaces the block by a diagonal matrix with the same repeated eigenvalue.

For J=[[-1,1],[0,-1]], solutions include t exp(−t) terms even though both eigenvalues are negative. They still decay asymptotically, but transient components can initially grow. For longer nilpotent chains the exponential includes higher finite powers of t. A defective representation is sensitive to perturbations, so Jordan form is a structural analytic tool rather than a universally stable numerical decomposition.

</LessonSection>
