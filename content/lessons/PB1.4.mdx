<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="A gravity meter made of string" layer="puzzle">

Would a heavier bob swing faster? Would a larger release angle change the period? Record your two predictions before opening the apparatus.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Timekeeping as an experiment" layer="story">

Pendulums linked regular motion to practical timing and measurements of gravity. A useful scientific instrument needs not only a repeatable signal but a model that connects that signal to the desired quantity. We avoid unverified historical dates in this draft.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="The restoring tendency" layer="intuition">

Gravity pulls the displaced bob toward the lowest point. Near that point the restoring influence is approximately proportional to displacement, the hallmark of simple harmonic motion.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Idealization and misconception" layer="concept">

Use a point bob on a massless inextensible string of length L, fixed pivot, uniform gravity, and negligible drag. Misconception: a formula that works for small swings must work equally well at any amplitude.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive the period, then test its limits" layer="theory" fullOnly>

About the pivot, gravity gives torque $-mgL\sin\theta$. For a point bob, $I=mL^2$, so $I\ddot\theta=-mgL\sin\theta$. Dividing by positive $mL^2$ gives

$$\ddot\theta+\frac gL\sin\theta=0.$$

For $|\theta|\ll1$ in radians, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$. Let $\omega^2=g/L$. The linear equation has solution $\theta=A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t$. Release from rest at amplitude $\theta_0$ gives $A=\theta_0$, $B=0$. One cycle corresponds to $\omega T=2\pi$, hence

$$T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg},\qquad g=\frac{4\pi^2L}{T_0^2}.$$

The small-angle assumption is the only approximation introduced in this derivation beyond the ideal apparatus assumptions.

For finite amplitude, energy conservation with release from rest gives

$$\frac12L^2\dot\theta^2=gL(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0).$$

Separate variables and integrate a quarter-cycle:

$$T=4\sqrt{\frac L{2g}}\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0}}.$$

Substitute $\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin\phi$, where $k=\sin(\theta_0/2)$. The square-root factors cancel with the differential, yielding

$$T=4\sqrt{\frac Lg}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\phi}}.$$

As $\theta_0\to0$, $k\to0$ and the integral tends to $\pi/2$, recovering $T_0$. The lab evaluates this integral numerically.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Infer gravity from ten swings" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For L = 1.00 m and a synthetic ten-cycle time of 20.0 s, T = 2.00 s and g = 4π²(1.00)/(2.00)² ≈ 9.87 m/s². This estimate is not a real measurement. A 1% relative period error contributes about 2% relative error to g.

</LessonSection>
