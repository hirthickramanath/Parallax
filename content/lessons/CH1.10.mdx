<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why can adding more ions increase conductivity while reducing molar conductivity? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Ionic transport depends on both carrier amount and mobility. Interactions can reduce per-mole transport even as the total number of carriers rises.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

More carriers can move more charge overall, but each carrier’s drift can be hindered by the surrounding ionic environment.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Conductivity relates current density to electric field in a specified linear isotropic regime. Molar conductivity divides that conductivity by the stated electrolyte concentration.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If bᵢ is the mechanical mobility defined by drift velocity per force on a particle, an ideal independent-ion expression is $\kappa=\sum_i n_i(z_ie)^2b_i$. With the Einstein relation bᵢ=Di/(kBT), it becomes a Nernst–Einstein estimate. If instead electrical mobility is defined as speed per field, the formula has a different charge factor; conventions must not be mixed.

Molar conductivity is Λm=κ/c for c in the matching molar concentration units. For strong electrolytes at low concentration, interactions cause systematic departures from independent infinite-dilution behavior. Weak-electrolyte dissociation changes carrier count as concentration varies. Conductivity therefore is not a direct universal count of fully independent ions.

Charge number, concentration gradients and electric fields combine in a dilute Nernst–Planck flux model. An ideal one-species form without flow is $J_i=-D_i\nabla c_i-(D_iz_iF/RT)c_i\nabla\phi$ for molar concentration and flux. Convective transport adds another term. The ideal dilute model requires modification in concentrated or correlated electrolytes.

Electroneutrality is often an excellent bulk approximation on scales much larger than screening lengths, but interfaces and double layers can carry local charge density. Imposing neutrality pointwise at every microscopic scale would erase the very field structure needed to describe such interfaces.

A conductivity cell measures conductance through geometry: G=κA/ℓ for a uniform simple cell, so cell calibration matters. Electrode polarization, alternating-frequency response and temperature drift can bias an inferred bulk conductivity. Report concentration scale, units, geometry and temperature rather than comparing raw conductance values as material constants.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a uniform cell with area A=2 and length ℓ=4 in matched geometric units, measured conductance G=3 implies conductivity κ=Gℓ/A=6. If concentration is 2 in the corresponding molar units, Λm=3.

</LessonSection>
