<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Causal response and initial-value problems are easier in a transform domain only when initial terms, inversion and the allowed half-plane are retained.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

The real part of the transform variable exponentially discounts the future. A growing signal needs a stronger discount before its integral exists.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The unilateral transform is F(s)=∫₀∞e⁻ˢᵗf(t)dt. Piecewise continuity and exponential order provide standard sufficient existence hypotheses in a right half-plane.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

If f is piecewise continuous on bounded intervals and bounded by Meᵃᵗ eventually, its unilateral Laplace transform converges absolutely for Re s>a. This sufficient bound need not locate the sharp abscissa. The formula $\mathcal L[e^{at}](s)=1/(s-a)$ describes the defining integral only for Re s>a; algebraic continuation of the rational expression elsewhere is a different operation.

Integration by parts gives $\mathcal L[f'](s)=sF(s)-f(0^+)$ under the required regularity and decay. A second derivative contributes s²F−sf(0⁺)−f′(0⁺). Dropping initial terms changes an initial-value problem into another problem. For an ordinary causal input, convolution on [0,t] transforms into a product, with sufficient integrability/exponential-order conditions for exchanging integrals.

A delayed causal function u(t−a)f(t−a), a≥0, transforms to e⁻ᵃˢF(s). This is not the same as u(t−a)f(t). Derive the shift by substituting τ=t−a in the integral; the distinction matters for switching and delayed forcing. Treat impulses in a declared distribution convention rather than pretending they are bounded ordinary functions.

For y′+by=g(t), y(0⁺)=y₀, transformation yields $Y(s)=[y_0+G(s)]/(s+b)$. Partial fractions invert simple rational cases. Repeated poles generate powers of time, and complex conjugate poles produce real oscillations when coefficients/data are real. Pole cancellation can remove an output mode, so a transfer-function plot alone does not establish every internal stability property.

A Bromwich inversion contour lies in a convergence half-plane to the right of relevant singularities under the inversion theorem assumptions. Unilateral causality is built into the setup; a bilateral transform needs a region of convergence to distinguish different time supports. Analytic continuation can help inversion but does not redefine the original integral on a divergent domain. Inspect late-time growth and the original differential equation after inversion.


### Derive, test and transfer

For the one-sided Laplace transform F(s)=∫₀∞e^(−st)f(t)dt, integration by parts gives L[f′]=sF−f(0) when the boundary term at infinity vanishes. The initial value is therefore built into the transformed initial-value problem rather than added as an optional correction.

For f(t)=exp(at), F(s)=1/(s−a) in the half-plane Re(s)>Re(a). The algebraic expression alone does not state the region of convergence, and inverse-transform interpretation depends on that analytic information. Products in the transform domain correspond to causal convolutions, not generally pointwise multiplication in time.

</LessonSection>
