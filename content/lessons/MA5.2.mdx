<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Astronomical spectra and image resolution require continuous-frequency reasoning, but sampling and finite observation change what a transform can reveal.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For $F(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-ikx}dx$, an L¹ function has a bounded continuous transform. Inversion needs hypotheses or an appropriate limiting interpretation; it is not an absolutely convergent inverse integral for every L¹ input. Plancherel extends the transform to L² with $\int|f|^2dx=(2\pi)^{-1}\int|F|^2dk$ under this convention.

Integration by parts gives the derivative rule $\widehat{f'}(k)=ikF(k)$ when derivative integrability and boundary decay justify it; distribution theory extends the rule to broader cases. Translation f(x−a) contributes e⁻ⁱᵏᵃ. A spatial rescaling f(ax) has transform |a|⁻¹F(k/a) for nonzero real a. Keeping the absolute value prevents a sign error for reflected coordinates.

For integrable functions with the needed convolution hypotheses, $\widehat{f*g}=FG$. Multiplication in space instead gives $\widehat{fg}=(2\pi)^{-1}(F*G)$. Thus a finite window broadens ideal spectral features. For a rectangular pulse of height one on [−a,a], direct integration gives F(k)=2sin(ka)/k with continuous limit F(0)=2a. A zero denominator at k=0 is a removable formula singularity.

For f(x)=exp(−x²/(2σ²)), the Gaussian integral yields $F(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-\sigma^2k^2/2}$. The normalized squared-amplitude widths obey ΔxΔk≥1/2 when finite variances and derivative hypotheses hold, with equality for the corresponding Gaussian family. Frequency ν in cycles per unit and angular frequency k differ by 2π; a numerical width cannot be moved between conventions unchanged.

A discrete Fourier transform samples a finite vector and assumes a periodic extension. It is not the full continuous transform without sampling, window and normalization factors. Aliasing folds frequencies under sampling; zero-padding interpolates the finite-data transform and does not recover lost resolution. Sampling theorems require a bandlimit and the stated sampling rate. Noise and a finite instrument response are additional inference problems.


### Derive, test and transfer

Choose $\hat f(k)=\int f(x)e^{-ikx}dx$ and inverse factor 1/(2π). Under this convention, differentiation maps to multiplication by ik when boundary terms vanish, and convolution maps to multiplication of transforms. Changing the convention changes normalization factors, so formulas must travel with their definitions.

For a centered Gaussian exp(−x²/(2σ²)), the transform is √(2π)σ exp(−σ²k²/2). Narrowing the original Gaussian broadens its transform. This scaling concerns continuous transform widths; discrete sampled data additionally have finite-window leakage, frequency resolution and aliasing. A continuous transform formula does not automatically specify a correct FFT sampling strategy.

</LessonSection>
