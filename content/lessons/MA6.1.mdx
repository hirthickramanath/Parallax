<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Why is complex differentiability much stronger than having two real derivatives? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Complex methods organize oscillations, response functions and potential fields. Their power comes from hypotheses stronger than ordinary smoothness on a plane.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A complex derivative must give one multiplier for tiny displacements in every direction. A general real-linear map can stretch different directions differently and need not be multiplication by one complex number.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For f=u+iv on an open subset of the complex plane, complex differentiability at z means the difference quotient has one limit as the complex increment tends to zero through every direction. Holomorphic means differentiable at every point of an open neighbourhood.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Approach the derivative along a real increment to obtain f′=uₓ+ivₓ, and along an imaginary increment to obtain f′=vᵧ−iuᵧ. Equality forces the Cauchy–Riemann equations $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$ when the corresponding partials exist. These are necessary at a differentiability point, but merely checking pointwise partial derivatives and these equations without sufficient differentiability assumptions does not prove the full limit.

If u and v have continuous first partial derivatives near a point and satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations there, their real differentiability gives a linear approximation whose anti-complex-linear part vanishes. Thus f is complex differentiable at that point. Satisfying these conditions throughout an open set gives holomorphicity there. The sufficient C¹ condition is convenient rather than logically necessary for every characterization.

For $f(z)=z^2$, u=x²−y² and v=2xy satisfy the equations everywhere and f′=2z. By contrast, conjugation f(z)=conj(z) has real-direction quotient 1 and imaginary-direction quotient −1. It is smooth as a real map but nowhere complex differentiable. For |z|², the quotient at zero tends to zero, so differentiability at a single point does not establish holomorphicity on a neighbourhood.

A local power series converges holomorphically inside its radius, with termwise differentiation on compact subdisks. Conversely, a holomorphic function admits local power series; that converse uses Cauchy theory rather than only the elementary derivative definition. Complex analyticity therefore entails derivatives of all orders and much stronger local structure than real differentiability alone.

With sufficient second-derivative regularity, differentiating Cauchy–Riemann gives $\Delta u=0$ and $\Delta v=0$. Thus real and imaginary parts are harmonic. A harmonic real function has a harmonic conjugate locally on a disk, but a global single-valued conjugate can fail on a domain with holes. For log|z| on the punctured plane, the would-be conjugate is an angle that cannot be chosen continuously around a full loop. Domain topology is part of the conclusion.

</LessonSection>

Calculate difference quotients for z² and conjugation using small increments along the real axis, imaginary axis and diagonal. Compare all directions as the step shrinks. Then examine |z|² at zero and at a nonzero point; distinguish one-point differentiability from holomorphicity on an open region.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For z² at z=1+2i, f′=2+4i. The real part of the derivative is 2. Also uₓ=2x=2 and vᵧ=2x=2 there, while uᵧ=−2y=−4 and −vₓ=−2y=−4. These checks agree with the full polynomial derivative rather than replacing its definition.

</LessonSection>
