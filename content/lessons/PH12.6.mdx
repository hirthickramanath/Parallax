Laser cooling uses velocity/position-dependent optical forces under a specified level and beam model; momentum diffusion limits simple friction-only predictions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In a low-velocity linearized model the mean force can be $F\approx-\alpha v$. Absorption and spontaneous-emission recoils cause fluctuations, so deterministic damping alone cannot determine the final temperature. The equilibrium follows the relevant friction/diffusion balance when that description applies.

The elementary Doppler temperature scale is ℏΓ/(2k_B) for the ideal two-level cooling setting, with Γ convention as an angular decay rate. Multilevel sub-Doppler mechanisms, recoil and trapping dynamics change attainable limits. It is not a universal minimum temperature for every optical technique.

A magneto-optical trap combines restoring and damping mechanisms with field gradients and polarization; optical molasses alone is not automatically a spatial trap. Evaporative cooling can further lower temperature by selectively losing high-energy atoms and rethermalizing through collisions.

An ideal uniform three-dimensional boson condensation criterion involves nλ_T³ exceeding the appropriate threshold, with λ_T=h/√(2πmk_BT). Traps, finite size and interactions change quantitative conditions. Condensation, coherence and superfluidity are related but distinct, and loss/heating must be included in actual experiments.


### Derivation and transfer

Photon recoil energy is (ℏk)²/(2m), so lighter atoms receive a larger energy kick at fixed wave number. Doppler cooling balances scattering-force damping and momentum diffusion under a specific transition and laser configuration; sub-Doppler and recoil limits involve different mechanisms.

The display isolates recoil in ℏ=1 units. It neither simulates cooling trajectories nor proves Bose–Einstein condensation. Condensation also depends on phase-space density, interactions, trapping and thermalization.

</LessonSection>
