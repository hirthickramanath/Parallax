<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In coordinates the affine equation is $\ddot x^\mu+\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta=0$. A general nonaffine reparametrization introduces a tangent-proportional term; omitting that distinction can make a correct path look like a failed geodesic.

For a Riemannian metric, stationary length/energy paths give the geodesic equation under the corresponding endpoint and parameter assumptions. They minimize length only locally before relevant conjugate/cut points, not necessarily globally. On a sphere multiple geodesics can connect special pairs of points.

In Lorentzian spacetime, freely falling test particles follow timelike geodesics and geometric-optics rays follow null geodesics under the theory’s assumptions. Proper time can be locally maximal for timelike geodesics in a suitable neighbourhood; calling it an ordinary shortest spatial route is wrong. Null proper time is zero and needs an appropriate affine formulation.

External forces, pressure and self-force effects can move matter away from test geodesics. A connection-dependent coordinate acceleration can be nonzero without a locally measured nongravitational force. Global paths also depend on topology and boundary/initial data, not just one local differential equation.

### Obtain the equation from stationary energy

For a smooth positive-definite metric and fixed endpoints, use $L=\tfrac12 g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$. Its Euler–Lagrange equation is

$$g_{kj}\ddot x^j+\partial_lg_{kj}\dot x^l\dot x^j-\tfrac12\partial_kg_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j=0.$$

Multiplying by the inverse metric and symmetrizing the two velocity indices yields the Christoffel expression and the affine geodesic equation. Metric compatibility then implies $d(g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j)/d\lambda=0$: the speed is constant in an affine parameter. Fixed endpoints eliminate the variation boundary term; without that condition additional boundary equations may be needed.

### Change the parameter carefully

If $x(\lambda)$ is affine and $\lambda=\lambda(s)$ with nonzero first derivative, the covariant acceleration with respect to $s$ is $(\lambda''/\lambda')dx/ds$. Only linear changes preserve the zero right-hand side in general. The geometric curve remains the same. Constant curves are a special case and do not establish the converse for moving paths.


### Derivation and transfer

A geodesic has zero covariant acceleration in an affine parameter. In flat polar coordinates its radial equation is r″−rθ′²=0, so r″ can be nonzero even for a Cartesian straight line. Coordinate acceleration is not invariant acceleration.

The display isolates the instantaneous radial coordinate term. It does not integrate a geodesic or prove that every geodesic is a global shortest path. Beyond conjugate points or on closed geometries, local and global minimizing properties differ.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

In Euclidean Cartesian coordinates x=3λ,y=4λ for 0≤λ≤1 is a constant-speed affine geodesic of length 5. Its speed squared is 25. Expressing that same path relative to another origin in polar coordinates changes the component equations without changing its geometric length.

In the Euclidean plane use $x=t$, $y=b$ with constant $b>0$, so $r=\sqrt{t^2+b^2}$ and a continuously chosen polar angle satisfies $\dot\theta=-b/r^2$. The nonzero polar coefficients are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}=1/r$. Compute $\ddot r=b^2/r^3$ and $r\dot\theta^2=b^2/r^3$, so the radial equation cancels. Differentiating the angle also gives $\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta/r=0$. Nonzero coordinate accelerations have described a straight line in flat space.

</LessonSection>
