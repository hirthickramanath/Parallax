<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Determinants encode orientation and local volume scaling. Their signs matter for coordinate transformations, while unsigned geometric volume uses the absolute value.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Think of a parallelepiped built from column vectors. Flattening it destroys volume; swapping two edges flips handedness without changing its size.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The determinant is the normalized alternating multilinear function of the columns of a square matrix. Normalization sets det I=1.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Multilinearity makes the determinant linear in each column when the others are fixed. Alternation makes it zero for repeated columns and reverses sign under a column swap. Expanding all columns in the standard basis gives the permutation formula $\det A=\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_i a_{i,\sigma(i)}$. This is a definition-compatible formula, not a recommendation for efficient computation at large n.

If columns are dependent, one can express a column as a combination of the others and multilinearity forces determinant zero. Conversely, a nonzero determinant implies independent columns and hence an invertible square matrix. Row elimination supplies a practical proof and computation: swapping rows flips sign, scaling a row scales the determinant, and adding a multiple of one row to another leaves it unchanged.

The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of diagonal entries. Tracking elimination then computes a dense determinant efficiently through a factorization. A naive permutation expansion has factorial growth in terms. For large or very small magnitudes, a signed log-determinant avoids representational overflow or underflow.

Composition scales volume in successive steps, yielding $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$; a purely algebraic proof also follows from the defining alternating multilinear property. Thus det(A⁻¹)=1/det A when the inverse exists. A determinant is defined only for square matrices in this conventional setting.

A small determinant alone is not a scale-independent condition-number estimate. Multiplying a well-conditioned n×n identity by a tiny nonzero scalar makes its determinant tiny without making its relative conditioning poor. Singular values, together with a declared norm, diagnose sensitivity more reliably. Determinants are useful for theoretical invertibility, orientation and Jacobians; numerical rank decisions require scale-aware tolerances.


### Derive, test and transfer

The determinant gives the signed volume scale of a square linear map. Exchanging two columns reverses sign; multiplying one column by c multiplies the determinant by c. A zero determinant means the image has lower dimension and the map is not invertible, rather than meaning every output vector is zero.

For A=[[a,b],[c,d]], oriented unit-square area becomes ad−bc. Unsigned area uses its absolute value. Determinant is multiplicative under composition, explaining why inverse matrices have reciprocal determinants. In higher-dimensional symplectic geometry determinant one is necessary but not sufficient for canonicity; volume preservation alone is weaker than preservation of all relevant pairings.

</LessonSection>
