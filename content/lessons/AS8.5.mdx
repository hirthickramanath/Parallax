<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Large-scale gravity redistributes orbital energy and angular momentum through collective effects, while gas adds dissipative physics.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Galaxy formation couples dark-matter assembly, baryonic cooling, star formation and feedback. A merger is a collective dynamical interaction rather than a collision of every constituent star.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Gravitational encounters perturb orbits over large scales even when stellar physical cross sections are small. Dynamical friction transfers momentum/energy to a gravitational wake under its approximation, not ordinary material rubbing. Tidal forces can strip outer matter and create streams.

Gas can shock, radiate and lose energy, unlike collisionless stars and dark matter. Cooling permits contraction when time scales allow, while feedback from stars and accreting black holes can heat or expel material. A simple gas consumption time is $t_{dep}=M_{gas}/\dot M_*$, conditional on a snapshot rate and no replenishment/outflow changes.

Merger outcomes depend on mass ratio, orbit, gas fraction and internal structure. Angular momentum redistribution can fuel central activity or change morphology, but not every merger produces the same burst or remnant. Secular evolution also changes galaxies without a major merger.

Cosmological simulations use sub-resolution prescriptions where stellar/black-hole processes are unresolved. Agreement with one calibrated relation is not independent proof of every feedback mechanism. Compare resolutions, conservation budgets and multiple observables with selection and uncertainty before claiming a unique assembly history.


### Derive, test and transfer

In a simple closed-box chemical-evolution model with instantaneous recycling and effective yield y, integration gives $Z=y\ln(1/f_g)$ for an initially metal-free well-mixed reservoir. As gas is consumed, its metallicity rises; the logarithmic behavior encodes the changing remaining gas mass.

Real galaxies gain and lose material, merge and form stars over delayed enrichment timescales. Metal-selective winds and fresh inflow can change the relation even at the same gas fraction. Merger dynamics also redistribute energy and angular momentum rather than merely adding two identical density profiles. The display isolates an analytic budget; it cannot reconstruct a galaxy's merger tree from one metallicity measurement.

</LessonSection>
