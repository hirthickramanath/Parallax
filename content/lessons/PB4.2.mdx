<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to the puzzle" layer="puzzle">

Does putting a second resistor in parallel always reduce the supply current? State your reasoning before continuing; a wrong prediction is a useful starting point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this question mattered" layer="story">

Electrical measurements compare what flows through a component with the potential difference across it. The power of a circuit law comes from choosing clear definitions and testing when a material behaves linearly, not treating every component as a fixed resistor.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the mental picture" layer="intuition">

A series element offers another obstacle along the same current path. A parallel branch opens an additional path at the same endpoint voltage. With an ideal fixed-voltage source, that additional path increases total current even though no charge is consumed at a resistor.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the idea precisely" layer="concept">

Conventional current is positive-charge flow direction: I = dQ/dt. In quasistatic steady circuits, charge conservation gives equal incoming and outgoing current at a node. An ohmic resistor at fixed conditions has V = IR. A diode, lamp or heating resistor need not have one constant resistance over an arbitrary operating range.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="The complete argument" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a wire of area A with mobile-carrier density n and signed drift speed v_d along a chosen direction, the magnitude of current is I = n|q|A|v_d|. Current density is J = I/A for a uniform flow.

In a simple relaxation-time model, a carrier’s average drift velocity responds to the field through $\mathbf v_d=(q\tau/m)\mathbf E$. Multiplying by carrier charge density yields

$$\mathbf J=\frac{nq^2\tau}{m}\mathbf E=\sigma\mathbf E.$$

This model assumes a fixed response regime; it is not a universal microscopic description of every conductor. For a uniform wire of length L and area A, V = EL and I = JA, hence

$$R=\frac VI=\frac L{\sigma A}=\rho\frac LA.$$

For two series resistors, the same steady current crosses each. Potential drops add: V = IR₁+IR₂, so R_eq = R₁+R₂. For parallel branches, endpoint voltage is shared and currents add:

$$I=\frac V{R_1}+\frac V{R_2},\qquad \frac1{R_{eq}}=\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}.$$

For positive finite R₁ and R₂, adding a parallel branch decreases equivalent resistance and increases total current from an ideal fixed-voltage source.

Power follows from energy per charge times charge per time: P = VI. With an ohmic resistor, substitute V=IR to obtain P=I²R=V²/R. The formulas refer to different controlled quantities; doubling resistance at fixed current increases power, while doubling it at fixed voltage decreases power.

An ideal voltmeter has infinite input resistance and is connected across a component. An ideal ammeter has zero resistance and is connected in series. Real instrument resistances alter the circuit, so loading must be included when precision matters.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="Work through an example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

At 6 V, resistors 100 Ω and 200 Ω in series give R_eq = 300 Ω and I = 0.020 A. The drops are 2 V and 4 V, summing to 6 V. In parallel, R_eq = 66.7 Ω, total I = 0.090 A, and branch currents are 0.060 A and 0.030 A. The total power is 0.54 W in the parallel case.
