<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Earth-bound observations and co-rotating orbital descriptions use accelerating coordinates. Apparent forces encode the changing frame; they are not new interactions that replace the original physical forces.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A rotating observer changes the basis arrows while watching the particle. Even if the inertial motion is straight, components relative to those arrows can curve.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For an origin accelerating at a₀ and axes rotating with Ω, the rotating-frame equation includes translational, Coriolis, centrifugal and Euler inertial terms.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a vector A expressed in rotating axes, the inertial derivative obeys $(d\mathbf A/dt)_I=(d\mathbf A/dt)_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A$. This follows because each rotating basis vector changes at rate Ω crossed with itself. Apply the rule twice to position relative to the moving origin to obtain

$$\mathbf a_I=\mathbf a_0+\mathbf a_R+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_R+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).$$

Moving the extra terms to the force side gives fictitious forces −ma₀, $-2m\Omega\times v_R$, $-m\dot\Omega\times r$ and $-m\Omega\times(\Omega\times r)$. Their signs follow from the transformation rather than a memorized direction. The last term points away from the rotation axis; it is not the inward physical force needed to keep an object stationary on a spinning platform.

The Coriolis term is perpendicular to vR, so its instantaneous power in the rotating frame is zero. It changes direction without directly changing rotating-frame speed. It vanishes for vR parallel to Ω, not for every radial motion in an arbitrary geometry. The Euler term vanishes only when Ω is constant as an inertial vector.

For constant Ω the centrifugal force derives from effective potential $U_{\rm cf}=-m|\Omega\times r|^2/2$. Combined with suitable time-independent physical potentials and a stationary origin, an effective rotating energy can be useful. Time-dependent frames require explicit care; inertial and rotating energies are different quantities.

On Earth, local gravity already incorporates the equilibrium centrifugal contribution used by the observer. Adding it again double-counts that effect. Real atmospheric and oceanic motion also includes pressure gradients, friction and geometry; Coriolis alone is not a full weather model.


### Extended derivation and transfer case

For any vector, the transport rule is $(d\mathbf A/dt)_I=(d\mathbf A/dt)_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A$. Apply it twice to the position vector and include an accelerating frame origin to obtain $\mathbf a_I=\mathbf a_O+\mathbf a_R+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_R+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$. Moving the extra terms to the force side fixes their signs.

The Coriolis force is perpendicular to $\mathbf v_R$, so its instantaneous power relative to that rotating velocity is zero. This does not say that all frame-related energy statements are identical. A point fixed in rotating coordinates has $\mathbf v_R=0$ but generally has nonzero inertial centripetal acceleration. The Euler term depends on angular acceleration and vanishes only for constant angular-velocity vector, not merely a fixed rotation axis.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

With Ω along +z and a rotating-frame velocity along +x, Ω×v points +y. The Coriolis acceleration is therefore along −y. If |Ω|=2 rad/s and v=3 m/s, its magnitude is 12 m/s² for perpendicular vectors.
