<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

In vacuum SI, field energy density is u=ε₀E²/2+B²/(2μ₀), and the Poynting vector is S=E×B/μ₀.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Dot Ampère–Maxwell with E and Faraday with B/μ₀. A vector identity combines the curl terms into a divergence, giving $\partial_t u+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E$. The right side is power delivered by fields to matter per volume. Integrating yields stored-energy change plus outward boundary flux equal to negative work on matter inside.

For a uniform vacuum plane wave, E and B are transverse, B=E/c in magnitude and energy is split equally between electric and magnetic terms. For sinusoidal peak electric amplitude E₀, $\langle S\rangle=\epsilon_0cE_0^2/2$. RMS and peak amplitudes differ by √2; mixing their conventions changes the intensity by a factor of two.

Vacuum momentum density is g=S/c². The Maxwell stress tensor represents field momentum flux and forces through boundary surfaces. For normal absorption of a plane beam, pressure is I/c; for ideal normal reflection it is 2I/c, including the reversed photon/field momentum. Oblique incidence and transmission need the full geometry and momentum balance.

In matter, the division of energy and momentum between fields and material response depends on the constitutive model. A simple εE²/2 expression is not generally adequate for dispersive or lossy media. Account for mechanical energy, heat, polarization and magnetization rather than interpreting an isolated field term as a complete conservation law. Near-field power flow can circulate locally without being net radiation to infinity.


### Derive, test and transfer

Electromagnetic energy density in vacuum is ε₀E²/2+B²/(2μ₀), and Poynting flux is S=E cross B/μ₀. Poynting's theorem balances stored field energy, outward flux and work on charges through J dot E. A source can transfer energy through the surrounding field even if a simple circuit diagram draws only wires.

For a plane wave with E perpendicular to B and B=E/c, electric and magnetic contributions are equal. Instantaneous intensity is proportional to E²; sinusoidal time averaging introduces a factor one half relative to peak amplitude squared. Near fields and standing waves can have different local energy-flow patterns, so a plane-wave relation must not be imposed everywhere.

</LessonSection>
