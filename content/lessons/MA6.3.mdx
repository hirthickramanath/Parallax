<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Residues can evaluate response integrals and spectral transforms efficiently, but only after contour orientation, pole location and the added arc have been controlled.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A contour sum isolates the local 1/(z−a) contribution of each enclosed pole. The rest of a real-integral calculation is proving that the contour represents the intended real quantity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

The residue at an isolated singularity is the coefficient of (z−a)⁻¹ in its Laurent expansion. For an admissible positively oriented contour, residues contribute with their winding numbers.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a meromorphic function with finitely many enclosed poles and none on an admissible closed contour, $\oint_\gamma f(z)dz=2\pi i\sum_a\operatorname{Ind}(\gamma,a)\operatorname{Res}(f,a)$. A simple positive boundary gives winding number one for interior poles. A contour containing holes or repeated traversals needs its actual indices; the integral is not always a count of geometrically visible poles.

At a simple pole, $\operatorname{Res}(f,a)=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)$. For a pole of order m, it equals the (m−1)st derivative of (z−a)ᵐf(z) at a divided by (m−1)!. If f=g/h with a simple zero of h and g holomorphic, the residue is g(a)/h′(a). A vanishing numerator may cancel the apparent pole, so check the actual singularity first.

For a>0, close 1/(z²+a²) along an upper semicircle. The only enclosed pole is ia and its residue is 1/(2ia). On a radius-R arc, the integrand is bounded by 1/(R²−a²), and the arc length is πR; hence its contribution tends to zero. Therefore $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx/(x^2+a^2)=\pi/a$. The real integral converges absolutely, so its value agrees with the symmetric limit used by the contour.

An exponential eⁱᵏᶻ decays in the upper half-plane for k>0 and in the lower half-plane for k&lt;0. This choice controls which closure can work, but decay estimates still need proof for the remaining factors. Closing downward gives clockwise orientation for the usual real-axis traversal and therefore a minus sign. A named arc lemma cannot replace checking its hypotheses.

A pole on the real axis requires a declared indentation or principal-value prescription. A Cauchy principal value can exist even when the ordinary improper integral fails; it is not interchangeable with that integral. Branch points require branch cuts and branch-contour accounting. Before interpreting any result physically, distinguish a mathematical prescription from a causal or radiation condition supplied by the model.


### Derivation and transfer

For a simple pole at a, the residue is the coefficient of (z−a)^−1. Thus exp(z)/(z−a) has residue exp(a). A contour enclosing that pole once positively has integral 2πi exp(a). Poles outside the contour contribute nothing.

A real improper integral derived by contour closure also needs a bound for the arc and a prescription for any real-axis poles. Residues alone do not justify discarding an arc or interchanging limits. The display varies a real pole location inside an assumed admissible contour; it is not a contour-selection algorithm.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Take a=2. The upper pole is 2i, its residue is 1/(4i), and multiplying by 2πi gives π/2. Thus the real integral divided by π equals 1/2. Doubling a from 1 to 2 halves the area, also consistent with substituting x=at.
