<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Vectors distinguish direction-sensitive physics from scalar bookkeeping. Work, torque, angular momentum and field geometry use different vector products because they answer different geometric questions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

The dot product asks how much two arrows align. The cross product asks for the oriented area they span in ordinary three-dimensional space.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

A vector has magnitude and direction; its components depend on basis. Scalar and vector products must not be interchanged.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

In an orthonormal Cartesian basis, $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_xb_x+a_yb_y+a_zb_z=|a||b|\cos\theta$. The projection of a onto a nonzero b is $(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\mathbf b/|b|^2$. A scalar component uses the unit vector along b. A zero dot product indicates perpendicular nonzero vectors, although it also occurs if either vector is zero.

In a right-handed 3D basis, $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(a_yb_z-a_zb_y,\ a_zb_x-a_xb_z,\ a_xb_y-a_yb_x)$. Its magnitude is $|a||b|\sin\theta$, and reversing order reverses its sign. Torque is r×F, so both the lever arm and direction matter. A cross product is not a general multiplication rule for arbitrary dimensions.

The scalar triple product $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$ is the signed volume of a parallelepiped. Its vanishing for three vectors means linear dependence in 3D. The vector triple product obeys $a\times(b\times c)=b(a\cdot c)-c(a\cdot b)$; it is not associative.

For a displacement dr, mechanical work is $dW=\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$. A centripetal force is perpendicular to instantaneous velocity and changes its direction without doing instantaneous work. Integrating work along a curve requires the local force direction; multiplying total force magnitude by path length generally fails.

Unit vectors carry direction but no units. Vector components carry the units of the physical vector. Always check component signs against a declared axis convention before interpreting a negative result as an impossible magnitude.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

The dot product $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta$ measures a projection; the cross-product magnitude is $|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\sin\theta$. For nonzero vectors, a zero dot product means perpendicular directions, whereas a zero cross product means collinearity. Component signs of a cross product depend on a stated right-handed orientation. The identity $\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=\mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)-\mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)$ also demonstrates why the cross product is generally not associative.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Let F=(3,4,0) N and displacement (2,0,0) m. Work is 6 J although the force magnitude is 5 N. For lever arm (2,0,0) m, torque is (0,0,8) N m. Work is scalar; torque is axial vector despite sharing energy-like dimensions.

</LessonSection>
