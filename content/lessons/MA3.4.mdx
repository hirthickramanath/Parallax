<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Eigenvectors identify directions preserved by a linear transformation. Their existence does not guarantee every state is a sum of independent eigenvectors; that distinction controls decoupling of coupled systems.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A diagonal representation lets each basis direction scale separately. Repeated roots of a polynomial do not automatically provide separate geometric directions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

An eigenvector is a nonzero vector v with Av=λv. A matrix is diagonalizable over a specified field when it has a full basis of eigenvectors over that field.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Eigenvalues solve $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. For each eigenvalue, the eigenspace is the kernel of A−λI. Its dimension is the geometric multiplicity, no larger than its algebraic multiplicity as a characteristic-polynomial root. The zero vector belongs to the eigenspace but is not called an eigenvector.

Eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues are independent. One proof assumes a shortest nontrivial dependence and applies A−λI for one selected eigenvalue, eliminating that term and contradicting minimality. Thus an n×n matrix with n distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable over a field containing them. Distinct eigenvalues are sufficient, not necessary: the identity is already diagonal despite all eigenvalues being equal.

For a diagonalizing basis S, $A=SDS^{-1}$. Powers and analytic functions then act on D entrywise, so Aⁿ=SDⁿS⁻¹ and $e^{tA}=S e^{tD}S^{-1}$. The quality of numerical eigencoordinates depends on S: a badly conditioned eigenbasis can amplify errors even when exact diagonalization exists.

The matrix J=[[1,1],[0,1]] has characteristic root 1 twice, but (J−I)v=0 forces the second component zero, leaving only one independent eigenvector. It is not diagonalizable. Its powers include an off-diagonal n term because J=I+N and N²=0, giving Jⁿ=I+nN.

The scalar field matters: a real planar quarter-turn has no real eigenvector but has complex eigenvalues ±i. A complex eigenbasis can simplify real dynamics, with conjugate pairing used to recover real solutions. Do not infer instability merely from a nonreal eigenvalue; for continuous-time linear systems real parts, Jordan structure and the time convention determine growth.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For A=diag(2,3), A⁴=diag(16,81). For J=[[1,1],[0,1]], J⁴=[[1,4],[0,1]], although both eigenvalues are 1. The off-diagonal growth shows what eigenvalues alone miss in a defective matrix.

</LessonSection>
