<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Roche geometry describes equipotentials in a rotating circular binary approximation. Filling a Roche lobe can enable mass transfer, whose stability depends on donor and orbital response.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a circular Newtonian binary, $J=\mu\sqrt{GMa}$ with total mass M and reduced mass μ=M₁M₂/M. Mass transfer changes μ and may carry angular momentum away. An orbital response cannot be inferred from donor mass loss alone without specifying what is retained or expelled.

Under the restrictive conservative fixed-total-mass, fixed-J approximation, a∝(M₁M₂)⁻². Transfer toward equal masses can shrink the separation before later evolution reverses the trend. Real winds, discs, tides and magnetic torques change this ideal relation.

The Roche lobe is not a rigid material surface; the critical equipotential depends on mass ratio and separation in the assumed synchronous circular model. Donor radius response relative to lobe response controls stability. Rapid unstable transfer can lead to a common-envelope phase with uncertain efficiency and complex dynamics.

Accretion can rejuvenate or alter the receiving star, generate radiation and change spin/composition. Eclipses, radial velocities and light-curve models constrain geometry and masses with inclination and atmosphere assumptions. A mass function alone normally gives a conditional constraint rather than both exact masses.


### Derive, test and transfer

In a circular corotating binary, the effective potential contains both gravities and the centrifugal term. The equipotential through the inner saddle defines Roche lobes; a donor approaching its lobe can exchange material through that saddle. The volume-equivalent lobe radius depends on mass ratio, not simply on which star is larger on a sky image.

For conservative transfer with fixed total mass and orbital angular momentum, $J=\mu\sqrt{GMa}$, where $\mu=M_1M_2/M$. Thus $a\propto(M_1M_2)^{-2}$ under these restricted assumptions. Transferring mass from the more massive to the less massive component can initially shrink separation; winds or angular-momentum loss change that conclusion. Transfer stability also compares the donor radius response with lobe response. Overflow onset is therefore not a complete prediction of the subsequent flow.

</LessonSection>
