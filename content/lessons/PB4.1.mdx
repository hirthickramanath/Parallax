<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Field theory separates the source distribution from the probe used to observe its influence. Electrostatic potential offers energy bookkeeping that scales to many charges without drawing every force arrow.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

A tiny positive test charge samples a field. It does not have to be present for the source configuration to define a field in the model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

Electric field is force per unit positive test charge in the negligible-perturbation limit. Electrostatic potential is potential energy per unit charge relative to a reference.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a point charge Q in vacuum, $\mathbf E=Q\hat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ away from the source. Fields superpose vectorially. The force on a probe q is qE; a negative probe accelerates opposite E. The point-source expression is singular at r=0 and cannot describe an extended charge internally without a distribution model.

Gauss' law is $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=Q_{\rm enclosed}/\epsilon_0$. Symmetry can make it an efficient field-solving tool. Without sufficient symmetry, knowing the total flux does not determine the field at every surface point. A zero net enclosed charge permits nonzero fields with cancelling flux.

Electrostatics has $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ and $V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l$. Adding a constant to V changes no field. For an isolated point charge with V∞=0, $V=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$. Potential superposes as a scalar, whereas field requires vector components.

An ideal conductor at electrostatic equilibrium has zero internal field, constant potential and excess charge on its surface. This is not a claim about a conductor carrying a resistive current. A parallel-plate capacitor with negligible fringing has $C=\epsilon A/d$, stored energy $U=CV^2/2$, and near-uniform gap field V/d. Inserting a dielectric changes charge or voltage according to whether the capacitor remains connected to a source.

Energy density in a linear dielectric under appropriate static conditions is $u=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D/2$. Molecular polarization, breakdown and frequency dependence limit treating permittivity as one constant.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

Electrostatic work gives $\Delta U=q\Delta V$ and $\Delta K=-q\Delta V$ when no other forces do work. The sign of the test charge therefore matters: electrons accelerate toward increasing electric potential in this energy balance. Locally, $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$; adding a spatially constant reference to $V$ leaves the field unchanged. Do not use that relation as a general local field law in a nonuniform geometry.

### Derivation and transfer

A point charge produces electric field kq/r² in the radial direction, while potential kq/r is a scalar defined relative to infinity. Fields add as vectors and potentials as scalars. A zero potential does not imply a zero electric field.

The display follows one radial field component away from the point singularity. It does not describe the interior of an extended charged object. The sign identifies inward versus outward radial direction, not a negative field magnitude.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked example" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Between plates at separation 0.01 m and potential difference 100 V, the ideal field magnitude is 10,000 V/m. A +2 μC charge experiences 0.02 N. Its potential-energy change across a +100 V rise is +2×10⁻⁴ J, while field work is the negative of that change.



### Worked investigation: Potential zero does not imply field zero

Put +q at x=-a and -q at x=+a. The electric potential at the midpoint is kq/a-kq/a=0 relative to infinity. The fields both point from the positive charge toward the negative one, so E_x=2kq/a² there, not zero. Potential is a scalar superposition; field is a vector and also equals minus the spatial gradient of potential.

For kq=9 N*m²/C and a=3 m, E_x=2 N/C. A single zero value of a function cannot determine its derivative. The reference chosen for potential changes all potential values by a constant while leaving the field unchanged; absolute potential zero is therefore not a force criterion.
