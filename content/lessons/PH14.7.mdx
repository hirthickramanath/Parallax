<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Superconductivity involves a coherent charged condensate description in applicable regimes, with characteristic electromagnetic response; microscopic mechanisms vary across materials.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The Meissner response expels weak magnetic field from the bulk under appropriate conditions, unlike merely preserving an arbitrary initial flux in an ideal conductor. A London penetration length describes exponential field screening in its model regime. Critical fields/currents limit the state.

For a conventional paired charge 2e condensate, flux quantization scale is $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$ with the relevant multiply connected geometry and phase assumptions. Type-II systems admit vortices between critical fields; saying every superconductor excludes all magnetic field under all conditions is false.

BCS theory describes pairing and a gap in an admitted weak-coupling framework, with quasiparticles and coherence factors. A pair does not have to be a small isolated molecule, and the microscopic pairing mechanism of unconventional materials is not universally settled by the word superconductivity.

Josephson relations connect current and phase, and voltage to phase evolution, under junction conventions. Dissipation, quasiparticles, fluctuations and device environment matter. A zero-resistance observation and a full thermodynamic/magnetic characterization provide different evidence; this course claims no new material discovery.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

With h=6 and e=1 in normalized units, the conventional flux quantum is 3. A single vortex carries that scale under the stated model. Doubling paired charge in a hypothetical formula h/q would halve the corresponding flux scale.

</LessonSection>
