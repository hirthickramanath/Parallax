<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For an isolated species with constant decay probability per time, N(t)=N₀e⁻λt and activity is λN.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The rate equation $dN/dt=-\lambda N$ gives half-life ln2/λ and mean lifetime 1/λ. These are not equal. A stochastic nucleus does not wait exactly one half-life before decaying; the law describes an ensemble survival probability.

A decay branch contributes a branching fraction to the relevant emitted signal. A detector records only a response-weighted subset, with background, dead time and energy/angular efficiencies. Activity in becquerels is inverse seconds, not directly a dose or biological-effect quantity.

Decay chains require coupled source/sink equations. Secular/transient equilibrium regimes depend on relative half-lives and initial conditions. Summing separate single-exponential curves without daughter production can misrepresent a chain.

Quantum short-time and long-tail qualifications exist, while exponential decay is an excellent model in many measured regimes. Uncertainty intervals for low counts require an appropriate count likelihood. Distinguish expected counts and one random realization.


### Derivation and transfer

For a two-step decay A→B with no initial B and distinct rates, NB=N0 λA[exp(−λAt)−exp(−λBt)]/(λB−λA). The daughter population can rise before falling, unlike an isolated parent exponential. Equal rates require the continuous limit N0λt exp(−λt).

The display fixes λB=2 and keeps λA below it. It computes expected counts, not sampled decay events or branching chains. Activity and population should not be conflated.

</LessonSection>
