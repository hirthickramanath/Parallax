Why does a tiny polar-coordinate cell have area r dr dθ rather than dr dθ? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Changing variables can simplify a physical integral, but new coordinate increments do not automatically represent the same area or volume. A Jacobian records the local distortion.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A small coordinate rectangle maps approximately into a parallelogram. The determinant of the derivative gives its signed area scale; ordinary area uses the magnitude.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a suitably regular one-to-one coordinate transformation, a multiple integral changes variables with the absolute determinant of its Jacobian. Domain and coverage are part of the theorem.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For a smooth planar map (u,v)↦(x,y), linearize the displacement using its two derivative columns. The infinitesimal area scale is $|\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)|$. In 3D, the determinant scales oriented volume. To use the usual change-of-variables formula, require an invertible transformation on the relevant interior, handle exceptional boundaries appropriately, and avoid counting a region repeatedly.

For polar coordinates x=r cosθ and y=r sinθ, the determinant is r. With r≥0, $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$. The origin has a coordinate singularity and angular endpoints repeat a boundary ray; these measure-zero features can be handled by the standard integration setup, not by claiming global bijectivity everywhere.

For spherical coordinates with polar angle θ from +z and azimuth φ, the volume element is $r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$. Geographic latitude uses a different trigonometric factor. The angular range must cover the sphere once: 0≤θ≤π and a 2π azimuth interval, with the usual boundary identifications.

For a continuous integrand on a rectangle, repeated integrals agree with the double integral. More general Fubini conclusions require integrability hypotheses. For a signed improper integrand, convergence of selected iterated integrals alone does not always permit exchanging their order. For a nonnegative measurable integrand, Tonelli allows iterated integrals even if the result is infinite, in the broader measure-theoretic setting.

For density ρ, total mass is ∫ρ dV and a centre coordinate is ∫xρ dV divided by total mass when defined and nonzero. A coordinate substitution changes the density integrand together with the volume element; dropping the Jacobian changes the modeled mass. Units provide a first check but do not detect every missing dimensionless geometric factor.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

The unit disk area in polar coordinates is ∫₀²π∫₀¹ r dr dθ=π. Omitting r would give 2π, showing that correct domain bounds alone do not repair a wrong area element. The unit ball volume similarly integrates r² sinθ to 4π/3.

</LessonSection>
