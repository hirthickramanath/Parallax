<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Contour methods simplify forcing, field and spectral calculations, but the domain enclosed by a loop must be admissible before applying a zero-integral theorem.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A contour integral accumulates complex tangential increments. A smooth local rule can still have global circulation when the region has an excluded point.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a piecewise C¹ path γ, define ∫γ f(z)dz=∫f(γ(t))γ′(t)dt. Orientation and multiplicity belong to the path data.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Parametrization gives a direct computational definition: a positively oriented circle is z=z₀+Reⁱᵗ for 0≤t≤2π, with dz=iReⁱᵗdt. Reversing orientation changes the integral sign, and traversing twice doubles it. The estimate $|\int_\gamma f(z)dz|\le\operatorname{length}(\gamma)\max_\gamma|f|$ holds for a bounded continuous integrand on a finite-length path.

A standard Cauchy theorem states that a holomorphic function on a simply connected open domain has zero integral around every closed piecewise smooth path in that domain. More general forms use null-homotopic or homologically admissible cycles. Holomorphicity only on a neighbourhood of the path is insufficient if excluded singularities lie inside it.

For f(z)=1/z on the punctured plane, a unit-circle substitution gives $\oint dz/z=2\pi i$. The function is holomorphic on the punctured domain, but the circle cannot contract there. On a suitable slit domain, a single-valued logarithm supplies a primitive and closed integrals vanish. A holomorphic primitive implies path independence; on simply connected domains holomorphicity guarantees such a primitive.

If f is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the closure of a disk and z₀ is inside its positively oriented boundary, Cauchy’s formula is $f(z_0)=(2\pi i)^{-1}\oint f(z)/(z-z_0)\,dz$. Differentiating the kernel gives $f^{(n)}(z_0)=n!(2\pi i)^{-1}\oint f(z)/(z-z_0)^{n+1}\,dz$. The contour and interior analyticity assumptions matter, as does the factorial.

Cauchy estimates give |f⁽ⁿ⁾(z₀)|≤n!M/Rⁿ on a radius-R circle where |f|≤M. In particular an entire bounded function is constant by letting R grow in the first-derivative estimate. The maximum modulus principle concerns holomorphic functions; an interior maximum of the real part alone is a related harmonic statement with its own formulation. Contour deformation must avoid crossing singularities and preserve the relevant winding data.


### Derivation and transfer

Parametrize a circle by z=R exp(it). For the integrand 1/z, dz=iR exp(it)dt, so the contour integral is 2πi for one positive turn. Cancellation removes R, not the orientation or winding number. Reversing the traversal changes its sign.

Cauchy's theorem requires holomorphicity on the appropriate enclosed domain; a pole inside the circle violates that hypothesis. The investigation plots accumulated imaginary integral along this one parametrized contour. It does not infer a theorem from finite samples or allow a contour through the pole.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For a positive circle around zero, ∮z²/z³ dz=∮dz/z=2πi. Equivalently Cauchy’s derivative formula for f=z² with n=2 gives 2πi·f″(0)/2!=2πi. The multiplier of 2πi is 1. Reversing the circle changes that multiplier to −1.

</LessonSection>
