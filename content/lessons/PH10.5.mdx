<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A single determinant enforces antisymmetry. Its energy contains direct Coulomb and exchange terms from the same two-body interaction; exchange is not an additional classical force. For an admissible normalized determinant, $E_{HF}\ge E_0$ for the same lower-bounded Hamiltonian, while practical basis truncation adds another restriction.

The Fock equations are self-consistent because the effective one-particle operator depends on occupied orbitals. Solving an iteration to a small residual does not prove the global variational minimum or the physically correct spin/symmetry state. Check multiple starts and stability where relevant.

Correlation energy conventionally denotes the remaining exact-minus-HF energy within a specified nonrelativistic Hamiltonian and basis-limit definition. Configuration interaction, coupled-cluster and perturbative methods include different correlation structures and have different size-consistency/convergence properties. No one method label guarantees accuracy for strong correlation.

Koopmans-style orbital-energy ionization estimates assume frozen orbitals and neglect relevant relaxation/correlation. Total-energy differences can answer a different question. Density-functional theory uses an exact formal framework with approximate practical functionals; it is not simply HF with renamed exchange.


### Derivation and transfer

A Slater determinant enforces fermionic antisymmetry and leads to exchange terms in Hartree–Fock. The resulting orbitals solve self-consistent equations; simply subtracting a universal constant does not account for all correlation. Correlation energy is defined relative to a specified reference and Hamiltonian.

The display uses the leading free Fermi-gas density scaling EF∝n^(2/3), not an executed self-consistent-field computation. Basis convergence, spin state and reference data are still needed for a real many-body calculation.

</LessonSection>
