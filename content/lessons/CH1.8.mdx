<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Colligative effects arise from the solvent chemical potential in a mixture. Their simple formulas describe dilute ideal limits, not arbitrary concentrated solutions.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Mixing lowers the liquid solvent’s chemical potential relative to the pure liquid. A different temperature is then needed to match the solid-solvent potential.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Colligative properties depend primarily on solute-particle amount in a stated dilute model. Molality counts solute amount per kilogram of solvent, unlike molarity.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

For an ideal dilute nonelectrolyte, solvent activity is approximately its mole fraction. Matching solvent chemical potentials between phases and expanding near the pure transition temperature yields $\Delta T_f=K_fm$ and $\Delta T_b=K_bm$ in the conventional dilute molality formulas. The constants depend on the solvent and thermodynamic approximations.

If solute dissociates or associates, an empirical van ’t Hoff factor i modifies the simplest formula to iKm. It is not automatically an exact integer at every concentration because ion pairing and nonideal activities affect the result. Counting nominal formula units is not the same as counting independent effective particles.

For ideal dilute osmotic equilibrium, solvent chemical-potential balance gives $\Pi\approx cRT$ for the solute number of moles per solution volume. Osmotic pressure is a pressure difference associated with selective solvent transport, not evidence that solute molecules exert a separate classical pressure in a completely unmixed container.

A phase diagram locates stable coexistence boundaries under specified pressure and composition constraints. Tie lines connect coexisting compositions; the lever rule follows from overall material balance. It predicts phase amounts, not transformation rates or spatial domain sizes. More complicated phase equilibria can include eutectic or other features beyond the dilute colligative approximation.

Freezing-point measurements can estimate molar mass only when concentration, solvent purity, dissociation and equilibrium assumptions are controlled. Supercooling, inaccurate solvent mass and unknown aggregation can bias the inferred amount. Use activities for nonideal mixtures rather than extrapolating linear dilute formulas indefinitely.


### Derive, test and transfer

In the ideal dilute freezing-point approximation, ΔTf=iKf m depends on solute particle molality and an effective particle factor. Osmotic pressure similarly approaches i cRT under its dilute assumptions. Dissociation can increase particle count, while association and nonideality can change the effective behavior.

These expressions are limiting laws, not complete high-concentration phase diagrams. Molality and molarity are different concentration definitions, and interchanging them silently changes units and temperature dependence. A measured colligative shift can constrain an effective particle number only with solvent constants and a valid model; it is not a unique molecular identity test.

</LessonSection>
