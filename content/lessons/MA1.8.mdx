<LessonSection id="puzzle" title="Commit to a prediction" layer="puzzle">

Can continuous functions converge to a discontinuous limit? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Interchanging approximation with differentiation, integration or limits is a major source of errors in mathematical physics. Pointwise convergence permits location-dependent error control; uniform convergence makes the control simultaneous.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Pointwise convergence lets each input wait its own amount of time. Uniform convergence requires one threshold to work everywhere in the domain.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Functions fₙ converge uniformly to f on D if for every ε>0 there is N such that n≥N implies |fₙ(x)−f(x)|&lt;ε for all x in D. Equivalently, the supremum error tends to zero when that supremum is finite.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

On [0,1], $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges pointwise to 0 for x&lt;1 and to 1 at x=1. Each fₙ is continuous, but the limit is not. For each n, points sufficiently near 1 keep xⁿ arbitrarily close to 1 while the limit there is zero, so the supremum error is 1. The convergence is not uniform. On [0,a] for any fixed 0≤a&lt;1, the error is at most aⁿ, giving uniform convergence.

Uniform limits preserve continuity: to bound |f(x)−f(a)|, insert fₙ(x) and fₙ(a). Choose one sufficiently late n to make both approximation errors below ε/3 everywhere, then use continuity of that fixed fₙ to control its middle difference. Pointwise convergence alone cannot select the same n for nearby x.

If integrable fₙ converge uniformly on a finite interval to f, then f is integrable and $|\int f_n-\int f|\le(b-a)\sup|f_n-f|\to0$. On unbounded domains the finite-length bound disappears and extra control is needed. Narrow spikes show pointwise convergence can fail to preserve an integral.

Uniform function convergence alone does not preserve derivatives. For $f_n(x)=\sin(nx)/n$, the functions converge uniformly to zero, while fₙ′(0)=1 for every n, not the derivative zero of the limit. A standard sufficient derivative theorem requires continuously differentiable fₙ on a closed finite interval, uniform convergence of their derivatives, and convergence of fₙ at one point. Integrating the derivative limit then establishes convergence of the functions to a differentiable limit with that derivative.

For a function series, the Weierstrass M-test uses |gₙ(x)|≤Mₙ uniformly and ∑Mₙ&lt;∞. The uniform tail bound makes the partial sums uniformly Cauchy. A geometric bound can therefore justify termwise integration on a finite interval; termwise differentiation still needs control of the derivative series. Always name the theorem and its domain before exchanging operations.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For fₙ(x)=xⁿ on [0,1/2], sup error is 2⁻ⁿ. At n=10 it is 1/1024, below 0.001. On [0,1], the pointwise limit differs at 1 and the supremum error stays 1. Restricting the domain changes the convergence mode.

</LessonSection>
