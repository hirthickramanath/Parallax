<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Conformal maps simplify two-dimensional boundary problems. Local angle preservation must be separated from global invertibility and the transformation of physical quantities.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

A nonzero complex derivative acts locally as a rotation and uniform scale. Globally, separate points can still be sent to the same location.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

A holomorphic map with nonzero derivative is locally conformal and orientation preserving. A global conformal equivalence additionally requires a one-to-one holomorphic map onto its target with a holomorphic inverse.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

A holomorphic map near z₀ satisfies $f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h+o(|h|)$. When f′(z₀)≠0, multiplication by that derivative rotates and scales all tangent vectors equally, preserving oriented angles. The real Jacobian determinant is |f′|². At a critical point where f′=0, this first-order argument fails; z² doubles angular sectors at zero rather than being locally conformal there.

For z², the derivative is nonzero away from zero, but z and −z share an image. Thus local conformality on the punctured plane does not make the map globally injective. Choosing an appropriate smaller domain can remove this overlap. The inverse-function theorem supplies a local analytic inverse at a noncritical point; it does not prove a single inverse exists over an arbitrary larger domain.

A Möbius map $w=(az+b)/(cz+d)$ requires ad−bc≠0. Its derivative is (ad−bc)/(cz+d)² at finite points away from its pole, and it is an automorphism of the Riemann sphere. It sends generalized circles, meaning circles or lines, to generalized circles. The point at infinity belongs to this spherical description, not to every ordinary plane chart.

The Cayley map $w=(z-i)/(z+i)$ sends the upper half-plane to the unit disk: if Im z>0, then |z−i|&lt;|z+i|, so |w|&lt;1. Its inverse is z=i(1+w)/(1−w). The real axis maps to the unit circle away from the exceptional chart points. Boundary correspondence and interior mapping should be checked separately rather than inferred from three sampled points alone.

If U is harmonic in the w-plane and w=f(z) is holomorphic, the chain rule gives $\Delta_z(U\circ f)=|f'(z)|^2(\Delta_wU)\circ f$ with sufficient smoothness. Harmonicity is preserved, while a Poisson source transforms with a scale factor and is not generally unchanged. Normal derivatives, fluxes and lengths also transform. Dirichlet values pull back directly; Neumann data require metric scaling. Riemann mapping applies to a nonempty simply connected proper plane domain, but does not automatically provide an elementary formula or arbitrary smooth boundary extension.


### Derivation and transfer

For w=z², a nonzero complex derivative scales local lengths by |2z| and rotates all tangent directions by arg(2z), preserving oriented angles. At z=0 the derivative vanishes and this local conformality argument fails. Global injectivity is separate: z and −z share an image.

The magnitude display varies the radius of the evaluation point. It gives a local differential scale, not a global distance-preserving transformation or a proof of one-to-one mapping. Domain restrictions matter when selecting an inverse branch.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For the Cayley map at z=2i, w=(2i−i)/(2i+i)=1/3. Its real part is 1/3 and imaginary part zero. For z² at z=1+i, |f′|²=|2+2i|²=8, so infinitesimal area scales by 8 rather than by the length scale √8.
