Why does a cavity spectrum stay finite where classical equipartition predicts an ultraviolet catastrophe? State a prediction and a reason before reading the argument.

<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Blackbody photons and lattice phonons use bosonic mode counting, but have different dispersions, cutoffs and physical meanings.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build a mental model" layer="intuition">

Start with the concrete worked example below. Track which quantities belong to the system, which are imposed by its surroundings, and which conclusions require a limiting approximation.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

Thermal photon modes have mean occupation 1/(eᵝʰᵛ−1) with zero equilibrium chemical potential when photon number is not conserved; the Debye model approximates acoustic phonons with a finite mode count.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Counting two transverse photon polarizations in a large cavity gives modes per volume per frequency 8πν²/c³. Multiplying by hν times the Bose occupation gives $u_\nu=8\pi h\nu^3/[c^3(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)]$. This is energy density per frequency, not specific intensity or flux. In isotropic equilibrium radiation, uν=4πBν/c and the one-sided blackbody surface flux is πBν.

At hν≪k_BT the Rayleigh–Jeans limit is 8πk_BTν²/c³. Extending that limit to arbitrarily high frequency makes the integrated energy diverge and violates its own regime. At high frequency the exponential tail regularizes the integral. Substituting x=hν/(k_BT) shows total energy density aT⁴; isotropic radiation pressure is u/3. A wavelength density requires the Jacobian |dν/dλ|, so the peaks of Bν and Bλ do not transform by simply replacing ν=c/λ at the maximizer.

The Debye approximation treats a solid’s low-frequency acoustic branches as a continuum with approximately linear dispersion and chooses a cutoff so there are 3N modes for N atoms. Its heat-capacity integral can be written $C_V=9Nk_B(T/\Theta_D)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}x^4e^x/(e^x-1)^2dx$. Low T gives a T³ law in a three-dimensional bulk acoustic model; high T approaches 3Nk_B.

Phonons are excitations of a material lattice and have sound speeds, branches and a finite validity range, unlike vacuum photons. Optical modes, anisotropy, electrons and defects contribute to real solids. The Debye T³ law is not a universal low-temperature law for every dimension or material. Zero-point mode energy does not directly contribute to the fixed-spectrum thermal heat capacity, although it can matter for other material properties.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

Doubling the temperature of an ideal blackbody multiplies its integrated energy density by 2⁴=16. At fixed cavity volume the total thermal energy has the same ratio. In the low-temperature Debye regime, doubling T instead multiplies the lattice heat capacity by 2³=8, provided both temperatures remain in that regime.

</LessonSection>
