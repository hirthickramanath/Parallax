<LessonSection id="story" title="Why the question matters" layer="story">

Relativistic propagation changes frequency, arrival time and beaming, while apparent superluminal motion is a geometric timing effect.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For a moving emitter, Doppler factor δ=[γ(1−βcosθ)]⁻¹ with θ between velocity and observer direction in the stated source-frame geometry.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

The observed frequency scales with δ before any cosmological redshift factor. Lorentz invariance of Iν/ν³ gives $I_\nu=\delta^3I'_{\nu'}$ at ν=δν′ for a local ray transformation. Flux beaming exponents depend on spectral slope and whether the model is a moving blob or a continuous jet; one exponent cannot be applied to every observable.

Light-travel-time geometry gives $\beta_{app}=\beta\sin\theta/(1-\beta\cos\theta)$ for an approaching moving feature. It can exceed one without material or information exceeding c. The measured pattern speed may differ from the fluid bulk speed.

Jet launching/collimation involves angular momentum, magnetic stress and an accretion/compact-object environment. A narrow outflow is not by itself proof of one unique magnetic extraction mechanism. Synchrotron, inverse-Compton and thermal components require particle/field and radiative-transfer models.

Orientation selection strongly biases flux-limited jet samples. Variability time constraints depend on Doppler factor, emitting geometry and redshift; they are not universal direct source-radius measurements. Compare approaching/receding sides with obscuration and environment as additional possibilities rather than attributing every asymmetry solely to beaming.


### Derive, test and transfer

The Doppler factor for an emitting element is $\delta=[\Gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)]^{-1}$. On-axis, relativistic motion can boost observed frequencies and shorten observed variability times. Off-axis, the same intrinsic jet may be much dimmer. The Lorentz factor and the Doppler factor are distinct quantities and need not be equal.

For a moving compact source with spectral convention $F_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$, a commonly used ideal boost is $\delta^{3+\alpha}$, while continuous-flow geometry changes the exponent. Therefore an unqualified δ cubed rule is not universal. Apparent transverse speed can exceed c because arrival-time intervals differ from emission intervals; no material locally outruns light. Orientation-dependent selection can bias a flux-limited sample toward aligned jets.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

For β=.6, γ=1.25 and θ=0, δ=1/[1.25(.4)]=2. At θ=90°, δ=.8. A local frequency of 3 is therefore observed at 6 in the approaching axial example before cosmological redshift.



### Worked investigation: Beaming changes observed frequency

For an emitting element moving with speed beta*c at angle theta to the line of sight, delta=1/[gamma*(1-beta*cos(theta))], gamma=1/sqrt(1-beta²). For beta=0.6, gamma=1.25. Directly approaching motion gives delta=2, transverse motion delta=0.8 and directly receding motion delta=0.5. Even transverse emission has a relativistic frequency shift.

Ignoring cosmological and gravitational shifts, nu_obs=delta*nu_em in this convention. Flux boosting depends on geometry, spectrum and whether the source is a moving blob or a continuous jet; there is no single exponent for every observation. Apparent superluminal transverse motion is a light-travel-time effect and does not require matter to exceed c.
