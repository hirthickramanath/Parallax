<LessonSection id="story" title="Why this matters" layer="story">

Radioactive decay links microscopic randomness with population statistics. Nuclear binding also reveals why mass differences, rather than total nucleon mass alone, determine released energy.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="intuition" title="Build the picture" layer="intuition">

A constant decay probability per surviving nucleus per unit time yields an exponential survival curve. A nucleus does not remember how long it has already survived in this model.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="concept" title="State the model" layer="concept">

For independent nuclei with constant decay rate λ, the expected surviving count follows dN/dt=−λN, and activity is λN.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derive and qualify the result" layer="theory" fullOnly>

Integrating the rate equation yields $N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ and $t_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda$. Activity A=λN is measured in decays per second, or becquerels. The deterministic curve is an expectation or large-population description; individual counts fluctuate. Background, detection efficiency and dead time separate registered counts from true activity.

For a fixed observation interval, independent survival yields a binomial count; in the rare-event limit a Poisson approximation is useful. Its standard deviation is approximately the square root of the expected count, not necessarily the square root of the unknown true activity. Subtracting background introduces additional uncertainty.

Nuclear binding energy is $B=[Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-M_{\rm nucleus}]c^2$. If atomic masses are used, electron masses and binding must be treated consistently. The reaction energy is Q=(initial mass−final mass)c² using a consistent set of masses and all products. Positive Q means energetically exothermic, not necessarily rapid: barriers and selection rules affect rate.

Alpha decay changes mass number by −4 and atomic number by −2. Beta-minus decay changes a neutron into a proton with electron and antineutrino emission, leaving mass number unchanged. Gamma decay lowers nuclear excitation without changing A or Z. Charge, energy, momentum and appropriate particle-number conservation must all be checked.

Fusion of light nuclei and fission of some heavy nuclei can release energy because the products have greater binding per nucleon. Neither process is guaranteed for every chosen pair or isotope. Reaction pathways and conditions matter. Radioisotope demonstrations should use simulations or appropriately supervised licensed equipment, never improvised sources.


### Transfer derivation and failure case

With constant decay probability per unit time $\lambda$, $dN/dt=-\lambda N$ integrates to $N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ and $t_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda$. The expected number of detected signal events is reduced by efficiency; independent background adds to the expected count. Subtracting an estimated background also introduces its uncertainty and does not recover unobserved event identities. At high rates, dead time makes observed counts nonlinear in the true rate, so a constant efficiency correction can fail. A deterministic exponential is an expectation law, not a prediction of the exact next decay time.


### Derivation and transfer

Radioactive survival is N(t)=N0exp(−λt), with half-life ln2/λ. Activity λN decreases with the surviving count, while an individual nucleus has no deterministic countdown. Independent decays lead to count fluctuations.

The display is the ensemble expected population, which need not be an integer. It does not sample individual decay events or include daughter-chain feeding. Half-life and mean lifetime 1/λ are different constants.

</LessonSection>
