<LessonSection id="concept" title="Definitions and hypotheses" layer="concept">

For noninteracting identical particles in thermal equilibrium, mean single-particle occupations are 1/(eᵝ⁽ᵋ⁻ᵘ⁾+1) for fermions and 1/(eᵝ⁽ᵋ⁻ᵘ⁾−1) for bosons, with state degeneracy counted separately.

</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="theory" title="Derivation, proof and limitations" layer="theory" fullOnly>

An independent fermion state permits n=0,1, so its grand sum is 1+a with a=e⁻ᵝ⁽ᵋ⁻ᵘ⁾. Differentiating with respect to its weight gives occupation a/(1+a). An independent boson state permits n=0,1,2,… and has sum 1/(1−a) for a&lt;1, yielding a/(1−a). This derives the plus and minus denominators from counting rather than from a force between particles.

The dilute limit a≪1 gives the Maxwell–Boltzmann occupation a for either case. Low occupation per single-particle state, not simply low temperature or low particle mass, is the relevant limit. For massive nonrelativistic particles the thermal wavelength and density combination nλ_T³, with spin-state factors included, diagnoses quantum degeneracy.

For fermions at zero temperature, the occupation approaches a step at the Fermi energy. Filling momentum states gives $p_F=\hbar(6\pi^2n/g)^{1/3}$ for spin degeneracy g in a uniform three-dimensional ideal gas. Nonrelativistic ε=p²/(2m) and ultrarelativistic ε≈pc give different pressure-density scalings. A rest-energy convention shifts both ε and μ and must remain consistent.

For bosons μ cannot exceed the lowest single-particle energy in the ideal equilibrium model. In a uniform three-dimensional nonrelativistic gas, excited states hold a finite maximum number at fixed T in the thermodynamic limit, allowing a macroscopic ground-state occupation below the critical temperature. Dimensionality, confinement and interactions change that conclusion. Bose condensation is not identical to superfluidity, and an ideal-gas formula is not a complete theory of either phenomenon.

### Occupation fluctuations follow from the same counting

For one state write its grand sum as $\Xi(a)=\sum_n a^n$. Then $\langle n\rangle=a\partial_a\ln\Xi$ and $\operatorname{Var}(n)=a\partial_a\langle n\rangle$. Fermions therefore have variance $f(1-f)$, while bosons have $b(1+b)$. The suppression or enhancement is a statistical statement for this independent equilibrium state, not evidence of a new interparticle force.

### Derive the degeneracy-pressure scaling

At zero temperature, count momentum states in a box: $N=gV(4\pi p_F^3/3)/(2\pi\hbar)^3$. For nonrelativistic dispersion, integrating $p^2/(2m)$ over the filled sphere gives $U/N=3E_F/5$. The isotropic kinetic-pressure relation gives $P=2U/(3V)=2nE_F/5$, hence $P\propto n^{5/3}$. With ultrarelativistic dispersion, $U/N=3p_Fc/4$ and $P=U/(3V)$, hence $P\propto n^{4/3}$. These exponents depend on three spatial dimensions, an ideal gas and the stated dispersion; interactions can change the equation of state.


### Derive, test and transfer

For noninteracting indistinguishable modes, fermions permit occupations zero or one and bosons permit nonnegative integer occupations. Summing the corresponding grand weights gives nF=1/(exp x+1) and nB=1/(exp x−1), with x=β(ε−μ). The bosonic expression requires the appropriate positive x for a regular excited mode.

For large positive x both approach exp(−x), explaining the dilute classical limit. Near x=0 the fermion result remains finite while the boson result grows, signaling why low-energy occupancy and condensation need careful treatment. Spin/internal degeneracy counts separate states; it should not be inserted without defining what one mode means. The display compares one explicitly selected occupation convention.

<LessonSection id="worked" title="A worked argument" layer="worked" fullOnly>

When β(ε−μ)=ln3, fermion occupation is 1/4 while boson occupation is 1/2. Their classical approximation is 1/3. The comparison shows the correction direction at the same energy and chemical-potential offset, not at automatically identical total densities.

At occupation $f=1/4$, a single fermion state's variance is $3/16$. At boson occupation $b=1/2$, it is $3/4$, four times larger. Both means came from the same $\beta(\epsilon-\mu)=\ln3$, but comparing variances at fixed offset is different from holding particle density fixed.

For spin degeneracy two, doubling number density multiplies $p_F$ by $2^{1/3}$ and nonrelativistic pressure by $2^{5/3}$. Check that $nE_F$ has pressure units, energy per volume.

</LessonSection>
